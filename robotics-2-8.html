      2.8 Vision-Guided Picking

      Gerald Lê
      Chapter 2, lesson 8 of 8 ← Previous: 2.7 Program Structure and Error Handling Next: Chapter 3: How to Contribute to Open Source →
      Start here

      When parts arrive loose on a conveyor or tilted in a bin, fixed taught points miss. This lesson explains how 2D, 2.5D, and 3D cameras measure part poses, how hand-eye calibration maps pixels to robot millimeters, and how vision results shift a robot frame.

      • You will learn when to choose 2D, 2.5D, or 3D vision and fixed versus wrist-mounted cameras.
      • You will learn intrinsic calibration and hand-eye calibration (AX = XB).
      • You will learn how to compute a rigid 2D frame shift instead of naively adding offsets.

      01. Why a taught point is not enough

      Every program in this chapter so far has assumed that the part sits in the exact same spot every time. Picture a part held in a tray versus a loose part arriving on a conveyor belt. In lesson 2.6, you taught a pick point inside a fixture frame, and the robot returned to that spot on every cycle. That works well when a fixture or tray holds the part tightly.

      It fails when parts arrive loose on a conveyor, lie at random angles on a table, or sit in a pile in a bin. A saved pick point cannot tell that today's part landed 15 millimeters (mm) to one side and turned by 30 degrees.

      Vision-guided robotics handles these cases. First, a camera takes a picture of the work area. Next, vision software finds the part and reports where it landed. Then the robot program adjusts its taught points to match. The motion instructions stay the same as in lesson 2.6. Only the reference frame changes, because that frame now moves with the part.

      Lesson 6.5 Sensors for Robots already covers how a camera works as a sensor, so we do not repeat that here. Instead, this lesson covers the robot side. You will learn which kind of vision a job needs and where to mount the camera. You will also learn how calibration turns pixels into millimeters in the robot's world, and how the vision result becomes a safe, accurate robot move.

      02. Choosing 2D, 2.5D, or 3D vision

      Not every picking job needs the same kind of camera. A part lying flat on a belt only needs a top-down view, while tangled parts in a bin need depth. Vision systems for robot guidance are grouped by how much of a part's pose—its position and angle—they measure. Choosing the simplest option that fits your job saves money and setup effort.

      • Two-dimensional (2D) vision: works only in the flat image plane, with height and width but no depth.[1] It also struggles when parts have little contrast against their background.[2] A 2D result gives the part's X and Y position plus its rotation about the vertical axis.[3] Height and tilt must come from elsewhere, so the program assumes the part lies flat on a known, taught plane. FANUC's training material stresses that you must set the right part Z height for 2D results to be accurate.[4]
      • 2.5D vision: adds height on top of 2D,[1] and some systems also estimate a small amount of tilt.[2] A FANUC engineer quoted by the Association for Advancing Automation (A3) says that 2.5D works when the robot only needs the distance to the object.[2]
      • Three-dimensional (3D) vision: measures X, Y, and Z together with rotation about all three axes.[2] The same A3 article argues that for bin picking “only 3D will do.” When a part is not lying on a flat surface, or its scale is unknown, a 3D point cloud is the way to go. Low contrast, such as boxes on a pallet that look alike, can push a job toward 3D even on a flat surface.[2]

      Follow one simple rule when choosing: use the simplest vision that measures everything the job needs. If your parts lie flat on a known surface, 2D is usually enough.

      For the curious: vendor terms, 2.5D differences, and 3D cost

      FANUC's training material calls the flat plane in which a 2D part moves the offset frame.[4] Also keep in mind that vendors do not all use “2.5D” the same way, so check which values a system actually reports.

      Cost matters when picking a system. One industry review reports that basic 3D vision-guided starter systems begin at around 40,000 US dollars. That can be more than the collaborative robot they guide.[1] Because that source is partly promotional, treat the price figure as rough.

      03. Where to mount the camera: fixed or on the wrist

      Once you pick a vision type, your next choice is where to put the camera. You can bolt it above the work area so it stays still, or mount it on the robot wrist so it travels with the gripper. Each spot trades speed for reach.

      Documentation for OpenCV (Open Source Computer Vision Library) uses two standard names for these setups.[5] In an eye-in-hand setup, the camera mounts on the robot gripper—the robot's “hand”—and moves with it. In an eye-to-hand setup, the camera stays static in the cell and looks at the work area. During calibration, a static camera can also look at a pattern carried by the robot.[5]

      A typical eye-in-hand product is the Robotiq Wrist Camera for Universal Robots (UR) arms. Its workflow centers on a taught snapshot position, the robot pose where the camera takes its pictures. You calibrate that pose with a calibration board. Program nodes called “Find Visual Offset” and “Apply Visual Offset” then shift the rest of the program based on what the camera sees.[6]

      • Fixed camera (eye-to-hand): it can take the next picture while the robot is still placing the last part. That lets vision time overlap with robot motion. It also needs only one calibration, though its field of view stays fixed.
      • Wrist camera (eye-in-hand): it can inspect many spots in the cell and move closer to a part to see fine detail. The cost is cycle time. The robot must stop at the snapshot position before every picture, and accuracy depends on reaching that pose repeatably.

      The table below gives a rough starting guide based on these trade-offs. It is a practical summary, not a rule from a standard:

      Application Usual vision type Usual mounting
      Picking from a moving conveyor 2D Fixed above the belt, with conveyor tracking
      Locating a part in a tray or fixture 2D Fixed, or on the wrist with a snapshot position
      Random bin picking 3D Fixed 3D sensor over the bin, or one on the arm
      Depalletizing boxes 2.5D or 3D Usually fixed above the pallet

      04. Calibrating the lens: pixels to millimeters

      A raw camera photo is not a ruler. Cheap lenses bend straight lines near the edges, and pixels alone do not tell you how many millimeters wide a part is. Before a robot can trust a camera, you must calibrate two things. First comes intrinsic calibration for the camera itself, followed by hand-eye calibration for where the camera sits. Remember the split this way: intrinsic calibration fixes the lens, and hand-eye calibration fixes where the camera is.

      OpenCV's calibration tutorial explains why lens calibration comes first. Inexpensive lenses introduce “significant distortion” into images. Because that distortion stays constant for a given lens, you can measure it once and correct it. Calibration also links image pixels to real-world units such as millimeters.[7] The camera's basic pinhole geometry is summed up in a 3×3 camera matrix:[7] 

              | fx   0   cx |
    K = |  0  fy   cy |
        |  0   0    1 |

      In this matrix, fx and fy are the focal lengths in pixels. The point (cx, cy) is the optical center of the image in pixels.[7]

      Real lenses also bend light away from this ideal pinhole model, so OpenCV models lens distortion in two parts. Radial distortion creates the curved “barrel” or “fish-eye” look near image edges. Tangential distortion happens when the lens is not quite parallel to the image sensor. Together, they use five distortion coefficients: k1, k2, p1, p2, k3.[7]

      You find all of these numbers by showing the camera a pattern of known size, such as a chessboard. Two chessboard photos are enough in theory, but real lenses need more views across the whole frame. In everyday words, take plenty of angled shots in the corners and at different distances; OpenCV's tutorial puts it this way: “you will probably need at least 10 good snapshots of the input pattern in different positions”.[7]

      Once software solves for the camera matrix and distortion, it checks the re-projection error. It projects the known board corners back into each photo and measures how many pixels off they land from the detected corners. In everyday words, a smaller number means the math matches the real photos; the tutorial says this error “should be as close to zero as possible”.[7] If the error is large, take cleaner photos and calibrate again before moving on.

      For the curious: distortion equations, pattern options, and OpenCV functions

      For a point at distance r from the image center, radial distortion shifts x as x_d = x(1 + k1·r² + k2·r⁴ + k3·r⁶), and does the same for y. Tangential distortion adds x_d = x + [2·p1·x·y + p2·(r² + 2x²)]. That gives the five coefficients k1, k2, p1, p2, k3.[7]

      Alongside chessboards, OpenCV supports ChArUco boards (which pair a chessboard with ArUco fiducial markers for sharper corner detection) plus symmetric and asymmetric circle grids. In code, findChessboardCorners locates inner corners, cornerSubPix refines them to a fraction of a pixel, calibrateCamera solves for the camera matrix and distortion, and projectPoints computes the re-projection error.

      05. Finding where the camera sits: hand-eye calibration

      Fixing the lens lets the camera measure a part relative to itself. Still, the robot arm needs to know where the camera sits in the cell before it can reach that part. For a wrist camera, you need the fixed transform from the camera to the gripper. For a fixed camera, you need the transform from the camera to the robot base. Finding that spatial link is hand-eye calibration.

      OpenCV documents a straightforward procedure. Keep a calibration pattern still, move the robot to several poses, and record two transforms at each pose.[5] The robot gives you the first one: the gripper-to-base pose computed from its joint angles. The camera gives you the second one: the pose of the pattern relative to the camera, measured from the image.

      Between any two poses, the gripper's motion (A) and the camera's motion (B) are linked by the unknown transform X. In everyday words, you match how the arm moved against how the picture moved; OpenCV notes the problem “is also known as solving the AX=XB equation”.[5] For an eye-to-hand fixed camera, the math is the same, except X becomes the camera-to-base transform.[5] OpenCV's documented function is:[5] 

      void cv::calibrateHandEye(
    InputArrayOfArrays R_gripper2base, InputArrayOfArrays t_gripper2base,
    InputArrayOfArrays R_target2cam,   InputArrayOfArrays t_target2cam,
    OutputArray R_cam2gripper,         OutputArray t_cam2gripper,
    HandEyeCalibrationMethod method = CALIB_HAND_EYE_TSAI );

      You pass in lists of rotations and translations, one pair per robot pose. The function returns the camera-to-gripper rotation and translation.

      Just sliding the robot flat or spinning around one axis is not enough to solve for X. In everyday words, you must tilt the wrist in different directions across several shots; OpenCV's documentation puts it this way: “A minimum of 2 motions with non parallel rotation axes are necessary ... So at least 3 different poses are required, but it is strongly recommended to use many more poses.”[5]

      You do not need the matrix proofs to use these tools. Still, “AX = XB” is what every hand-eye calibration tool solves underneath.

      For the curious: the published math behind AX = XB

      OpenCV implements several published hand-eye methods. Its default, CALIB_HAND_EYE_TSAI, is the classic 1989 method by R. Y. Tsai and R. K. Lenz.[5][8] Their paper derived simple, efficient algorithms from geometric lemmas and tested them on an International Business Machines (IBM) Cartesian robot.[9]

      In 1994, F. C. Park and B. J. Martin framed the same task as solving AX = XB on the Euclidean group, which is the mathematical group of rigid motions.[10]

      06. The 2D factory shortcut: calibrate to a user frame

      Tilting a robot through many poses takes time. When parts lie flat on a table or conveyor, factory vision tools usually skip the full AX = XB setup. Instead, you lay a calibration grid on the flat work surface and calibrate the camera directly to a robot user frame (an ABB work object, see lesson 2.6). Once both the camera and the robot know that same grid, an image coordinate converts straight into millimeters in that frame.

      • FANUC iRVision: FANUC's training material describes grid-based camera calibration. Related training videos cover the GRID_UFRAME and GRID_UTOOL utilities that work with the calibration grid.[11]
      • ABB Integrated Vision: an integrator's guide describes calibrating the camera with the grid, then teaching the work object's user frame aligned with the grid's X and Y axes.[3] Forum advice adds that the grid should sit at the same height as the features you want to measure.[12] Also note that the grids are printed for International Organization for Standardization (ISO) paper, so on US Letter paper you must measure the real square size.[13]
      • Cognex In-Sight: “Sequential N Point” calibration pairs one feature with its robot coordinates over several images. The robot moves the part between shots.[14]

      Cognex adds one warning that applies to every method in this lesson. Your calibration stays valid only while the lens and camera mount stay unchanged, and you must redo it if either moves.[14] A bumped bracket, a refocused lens, or a new zoom setting breaks the math. Lock the lens rings and pin the camera mount once calibration is done.

      07. Turning a camera result into a robot move

      After calibration, the vision system reports each part's pose inside a robot user frame. Across every brand, the workflow is the same. You never rewrite your taught pick points. Instead, the program runs those points inside a coordinate frame that shifts and turns to match the camera result.

      The vendor details below come from training notes and forum posts that could not be checked against official controller manuals. Confirm the exact instruction names in your own manual before using them.

      • ABB: an ABB work object holds a user frame and an object frame. With Integrated Vision, you teach only the user frame. At run time, the program copies the camera's found pose into the object frame, so targets taught inside that work object follow the part.[3][12]
      • FANUC: FANUC applies the vision result as a single offset that shifts the part's taught points.[15] It advises a fixed frame offset when picking, because the part moved on the table. It advises a tool offset when placing, because the part shifted inside the gripper.[16] To re-teach a point that already runs with an offset, the “subtract offset” option clears the active offset so the point saves in the unshifted frame.[15]
      • Universal Robots: a camera offset in millimeters and degrees becomes a pose and updates a reference point using the URScript function pose_trans.[17] Alternatively, you can define points relative to a feature plane and let the vision result move that plane.[18]

      All of these methods work because a frame shift is a full rigid transform—a move that combines translation and rotation without stretching the shape. When a part turns, the pick point must swing around with it like the tip of a lever. Simply adding the X and Y offset misses that swing, as the next section shows.

      08. Step-by-step math: shifting and rotating a pick point

      If a part shifts sideways, it is tempting to just add that offset to your taught coordinates. That shortcut fails as soon as the part also rotates. Think of turning a wrench: the far end swings through an arc instead of sliding straight. To capture both the slide and the swing, robots pack position and angle into a single transform.

      A planar 2D pose—a position (x, y) and a rotation θ around Z—can be written as a 3×3 homogeneous transform. The numbers below were computed for this lesson and checked numerically. They are not from a vendor manual, and all positions are in millimeters inside the robot's user frame. 

                    | cosθ  -sinθ   x |
    T(x,y,θ) = | sinθ   cosθ   y |
              |   0       0     1 |

      At teach time, vision saw the part at T_ref = (200, 150, 0°), and you taught the pick point at P_taught = (210, 150, 0°). Seen from the part itself, that pick point sits 10 mm along the part's own X axis: T_ref⁻¹ · P_taught = (10, 0, 0°). That local offset stays constant because it belongs to the part, not to the table.

      At run time, vision finds the new part at T_found = (215, 140, 30°). It moved +15 mm in X, −10 mm in Y, and turned 30 degrees.

      Method A, part-relative. Carry the local pick point along with the moved part: P_new = T_found · (T_ref⁻¹ · P_taught). Rotating (10, 0) by 30° gives (10 cos 30°, 10 sin 30°) = (8.660, 5.000). Adding the part's new position (215, 140) gives P_new = (223.660, 145.000, 30°). This is what an ABB object frame or a UR pose_trans computes underneath.

      Method B, frame offset. You can also compute the frame offset itself: T_off = T_found · T_ref⁻¹. Its rotation is 30°. Its translation is (215, 140) minus (200, 150) rotated by 30°, which equals (215 − 98.205, 140 − 229.904) = (116.795, −89.904). Applying it gives P_new = T_off · P_taught = (223.660, 145.000, 30°), the exact same answer. Fixed frame offsets use this form. Its translation looks nothing like “+15, −10” because the rotation pivots around the frame's origin, not around the part.

      The common mistake. A beginner sees “the part moved +15, −10” and simply adds that to the taught point. That gives (225, 140) and ignores rotation. The distance from the true point is the square root of (1.340² + 5.000²), or 5.18 mm, and the gripper is also 30° misaligned. That is enough to miss a small part or bump it with a finger. The cause is the lever arm: the pick point sits 10 mm from the part's reference point, so rotating the part swings that arm to a new spot.

      The vision frame shift simulator needs JavaScript. With it on, you can adjust part rotation and translation to compare true rigid transforms against naive offset mistakes.

      The Python script below repeats these steps so you can test new numbers and check your hand math. It is this lesson's own example in plain Python with no extra libraries. 

      import math

def T(x, y, deg):
    """Planar pose (mm, degrees) as a 3x3 homogeneous matrix."""
    c, s = math.cos(math.radians(deg)), math.sin(math.radians(deg))
    return [[c, -s, x], [s, c, y], [0, 0, 1]]

def mul(A, B):
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(3)) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def inv(A):
    # inverse of a rigid transform: transpose the rotation, rotate and negate the translation
    c, s, x, y = A[0][0], A[1][0], A[0][2], A[1][2]
    return [[c, s, -(c * x + s * y)], [-s, c, s * x - c * y], [0, 0, 1]]

def pose(A):
    return (round(A[0][2], 3), round(A[1][2], 3), round(math.degrees(math.atan2(A[1][0], A[0][0])), 3))

T_ref   = T(200, 150, 0)    # where vision saw the part at teach time
P_taught = T(210, 150, 0)   # pick point taught at teach time
T_found = T(215, 140, 30)   # where vision sees the part now

local = mul(inv(T_ref), P_taught)   # pick point in the part's own frame
P_new = mul(T_found, local)        # same point, carried by the moved part
print("local:", pose(local))       # (10.0, 0.0, 0.0)
print("P_new:", pose(P_new))       # (223.66, 145.0, 30.0)

T_off = mul(T_found, inv(T_ref))   # the frame offset itself
print("T_off:", pose(T_off))       # (116.795, -89.904, 30.0)

      If your vision tool reports coordinates in pixels, convert them into the user frame through calibration first. That uses the millimeters-per-pixel scale plus the camera-to-frame transform. Only after that step should you apply the frame shift.

      09. How accurate can it be? Pixels, calibration and light

      Before you buy a camera, check whether it can see finely enough for your gripper. A camera viewing an entire table spreads its pixels thin, while changing light can make part detections jump from shot to shot. Every small error—in pixel size, calibration, and robot motion—adds up at the gripper tip.

      Start by checking resolution. The size of one pixel on the part equals the field of view (FOV) divided by the number of pixels across it. The Imaging Source gives a clear example: a 1920-pixel sensor viewing 200 mm gives 9.6 pixels per millimeter, and a rule of thumb is 2 to 3 pixels across a feature.[19] Machine-vision supplier 1stVision says at least 2 pixels are needed to find an edge when contrast is excellent. Using 3 to 4 pixels is more robust. For example, to resolve a 0.25 mm feature with 4 pixels, you need 0.0625 mm per pixel, so a 40 mm FOV needs 640 pixels. Those figures assume the lens is not the limit, and they depend on lighting, optics, and algorithm.[20]

      Now apply that math to a typical cell. A camera 1280 pixels wide viewing a 320 mm field gives 4 pixels per millimeter, or 0.25 mm per pixel. With the 2 to 4 pixel rule, the smallest feature it can locate reliably is about 0.5 to 1 mm. Halve the FOV to 160 mm, and each pixel covers half as much. That is why a wrist camera that moves closer can see finer detail than a fixed camera watching the whole table.

      Rotation errors also grow with distance, for the same lever-arm reason as in section 08. A 0.5° angle error moves a pick point 100 mm from the part's reference point by 100 × sin(0.5°), or about 0.87 mm. A poorly taught user frame or hand-eye calibration behaves the same way. The error grows larger as the part rotates more and sits farther from the frame origin. Other error sources stack on top:

      • Lens calibration: uncorrected distortion makes positions near the image edges wrong. Sub-pixel corner refinement and a low re-projection error are your checks.[7]
      • Camera-to-robot calibration: users of wrist cameras report parts ending up “a few mm off” when this step is not done well.[21]
      • Part height: in 2D, the distance from the camera to the part enters the conversion from pixels to millimeters. Because of this, a part taller or shorter than your setup height is measured wrongly. FANUC's part Z height setting accounts for this distance.[4]
      • Robot repeatability: the robot adds its own small error to every move. A wrist camera adds that error twice—once at the snapshot position and again at the pick.
      • Lighting and contrast: these decide whether the camera finds the part reliably at all. Low contrast is a known weakness of 2D vision.[2] Changing daylight or reflections can make detections jitter from image to image.

      No source checked for this lesson gives a reliable typical end-to-end accuracy for 2D vision guidance, so none is quoted here. Measure it on your own cell instead. Place a part at known positions and angles, and record how far the gripper lands from where it should.

      Whichever brand you use, the workflow stays the same. First, choose 2D or 3D, and choose where the camera goes. Next, calibrate the lens and calibrate the camera to the robot. Finally, teach the pick points once in a frame, and let the vision result shift that frame on every cycle.

      Try it
      1. On paper, use the example from section 08: part taught at (200, 150, 0°), pick point at (210, 150, 0°), part found at (215, 140, 30°). Write the pick point in the part's own frame, rotate it by 30°, and add the new part position. Check that you get (223.660, 145.000, 30°).
      2. Make the beginner's mistake on purpose: add (+15, −10) to the taught point to get (225, 140). Compute its distance from the correct point and confirm it is 5.18 mm. Sketch both points and the part to see where the 30° lever-arm swing went.
      3. Run the Python script from section 08 with the camera result changed to (215, 140, 90°). Predict the corrected pick point first. How large is the "just add the offset" error now?
      4. In URSim or RobotStudio (see 2.6), create a user frame or work object with its origin at (200, 150) and axes parallel to the original frame, and teach the pick point in it (it should read (10, 0)). Move the frame's origin to (215, 140), rotate it 30° about its own Z axis, and check that the robot now goes to (223.660, 145.000) in the original frame. Test this in the simulator first, never on a real cell.
