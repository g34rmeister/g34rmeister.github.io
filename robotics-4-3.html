4.3 Robots and Society

Gerald Lê Draft

01. Beyond the robot cell

Ethics in Engineering covered the duties engineers owe the public, and Ethics in Robotics covered keeping people physically safe around a robot. This article looks further out, at three questions robots raise for society as a whole: what they do to work, what they do with the data their sensors collect, and who answers for a decision a machine makes on its own. None has a single settled answer, so the aim is to understand the evidence well enough to make your own design choices.

02. Jobs and automation

According to the International Federation of Robotics (IFR), more than 600,000 new industrial robots were installed worldwide in 2025, and a record 5 million were in operation. What that means for workers is much studied, and the research points in more than one direction:

  • Local job losses are real: Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo studied US labor markets from 1990 to 2007 and found that one more robot per thousand workers reduced the employment-to-population ratio by 0.2 percentage points and wages by 0.42% in the areas most exposed to robots.
  • Tasks, not whole jobs: automation usually takes over particular tasks within a job. An OECD study of the countries it surveyed estimated in 2018 that about 14% of jobs were at high risk of automation, while a further 32% were likely to change significantly in how they are done.
  • New tasks appear too: the same economists describe a “displacement effect,” where machines take over tasks people did, and a “reinstatement effect,” where technology creates new tasks in which people have the advantage, such as programming, maintaining and supervising robots. How the two balance out depends on choices made by companies and governments.

People disagree about how fast these changes will happen and how to respond, from retraining and adult education to changes in how automation is taxed or adopted. Most sources agree that workers whose tasks change need a way to learn new ones. Engineers influence this too: a cell that operators can retool, troubleshoot and teach new paths creates skilled work around the robot.

03. Privacy: robots are sensors on wheels

To work, many robots must see and remember their surroundings. A robot vacuum or a warehouse AMR builds a map of the building, and many carry cameras, microphones or lidar. That data can reveal far more than the robot needs.

In December 2022, MIT Technology Review reported that images taken in 2020 by development versions of iRobot’s Roomba J7 series, including one of a woman on the toilet, had been posted in private online groups by gig workers in Venezuela who were labeling the images to train the robot’s AI. iRobot said the devices were special test units given to paid data collectors and employees who had signed consent agreements, not consumer products. The case still shows how sensor data can travel far from where it was collected, and that the people in the images are not always the people who agreed.

The main defense is data minimization: the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) requires that personal data be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary” for its purpose. For a robot this means collecting only what the task needs, processing it on the robot when possible, keeping it no longer than necessary, protecting it in transit and storage, and telling people clearly what is recorded. The IEEE Code of Ethics covered in 4.1 also lists protecting the privacy of others as a duty.

04. Accountability and autonomy

When a robot follows a fixed program, it is fairly clear who is responsible for it. As robots use learned models for vision, grasping or navigation, the chain of responsibility gets longer: the maker of the model, the integrator who built the cell, the company that deployed it and the operator on shift all play a part. A common safeguard is to keep a human in the loop, a person who can understand, check and override what the system does.

  • EU AI Act: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 sorts AI systems into four levels of risk: unacceptable (banned), high, transparency and minimal. AI-based safety components of products such as robot-assisted surgery count as high risk, and high-risk systems need risk management, logging, documentation and appropriate human oversight. A 2026 amendment moved AI in machinery, such as industrial robots, under the EU Machinery Regulation, which is to add the AI requirements by 2 August 2028.
  • IEEE Ethically Aligned Design: published in 2019, it sets out eight general principles for autonomous and intelligent systems, among them transparency (the basis of a decision should be discoverable) and accountability.
  • IEEE 7000-2021: a standard that gives engineers a process for identifying stakeholders’ values and turning them into system requirements during design.
AI in robots, and in this course

The same questions apply to AI that talks rather than moves. Under the AI Act, people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system such as a chatbot. This course plans to add an AI tutor: treat it as an assistant that can be wrong, check its answers against the lessons and their references, and remember that you, not the tool, are responsible for the robot code you run.

05. Questions to ask before you build

  • People: whose work changes because of this robot, and how will they learn the new tasks?
  • Data: what does it sense and store, is all of it needed, where does it go and who can see it?
  • Oversight: can a person understand what it is doing, and stop or override it?
  • Responsibility: if it makes a mistake, who finds out, who fixes it, and are its decisions logged?
  • Rules: which safety standards and laws apply where it will be used (see 4.2)?
