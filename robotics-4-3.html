4.3 Robots and Society

Gerald Lê

01. Expanding our perspective: robots in society

The previous lessons examined professional engineering ethics and physical machine safety within industrial work cells. However, when robotic systems leave controlled manufacturing enclosures and enter the wider economy, they reshape society. Automated mobile robots transport pallets across logistics fulfillment centers, autonomous delivery vehicles navigate public sidewalks, robotic vacuums map private residences, and surgical arms operate inside human patients.[1]

These deployments raise urgent societal questions: how do industrial robots alter employment and wages? How can privacy be preserved when mobile platforms carry arrays of cameras and LiDAR sensors? Who bears moral and legal responsibility when an autonomous machine makes an erroneous decision? Engineering decisions cannot be divorced from their economic and legal consequences. This lesson reviews rigorous empirical evidence and governance frameworks to guide responsible design.[1]

02. Empirical evidence on jobs and automation

Popular debates on robotics frequently fluctuate between utopian promises of effortless abundance and dystopian warnings of permanent mass unemployment. Rigorous labor economics replaces speculative rhetoric with empirical econometric data.[1]

The benchmark study on the employment effects of industrial robotics was conducted by MIT economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, published in the Journal of Political Economy in 2020.[1] Acemoglu and Restrepo analyzed the adoption of industrial robotics across 722 US commuting zones between 1990 and 2007, tracking local labor market outcomes while controlling for imports from China, offshoring, computerization, and demographic shifts.[1]

Acemoglu and Restrepo studied US labor markets from 1990 to 2007. What did they find about one more robot per thousand workers? Their empirical findings were precise and sobering: it reduced the employment-to-population ratio by 0.2 percentage points and wages by 0.42% in the local commuting zones most exposed to industrial robotics.[1]

In absolute numbers, each additional industrial robot introduced into a local commuting zone displaced an average of approximately 3.3 to 6.2 workers across manufacturing and related regional service sectors.[1] Furthermore, these economic impacts were heavily concentrated. Workers without a college degree engaged in routine manual assembly, welding, painting, and machining bore the overwhelming brunt of job displacement and wage stagnation, while managers and workers holding advanced technical degrees experienced modest income gains.[1]

03. The task framework: displacement, productivity, and reinstatement

To explain why automation affects labor in nuanced ways, Acemoglu and Restrepo formulated the task framework of production in the Journal of Economic Perspectives.[2] Under this framework, an economy does not produce output by combining abstract "capital" and "labor." Instead, production consists of a continuous spectrum of distinct tasks: machining parts, routing circuit boards, scheduling logistics, and troubleshooting errors.[2]

When automation advances, it influences employment through three competing economic mechanisms:[2]

  • 1. The Displacement Effect: Capital equipment directly substitutes for human labor in specific routine tasks. When an automotive manufacturer replaces manual spot-welders with robotic arms, labor's share of value added in that task drops to zero, reducing demand for routine manual labor and putting downward pressure on wages.[2]
  • 2. The Productivity Effect: By automating routine tasks, production costs decline significantly. Cheaper production reduces consumer prices, expanding aggregate market demand. As factory output surges, the firm hires more workers to perform non-automated tasks, such as quality assurance, specialized assembly, and supply-chain logistics.[2]
  • 3. The Reinstatement Effect: Technological progress creates entirely new labor-intensive tasks and occupations in which human workers have a comparative advantage.[2]

What is the reinstatement effect? According to the task framework of automation, the reinstatement effect occurs when technological change creates new complex tasks and occupations in which humans have a comparative advantage.[2] The introduction of robotics creates brand new job categories that did not previously exist: robotic systems integrators, offline simulation programmers, PLC field technicians, safety compliance officers, and predictive maintenance engineers.[2]

Whether automation benefits or harms overall employment depends on the dynamic balance between the displacement effect and the reinstatement effect. Over the past four decades, the displacement effect has outpaced the reinstatement effect in manufacturing, resulting in wage polarization and job displacement in industrial regions.[1][2]

04. Global robot density and industrial trends

The scale of industrial automation is tracked annually by the International Federation of Robotics (IFR) in its benchmark World Robotics reports.[3] By 2024, the global operational stock of industrial robots exceeded 5 million units operating in factories worldwide, with annual global installations consistently surpassing 500,000 new units per year.[3]

To measure automation intensity across nations, the IFR tracks robot density: the number of operational industrial robots per 10,000 employees in the manufacturing sector.[3] The global average has climbed to 162 robots per 10,000 employees, but national densities vary dramatically:[3]

Country / Territory Robot Density (Robots per 10,000 Employees) Primary Industrial Sectors
Republic of Korea 1,012 Semiconductors, memory chips, consumer electronics, automotive.
Singapore 770 High-precision semiconductor packaging, biomedical devices.
China 470 Electric vehicles, lithium batteries, solar panels, electronics assembly.
Germany 429 Automotive powertrain and assembly, heavy industrial machinery.
Japan 419 Automotive manufacturing, robot manufacturing, precision electronics.
United States 295 Automotive, aerospace, metal fabrication, food packaging.

China represents the world's fastest-growing robotics market, accounting for more than 50% of all new industrial robot installations worldwide each year.[3] High robot density in countries such as South Korea, Singapore, and Germany demonstrates that intense automation does not automatically result in high national unemployment: both South Korea and Germany have maintained low structural unemployment rates while deploying record numbers of robots. However, the nature of work in these economies shifts decisively from manual labor to technical supervision.[3]

05. Job polarization and workforce transition

The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has investigated how robotics and AI affect employment across member nations.[4][5] In its policy analysis on the future of work, the OECD estimated that:[4]

  • 14% of jobs are at high risk: Defined as occupations where at least 70% of day-to-day tasks can be automated using existing mechanical and digital technologies.[4]
  • 32% of jobs will experience major task transformation: Between 50% and 70% of tasks will change significantly, requiring workers to master software tools, collaborative robots, or automated inspection systems.[4]

The central economic consequence of automation is not the total disappearance of human jobs, but labor market polarization (often called the "hollowing out" of the middle class).[5] High-skill conceptual occupations (software engineering, data analysis, mechanical design) and low-skill non-routine service roles (personal care, hospitality, custodial maintenance) grow in demand. Meanwhile, middle-skill routine occupations (machine tending, assembly line operation, clerical data entry) shrink rapidly.[5]

Mitigating the human cost of this transition requires deliberate investment in technical apprenticeships, vocational upskilling, and lifelong education. Engineers who design robot systems bear a direct ethical responsibility to design intuitive, transparent user interfaces, such as collaborative lead-through teaching and block-based offline software, allowing incumbent shop-floor operators to transition into skilled robot programmers rather than facing displacement.[4][5]

06. Privacy: robots as mobile surveillance platforms

In traditional software systems, privacy risks originate primarily from web cookies, search histories, and digital transactions. In robotics, privacy hazards are physical and spatial.[6]

An autonomous mobile robot (AMR) is essentially an embodied sensor suite mounted on a motorized chassis. To navigate dynamic spaces, compute collision-free trajectories, and execute simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), mobile robots collect high-bandwidth spatial data:[6]

  • RGB and depth cameras: Optical lenses capture domestic living areas, office desks, whiteboard notes, and personal human activities in high resolution.[6]
  • 3D LiDAR point clouds: High-frequency laser pulses construct precise millimeter-accurate three-dimensional blueprints of private interior architecture and furniture arrangements.
  • Microphone arrays: Directional acoustic sensors record ambient human conversations to support natural voice commands.

The severity of these privacy risks was highlighted in December 2022 when an MIT Technology Review investigation revealed that development-series iRobot Roomba J7 vacuums had recorded intimate household photographs in 2020.[6] The images included private photographs of a woman seated on a toilet. The images were transmitted to cloud servers and subsequently shared with third-party data annotation contractors in Venezuela employed by Scale AI. Contractors breached confidentiality agreements and shared multiple sensitive screenshots on private Facebook and Discord groups.[6]

This incident demonstrated that consumer robotics data flows through complex, multi-tiered global supply chains. Even when end users sign standard terms of service, third-party contractors and automated cloud pipelines may expose intimate private moments to unauthorized human eyes.[6]

07. Data minimization and edge privacy architectures

To protect user privacy, robotics software architectures must enforce the principle of data minimization.[7] Article 5(1)(c) of the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) mandates that personal data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed."[7]

Which practice best applies data minimization to a robot vacuum's camera? The correct engineering practice is to collect only what the task needs, process it on the robot when possible and keep it no longer than necessary.[6][7]

Translating data minimization into concrete technical architecture involves several key design patterns:

  • On-device edge computing: Run computer vision models and SLAM algorithms locally on onboard embedded processors (such as an NVIDIA Jetson or Qualcomm Robotics RB5). Once an obstacle's 3D bounding box is computed, the raw camera frame is immediately purged from RAM without ever touching persistent flash memory or leaving the robot.
  • Feature extraction over raw pixels: Transmit abstract vector coordinates or occupancy grid costmaps rather than streaming raw RGB textures to remote cloud telemetry databases.
  • Hardware privacy interlocks: Equip consumer and healthcare robots with physical mechanical lens covers and hardware power disconnect switches for microphones, giving users physical proof that passive recording cannot occur.
  • Ephemeral logging: Ensure diagnostic error logs and crash telemetry are strictly time-bounded, cryptographically encrypted at rest, and stripped of personally identifiable information (PII) before transmission.

08. Accountability and the moral crumple zone

When a manual machine causes injury, determining legal responsibility is relatively straightforward: the human operator who pressed the lever or the technician who failed to service the brakes is held accountable. When an autonomous robot causes physical damage or injury, the chain of causation becomes fragmented across hundreds of human actors: the perception software engineer, the training data curator, the systems integrator, the factory owner, and the on-shift operator.[8]

In 2019, science and technology studies scholar Madeleine Clare Elish conceptualized this dynamic as the moral crumple zone.[8] Just as the crumple zone in an automobile is engineered to absorb the physical impact of a collision, the human operator in an automated system becomes the "moral crumple zone" engineered to absorb legal and moral liability when an autonomous system fails.[8]

Elish documented how, when semi-autonomous systems (such as autopilot-equipped vehicles or commercial aviation flight control systems) encounter edge cases and crash, public discourse, corporate legal teams, and regulatory investigations routinely blame the human operator for failing to intervene within fractions of a second. Yet the automation was marketed as self-driving, and human physiology is fundamentally ill-suited to maintain hyper-vigilant passive monitoring over extended periods of time.[8]

To eliminate moral crumple zones, engineers and system architects must design clear governance and control paradigms:

  • Human-in-the-Loop (HITL): The robot cannot execute a critical action without explicit, affirmative human validation. For example, a semi-autonomous surgical robot identifies tissue boundaries, but requires the surgeon to depress a foot pedal to actuate the cutting laser.
  • Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL): The robot executes actions autonomously, but a human supervisor monitors operations in real time and retains override authority to intervene or halt the system if abnormal behavior is detected.
  • Human-in-Command (HIC): The human oversees the broader strategic and societal context, defining operational safety parameters, ethical rules of engagement, and retaining the legal power to disable the autonomous system entirely.

09. Questions to ask before you build

Before deploying an autonomous robotic system into a shared workspace, public environment, or domestic home, engineers should evaluate five fundamental socio-technical questions drawn from IEEE 7000-2021 and the EU AI Act:[9][10]

  • 1. People: Whose daily tasks and livelihoods will change because of this robot? Are mechanisms in place to provide operators with technical retraining and career pathways?
  • 2. Data: What ambient sensors does the robot carry? Is all captured data strictly necessary for navigation? Does the system practice data minimization through local edge computing?
  • 3. Oversight: Can human operators readily comprehend what the robot is intending to do, and can they reliably intervene or override its actions in real time?
  • 4. Responsibility: If the robot causes property damage or injury, who is legally and ethically accountable? Are operational decisions logged deterministically in a tamper-proof flight recorder?
  • 5. Rules: Which harmonized safety standards (ISO 10218, ISO 13849) and legal regulations (EU Machinery Regulation, EU AI Act) apply to the deployment location?
Try it
  1. Select a commercial autonomous mobile robot (such as an automated hospital delivery bot or warehouse order picker) and audit its sensor suite against GDPR data minimization principles.
  2. Analyze an automated factory upgrade using the task framework: list two specific manual tasks that are displaced, one task that experiences productivity expansion, and two newly reinstated technical tasks.
  3. Draft an architectural proposal for an autonomous drone delivery system that eliminates the "moral crumple zone" by establishing clear algorithmic handoffs and logging mechanisms between autonomous flight software and remote human dispatchers.
