4.3 Robots and Society

Gerald Lê

01. Expanding our perspective: robots in society

The previous lessons examined professional engineering ethics and physical machine safety within industrial work cells. When robots leave controlled manufacturing enclosures and enter the wider economy, they reshape society as well. According to the International Federation of Robotics (IFR), factories worldwide installed more than 600,000 new industrial robots in 2025, and a record 5 million were in operation.[1] Beyond factories, mobile robots move pallets in warehouses, robot vacuums map private homes, and surgical robots assist inside patients.

These deployments raise societal questions: how do industrial robots change employment and wages? How can privacy be protected when mobile platforms carry cameras and lidar? Who bears moral and legal responsibility when an autonomous machine makes a wrong decision? Engineering decisions cannot be separated from their economic and legal consequences. This lesson reviews the empirical evidence and the governance frameworks that can guide responsible design.

02. Empirical evidence on jobs and automation

Popular debates on robotics swing between promises of effortless abundance and warnings of permanent mass unemployment. Labor economics tries to replace speculation with measurement.[2]

A benchmark study of the employment effects of industrial robots was carried out by Daron Acemoglu (MIT) and Pascual Restrepo (Boston University) and published in the Journal of Political Economy in 2020.[2] They compared US commuting zones (local labor markets) with different exposure to robots, measured from industry-level advances in robotics and each area's mix of industries. They showed that the areas most exposed to robots after 1990 had no different trends before then, and that the impact of robots was distinct from that of other capital and technologies.[2]

Acemoglu and Restrepo studied US labor markets from 1990 to 2007. What did they find about one more robot per thousand workers? It reduced the employment-to-population ratio by 0.2 percentage points and wages by 0.42%.[2]

The size of the estimate changed between versions of the study. The 2017 working paper gave a range of 0.18 to 0.34 percentage points for employment and 0.25 to 0.5 percent for wages, and its summary described the arrival of one new robot in a local labor market as coinciding with an employment drop of 5.6 workers.[3] Headline numbers like these are averages across many regions; they describe exposed local labor markets, not every worker or every country.

03. The task framework: displacement, productivity, and reinstatement

To explain why automation affects labor in more than one way, Acemoglu and Restrepo set out a task-based framework in the Journal of Economic Perspectives.[4] Instead of treating production as a mix of abstract "capital" and "labor," the framework treats it as a set of tasks (such as machining parts, routing circuit boards, scheduling logistics and troubleshooting errors), each of which can be done by people or by machines.[4]

When automation advances, it affects employment through three competing mechanisms:[4]

  • 1. The displacement effect: Machines take over tasks that people used to do. Automation always reduces labor's share of value added, and it may reduce the demand for labor even while it raises productivity.[4] For example, replacing manual spot-welders with robot arms removes the welding task from human workers.
  • 2. The productivity effect: Automation lowers costs and raises productivity, which can increase demand for labor in the tasks that are not automated.[4]
  • 3. The reinstatement effect: New technology creates new tasks in which labor has a comparative advantage. New tasks always raise labor's share and labor demand.[4]

What is the reinstatement effect? In the task framework, it is technological change creating new complex tasks and occupations in which humans have a comparative advantage.[4] Robotics itself offers examples of such tasks: integrating robot cells, programming robots offline in simulation, maintaining and troubleshooting robots, and checking that cells meet safety standards.

Whether automation helps or harms overall employment depends on the balance between these effects. Acemoglu and Restrepo's decomposition of US data attributes slower employment growth over the last three decades to an acceleration of the displacement effect, especially in manufacturing, together with a weaker reinstatement effect and slower productivity growth than in earlier decades.[4]

04. Global robot density and industrial trends

The scale of industrial automation is tracked every year by the IFR in its World Robotics reports. In 2025, installations grew 11% to more than 600,000 units, and the global operational stock grew 9% to a record 5 million units.[1]

To compare automation intensity between countries, the IFR uses robot density: the number of operational industrial robots per 10,000 employees in manufacturing. In 2023 the global average reached a record 162, but national densities varied widely:[5]

Source: IFR, World Robotics 2024.[5] China's density rose from 402 in 2022 to 470 in 2023, overtaking Germany and Japan, and the IFR attributes this to massive investment in automation technology.[5] Density figures show where robots are used most, but on their own they do not tell us what robots do to jobs and wages; that question needs studies such as Acemoglu and Restrepo's, which compare places with different exposure over time.[2]

05. Job polarization and workforce transition

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has studied how automation could affect jobs in its member countries.[6] Using data from its Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC) for 32 countries, the OECD estimated in 2018 that:[6][7]

  • About 14% of jobs are at high risk: These jobs have a probability of automation above 70%.[7]
  • A further 32% are likely to change significantly: These jobs have a probability of automation between 50% and 70%, so many of their tasks could change even if the job itself remains.[7][6]

A central consequence of automation so far has been labor market polarization rather than the disappearance of work. David Autor describes how, in recent decades, wage gains in the United States went disproportionately to workers at the top and the bottom of the income and skill distribution, not to those in the middle.[8] Computers substitute most easily for workers in routine, codifiable tasks, while they amplify the advantage of workers who supply problem-solving skills, adaptability and creativity. Autor also argues that this polarization is unlikely to continue very far into the future.[8]

Reducing the human cost of this transition takes deliberate investment in apprenticeships, retraining and adult education. Engineers can help too: a robot cell that operators can retool, troubleshoot and reprogram (for example through lead-through teaching or simple graphical programming) creates skilled work around the robot instead of only removing tasks.

06. Privacy: robots as mobile surveillance platforms

In traditional software, privacy risks come mainly from cookies, search histories and online transactions. In robotics, the risks are also physical and spatial: to work, many robots must see and remember their surroundings.[9]

A mobile robot is a set of sensors on a motorized base. To navigate, avoid collisions and build maps, it may collect:

  • RGB and depth cameras: Images of living rooms, office desks, whiteboards and people going about their lives, as the Roomba case below shows.[9]
  • Lidar point clouds and maps: Detailed geometry of rooms and furniture, which reveals the layout of a home or a facility.
  • Microphone arrays: Audio of the surroundings, recorded to support voice commands.

The severity of these risks was highlighted in December 2022, when MIT Technology Review reported that development versions of iRobot's Roomba J7 series had taken intimate photographs inside homes in 2020, including one of a woman on the toilet.[9] The images were sent to Scale AI, whose contract workers in Venezuela labeled them to train the robot's AI, and some of those workers posted them in private groups on Facebook, Discord and other platforms.[9] iRobot said the images came from special development robots given to paid data collectors and employees who had signed written agreements acknowledging that video and other data would be sent back for training.[9]

The incident shows that robot data can flow through long, multi-company supply chains. Even when the people who own or test a device have signed an agreement, others who appear in the images have not, and contractors and cloud pipelines can expose private moments far from where they were recorded.[9]

07. Data minimization and edge privacy architectures

The main defense is data minimization. Article 5(1)(c) of the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) requires that personal data be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed."[10] The IEEE Code of Ethics also lists protecting the privacy of others as a duty.[11]

Which practice best applies data minimization to a robot vacuum's camera? The correct engineering practice is to collect only what the task needs, process it on the robot when possible and keep it no longer than necessary, and to tell people clearly what is recorded.[10]

Turning data minimization into a technical architecture involves several design patterns:

  • On-device edge computing: Run vision models and mapping on the robot's own embedded computer. Once the robot has extracted what it needs (for example, an obstacle's position), it discards the raw camera frame instead of storing or uploading it.
  • Features instead of raw pixels: Send compact results such as occupancy-grid maps or obstacle coordinates to remote services, rather than streaming raw images.
  • Hardware privacy controls: Fit consumer and healthcare robots with lens covers and hardware switches that disconnect microphones, so users can see that recording is impossible.
  • Short-lived, protected logs: Keep diagnostic logs for a limited time, encrypt them, and strip personal information before sending them anywhere.

08. Accountability and the moral crumple zone

When a manually controlled machine causes an injury, it is often fairly clear who is responsible. When an autonomous system causes harm, responsibility is spread across many people: the perception software engineer, the people who prepared the training data, the systems integrator, the company that deployed the system and the operator on shift.[12]

In 2019, Madeleine Clare Elish of the Data & Society Research Institute introduced the idea of a moral crumple zone to describe how responsibility for an action may be misattributed to a human actor who had only limited control over an automated or autonomous system.[12] Just as the crumple zone of a car is designed to absorb the force of a crash, the human operator can end up absorbing the moral and legal blame when the larger system fails.[12]

Elish developed the idea from high-profile accidents involving complex automated socio-technical systems; her sources include the Three Mile Island nuclear accident, the loss of Air France flight 447 and the 2018 crash of an Uber self-driving test vehicle in Tempe, Arizona.[12] In such cases the human operator tends to be blamed for failing to intervene in time, even though people are poorly suited to monitoring an automated system passively for long periods and then taking over at a moment's notice.

To avoid moral crumple zones, engineers need to design oversight deliberately. The EU's High-Level Expert Group on AI names three approaches to human oversight:[13]

  • Human-in-the-loop (HITL): A person takes part in each decision cycle. For example, a surgical robot proposes a cut, but the surgeon must confirm it before the robot acts.[13]
  • Human-on-the-loop (HOTL): The system acts on its own, but a person monitors its operation and can intervene or stop it.[13]
  • Human-in-command (HIC): People oversee the system's overall activity and wider impact, and decide when and how it is used, including deciding not to use it.[13]

09. Questions to ask before you build

Several frameworks can structure this thinking. IEEE 7000-2021 gives a process for eliciting stakeholders' values and tracing them into system requirements.[14] The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) sorts AI systems into four levels of risk (unacceptable, high, transparency and minimal) and requires high-risk systems to have risk management, logging, documentation and appropriate human oversight.[15][16] In 2026, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 moved AI in machinery, such as industrial robots, under the EU Machinery Regulation (Annex I, Section B of the AI Act), which is to add the AI requirements through delegated acts by 2 August 2028.[17] Before deploying an autonomous robot into a shared workspace, a public space or a home, ask five questions:

  • 1. People: Whose daily tasks and livelihoods will change because of this robot? How will they learn new tasks, and what career paths will they have?
  • 2. Data: What does the robot sense and store? Is all of it needed? Can it be processed on the robot? Where does it go and who can see it?
  • 3. Oversight: Can a person understand what the robot is about to do, and stop or override it in time?
  • 4. Responsibility: If the robot causes damage or injury, who is accountable? Are its decisions logged so that someone can find out what happened?
  • 5. Rules: Which safety standards (such as ISO 10218 and ISO 13849) and laws (such as the EU Machinery Regulation and the EU AI Act) apply where it will be used? (See 4.2.)
Try it
  1. Select a commercial autonomous mobile robot (such as an automated hospital delivery bot or warehouse order picker) and audit its sensor suite against GDPR data minimization principles.
  2. Analyze an automated factory upgrade using the task framework: list two specific manual tasks that are displaced, one task that experiences productivity expansion, and two newly reinstated technical tasks.
  3. Draft an architectural proposal for an autonomous drone delivery system that eliminates the "moral crumple zone" by establishing clear algorithmic handoffs and logging mechanisms between autonomous flight software and remote human dispatchers.
