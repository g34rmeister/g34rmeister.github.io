5.1 Arduino for Robotics

Gerald Lê

01. Microcontrollers at the physical frontier

High-level robotic architectures such as ROS 2 often execute on single-board computers or industrial servers running general-purpose operating systems like Linux. However, general-purpose operating systems are not hard real-time systems: the Linux task scheduler can preempt a navigation thread to handle networking or disk I/O, introducing unpredictable jitter ranging from hundreds of microseconds to several milliseconds.[1]

At the physical hardware frontier, robots demand microsecond-deterministic timing. Motor commutation loops, quadrature encoder tick counting, and ultrasonic echo measurements cannot tolerate scheduling delays. For embedded physical control, roboticists rely on microcontrollers: self-contained integrated circuits containing a processor core, memory, and programmable input/output peripherals on a single silicon die.[1] The Arduino platform serves as the premier open-source environment for prototyping robotic embedded control systems.[1]

02. Hardware architecture: Arduino Uno R3 versus Uno R4

When designing an embedded robot controller, selecting the appropriate board generation dictates electrical limits and computational throughput. The classic benchmark is the Arduino Uno R3, powered by the Microchip ATmega328P microcontroller,[2] while modern designs increasingly utilize the 32-bit Arduino Uno R4,[3] powered by the Renesas RA4M1.[4]

Hardware Specification Arduino Uno R3 Arduino Uno R4 (Minima / WiFi)
Microcontroller (MCU) Microchip ATmega328P (8-bit AVR RISC)[2] Renesas RA4M1 (32-bit Arm Cortex-M4 with FPU)[4]
Clock Frequency 16 MHz 48 MHz (3x clock speed, 32-bit operations)
Flash Memory / SRAM 32 KB Flash, 2 KB SRAM, 1 KB EEPROM 256 KB Flash, 32 KB SRAM, 8 KB Data Flash
Operating Voltage 5.0 V logic 5.0 V logic (5V shield compatibility)
Input Voltage Range (VIN) 7 V to 12 V (linear regulator) 6 V to 24 V (high-efficiency buck regulator)
DC Current per I/O Pin 20 mA recommended (40 mA absolute max)[2] 8 mA maximum (cannot drive optocouplers directly)[4]
ADC Resolution 10-bit (1024 steps, ~4.88 mV per step)[2] 14-bit (configured as 12-bit: 4096 steps, ~1.22 mV)[4]
True Analog DAC Output None (PWM approximation only) 12-bit true DAC on analog pin A0
External Interrupt Pins Pins 2 (INT0) and 3 (INT1) only[2] All digital pins (pins 0 to 13)[4]
Built-in Peripherals UART, SPI, I2C UART, SPI, I2C, CAN Bus, Op-Amp, USB HID

The current limit per GPIO pin is a vital distinction for roboticists. On the Uno R3, an ATmega328P pin can comfortably supply up to 20 mA, sufficient to directly light standard LEDs or trigger low-current optocouplers.[2] On the Uno R4, the Renesas RA4M1 limits DC current to 8 mA per pin: attempting to draw 20 mA directly from an R4 pin will trigger thermal degradation or burn out the internal silicon output buffer.[4]

03. GPIO pin functions and input modes

An Arduino Uno exposes 14 digital input/output pins (labeled 0 through 13) and 6 analog input pins (labeled A0 through A5).[1] Understanding their electrical behavior prevents erratic software bugs:

  • Digital Pins (0–13): Read or output binary voltages: logic HIGH (5.0 V) or logic LOW (0 V / Ground). Pins 0 (RX) and 1 (TX) connect directly to the USB serial transceiver. Connecting sensors or motors to pins 0 and 1 while attempting to debug via the Serial Monitor causes bus contention, communication errors, and sketch upload failures.[1]
  • PWM Output Pins (~): On the Uno R3, pins 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, and 11 feature internal timer-counter peripherals capable of generating Pulse Width Modulation waves to modulate average electrical power.[2]
  • Analog Input Pins (A0–A5): Connect to an internal successive-approximation Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). On the Uno R3, analogRead(pin) maps voltages between 0 V and 5.0 V to an integer between 0 and 1023. Each step represents approximately \(5.0\text{ V} / 1024 \approx 4.88\text{ mV}\).[2]

When configuring a digital input pin using pinMode(), leaving the pin unconnected results in a floating input.[5] In a high-impedance state, an unconnected pin acts like a miniature antenna, picking up ambient capacitive and electromagnetic noise from nearby power lines and motors. The digital read will flutter randomly between 0 and 1.[5]

To prevent floating inputs when connecting mechanical switches or limit bumpers, roboticists configure the pin mode as pinMode(pin, INPUT_PULLUP).[5] This activates an internal pull-up resistor inside the microcontroller (20 to 50 k\(\Omega\) on the ATmega328P), tying the input pin firmly to the 5 V rail. The default unpressed state is guaranteed to read HIGH. When the robot's bumper switch closes, it connects the pin to Ground, pulling the voltage to 0 V and producing a clean, noise-free logic LOW.[2][5]

04. Actuator power: why microcontrollers cannot drive motors

A beginner mistake in robotics is attempting to wire a DC motor or large hobby servo directly to an Arduino GPIO pin or to the board's 5V power header. This will instantly malfunction or permanently destroy the microcontroller board.[1]

Two fundamental physical laws explain why direct motor connection fails:

  • Current demands versus pin limits: An Arduino Uno R3 pin can supply a maximum of 20 mA (Uno R4: 8 mA), and the onboard 5V linear voltage regulator can provide at most 500 mA before overheating. In contrast, even a miniature yellow plastic hobby DC gearmotor draws 150 to 300 mA when spinning freely with no load. When the motor starts up, accelerates, or encounters mechanical resistance, its stall current surges to 1.5 A to 3.0 A: between 75 and 150 times the safe current rating of a microcontroller pin.[2]
  • Inductive kickback and back-EMF: A motor is fundamentally an electromagnetic coil: a powerful inductor. According to Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, the voltage across an inductor is proportional to the rate of change of current: \(V_L = -L \frac{di}{dt}\). When the microcontroller abruptly switches a motor off, the sudden interruption of current causes the collapsing magnetic field to generate an enormous reverse voltage spike (often exceeding 50 to 100 V). This high-voltage inductive kickback punches through the microscopic silicon oxide insulation of the microcontroller's CMOS transistors, permanently frying the chip.[6]

05. H-bridge motor drivers: L298N versus TB6612FNG

To protect the microcontroller while supplying the high currents required by motors, roboticists use an intermediate power amplifier known as an H-bridge motor driver.[6] An H-bridge consists of four solid-state electronic switches arranged in an "H" shape around the motor. By closing diagonal pairs of switches, current flows forward or backward through the motor windings, enabling bidirectional rotation. Modulating the switches with a high-speed logic signal regulates speed.[6]

Two popular motor driver modules illustrate the evolutionary leap in power electronics:

  • STMicroelectronics L298N: An older dual full-bridge driver constructed using Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJTs).[6] Because BJTs suffer from a substantial collector-emitter saturation voltage drop (\(V_{CE(sat)} \approx 1.8\text{ V}\) to \(3.2\text{ V}\) across upper and lower transistors combined), significant energy is wasted directly as heat. When driving a 2 A load, the L298N dissipates up to 6 watts of pure heat, requiring a massive finned aluminum heatsink. Furthermore, BJTs lack intrinsic body diodes: an L298N circuit requires external high-speed flyback diodes (such as 1N4007 or Schottky diodes) wired across each output to clamp inductive voltage spikes.[6]
  • Toshiba TB6612FNG: A modern, highly efficient dual full-bridge driver constructed using Power MOSFETs.[7] Power MOSFETs operate with an exceptionally low on-resistance: the TB6612FNG features an on-resistance of just \(R_{DS(ON)} \approx 0.5\ \Omega\) combined across high-side and low-side transistors. At 1 A current, the voltage drop is less than 0.5 V. The module remains cool to the touch and requires no bulky heatsink. It delivers 1.2 A continuous current (3.2 A peak pulse), includes built-in fast flyback protection diodes, and features internal thermal shutdown circuitry.[7]

06. Power architecture: the common ground rule

How should you power a DC motor in an Arduino robot? The foundational rule of robotics electrical architecture is clear: from a separate battery through a motor driver, with grounds connected.[6][7]

Operating a robot requires two isolated power domains:

  1. The Logic Domain: The sensitive 5V digital circuitry of the Arduino board, which can be powered via a USB power bank or a dedicated battery through a clean switching regulator.
  2. The Motor Power Domain: A high-discharge battery pack (such as a 7.4V or 11.1V Lithium Polymer pack) connected directly to the high-voltage power terminals (VCC / VMOT) of the motor driver.

Crucially, the Ground (GND) wire from the motor battery and the Ground (GND) pin on the Arduino must be connected together.[6] What happens if you do not connect the Arduino ground to the motor battery ground? The logic signals float without a shared 0V reference, causing erratic motor behavior or complete failure to switch.[6]

In electrical engineering, voltage is not an absolute quantity: it is a difference in electrical potential between two physical points. When the Arduino sets digital pin 9 to HIGH (5.0 V), that 5.0 V is measured relative to the Arduino's own Ground pin. If the motor driver's logic circuit does not share that exact same Ground, it cannot measure whether pin 9 is at 5 V or 0 V. The floating control inputs pick up electrical noise, resulting in unpredictable motor stuttering, sudden full-speed runaway, or fried driver logic.[6]

07. Mobile robot power: Lithium Polymer (LiPo) safety

Autonomous mobile robots require lightweight, high-energy-density power sources. Modern mobile robotics relies almost universally on Lithium Polymer (LiPo) rechargeable batteries.[8] However, the high electrochemical reactivity of lithium makes LiPo packs hazardous if mistreated. Every robotics engineer must understand cell chemistry and safety limits:[8]

  • Nominal cell voltage: A single LiPo cell (1S) has a nominal voltage of 3.7 V. Multiple cells are wired in series to create higher voltages: a 2S pack produces 7.4 V nominal, while a 3S pack produces 11.1 V nominal.[8]
  • Maximum charged voltage: When fully charged, each cell reaches exactly 4.20 V (8.4 V for 2S, 12.6 V for 3S). Charging a cell beyond 4.25 V causes lithium plating and electrolyte breakdown, which can ignite violent combustion.[8]
  • Critical discharge floor: Discharging a LiPo cell below 3.0 V per cell causes permanent chemical damage. Below 3.0 V, copper from the anode dissolves into the electrolyte solution. When recharged, the dissolved copper forms microscopic metallic spikes (dendrites) that puncture the polymer separator, causing an internal short-circuit and thermal runaway. A safe operational cutoff is 3.5 V per cell under resting conditions.[8]
  • Storage charge: If a robot will sit unused for more than a few days, cells should be maintained at a storage voltage of 3.80 V to 3.85 V per cell to minimize chemical aging and swelling.[8]
  • Discharge C-Rating: The "C" rating denotes maximum safe discharge current. Maximum current equals \(I_{max} = C \times \text{Capacity in Amp-hours}\). For example, a 2200 mAh (2.2 Ah) 25C battery can safely deliver \(2.2 \times 25 = 55\text{ A}\) continuous current.[8]
LiPo safety protocols

Always charge LiPo batteries using a computerized balance charger that monitors individual cell voltages through the multi-pin JST-XH balance lead. Mount a low-voltage battery monitor/buzzer on your mobile robot chassis to sound an alarm before cells drop below 3.5 V. Always store and charge batteries inside fireproof fiberglass LiPo safety bags.

08. Sketch structure and non-blocking sensor timing

An Arduino program (termed a "sketch") is written in C++ and compiled via AVR-GCC or Arm-GCC into raw machine code. Every sketch requires two core functions that mirror robotic execution:[5] 

void setup() {
    // Runs exactly once at hardware power-on or microcontroller reset.
    // Configure pin directions, initialize serial communication, attach servos.
    Serial.begin(115200);
    pinMode(9, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP);
}

void loop() {
    // Executes repeatedly forever in an infinite cyclic loop.
    // Sample sensors, compute control laws, and update motor outputs.
}

A classic beginner pitfall is using delay(milliseconds) to sequence robot movements. The delay() function is a blocking call: it forces the microcontroller CPU into an idle loop, burning clock cycles without executing any other code. If a robot is commanded to turn for 1,000 milliseconds using delay(1000), it cannot read bumper switches, measure distance sensors, or check for an emergency stop command during that entire second.[5]

Professional robotic software uses non-blocking timing via millis() or micros(), recording timestamps and checking elapsed time against target intervals on every loop pass:[5] 

unsigned long previousMillis = 0;
const unsigned long interval = 50; // Execute control loop at 20 Hz

void loop() {
    unsigned long currentMillis = millis();
    if (currentMillis - previousMillis >= interval) {
        previousMillis = currentMillis;
        // Periodic control loop: read sensors and update motors
        updateRobotControl();
    }
    // High-frequency safety checks run on every single pass without delay
    checkEmergencyStop();
}

A standard initial distance sensor for mobile robots is the HC-SR04 ultrasonic rangefinder.[9] The sensor features an acoustic transmitter and receiver. To take a measurement, the microcontroller sends a 10-microsecond HIGH pulse on the Trigger pin, prompting the sensor to emit an 8-cycle ultrasonic burst at 40 kHz. The sensor pulls its Echo pin HIGH until the reflected sound pulse returns.[9]

In dry air at room temperature (20 °C), sound travels at approximately 343 meters per second (or 0.0343 centimeters per microsecond).[10] Because the sound pulse travels from the sensor to the obstacle and bounces back, the total distance traveled is twice the physical separation distance. The distance is computed as:[9][10] 

distance_cm = (echo_time_microseconds * 0.0343) / 2.0;
Try it
  1. Wire an active-low bumper switch to digital pin 2 using pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP). Connect the other terminal of the switch to GND. Verify using the Serial Monitor that the pin reads 1 when open and 0 when pressed, with zero electrical floating.
  2. Connect an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor to an Arduino, trigger a measurement pulse, and print the resulting distance in centimeters to the Serial Monitor at 115,200 baud. Test against flat cardboard targets at 10, 30, and 100 cm.
  3. Wire a dual H-bridge driver (such as a TB6612FNG or L298N) to a separate battery and motor, ensuring the battery Ground and Arduino Ground pins are securely bridged. Test bidirectional motor rotation by toggling the direction pins.
