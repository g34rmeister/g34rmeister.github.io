5.1 Arduino for Robotics

Gerald Lê Draft

01. Why start with Arduino

Before a robot can navigate a room or pick up an object, its “brain” has to be able to interact with the physical world. For most beginners that brain is an Arduino, a small microcontroller board programmed in a simplified form of C++. Robot programming is largely the art of managing inputs (sensors) and outputs (actuators) in real time, and an Arduino makes both easy to try.

02. Know your pins

The pins you choose decide what the robot can do. On a common board like the Arduino Uno:

  • Digital pins (0–13): either HIGH (5 V) or LOW (0 V). Used for simple on/off signals like an LED or a bump switch.
  • PWM pins (marked ~: 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11): can simulate an in-between voltage. This is how you set the speed of a DC motor or the angle of a servo (see PWM).
  • Analog inputs (A0–A5): read a voltage and return a number from 0 to 1023. Used for sensors that report “shades of gray,” such as light sensors or infrared distance sensors.
  • Power pins (VIN, 5V, GND): supply power to small parts.
Rule of thumb

Never power motors directly from the Arduino’s pins or 5V supply. Use a separate battery and a motor driver, and connect the grounds (GND) of the two together.

03. The structure of a sketch

An Arduino program (a “sketch”) has two required functions that mirror how a robot works:

  • setup(): runs once at power-on. This is where you declare which pins are inputs and which are outputs.
  • loop(): runs over and over forever. This is where the robot reads its sensors and updates its motors.

Typical variable types are int for whole numbers such as a PWM value from 0 to 255, float for decimal values such as a distance in centimeters, and bool for true/false state such as obstacleDetected.

04. Driving actuators

  • Servos: hobby servos turn to a commanded angle, usually 0–180°. The built-in Servo library handles the signal: myServo.attach(pin) once, then myServo.write(angle).
  • DC motors: a plain DC motor just spins, and it needs more current than a pin can supply. A motor driver (an H-bridge such as the L298N) sits between the Arduino and the motor. Typically two digital pins set the direction and one PWM pin sets the speed.

05. Reading sensors

The Serial Monitor is your window into what the robot “thinks.” Call Serial.begin(9600) in setup(), then Serial.println(value) to print sensor readings while you debug.

A common first sensor is the HC-SR04 ultrasonic range finder. It sends out a sound pulse and times the echo. Sound travels about 0.034 cm per microsecond, and the pulse goes out and back, so: 

distance_cm = echo_time_us * 0.034 / 2

Inputs that are not connected to anything can “float” and return random readings. Using pinMode(pin, INPUT_PULLUP) turns on the board’s internal pull-up resistor so a switch input reads a stable HIGH until it is pressed.
