4.1 Ethics in Engineering

Gerald Lê

01. Why ethics is an engineering discipline

Engineering is not merely applied mathematics and physics. It is the practice of changing the physical world to serve human needs. When an electrical engineer designs a power grid, a civil engineer designs a bridge, or a robotics engineer programs an industrial manipulator, their technical decisions affect whether people stay safe. That is why the first duty in the main US engineering code of ethics is to the safety, health and welfare of the public.[1]

In robotics, the ethical stakes are physical and immediate. OSHA's guidance on industrial robots describes how a moving arm can strike a worker, or crush and trap a worker against a post or fixture, and how a single bypassed safeguard has led to deaths.[2] As robots leave fenced cells to work beside people, and mobile robots move through shared spaces, these decisions become central to everyday engineering work.

02. The NSPE Code of Ethics and the Fundamental Canons

To guide professional conduct, engineering societies publish formal codes of ethics. In the United States, the National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) publishes the NSPE Code of Ethics for Engineers, most recently revised in July 2019.[1]

The foundation of the NSPE code is its six Fundamental Canons. Engineers, in the fulfillment of their professional duties, shall:[1]

  1. Hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public.
  2. Perform services only in areas of their competence.
  3. Issue public statements only in an objective and truthful manner.
  4. Act for each employer or client as faithful agents or trustees.
  5. Avoid deceptive acts.
  6. Conduct themselves honorably, responsibly, ethically, and lawfully so as to enhance the honor, reputation, and usefulness of the profession.

According to the NSPE Code of Ethics, what must engineers hold paramount? The answer is unambiguous: the safety, health and welfare of the public.[1] Notice the word "paramount." It does not make safety one priority among equals, to be traded against profits, bonuses or delivery deadlines. When public safety conflicts with financial or schedule pressure, safety comes first.

03. The IEEE Code of Ethics

The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) is a worldwide professional society for electrical, computer and robotics engineers. Its Board of Directors approved a revised IEEE Code of Ethics at its meeting of 28 to 29 June 2020, organizing ten numbered duties under three sections:[3][4]

  • I. Integrity and responsible conduct: Members commit to hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public, to protect the privacy of others, and to disclose promptly factors that might endanger the public. They also commit to improve public understanding of conventional and emerging technologies, including intelligent systems, to avoid real or perceived conflicts of interest whenever possible, and to maintain their technical competence.[4]
  • II. Fair treatment and respect: Members commit to treat all persons fairly and with respect, and not to engage in discrimination or in harassment of any kind, including sexual harassment or bullying.[4][3]
  • III. Upholding the code: Members commit to strive to ensure the code is upheld by colleagues and co-workers, to support them in following it, and not to retaliate against individuals who report a violation.[4][3]

04. Conflicts of interest and professional competence

A central part of engineering ethics is handling conflicts of interest. What should an engineer do about a conflict of interest? The NSPE code's answer is to disclose it: "Engineers shall disclose all known or potential conflicts of interest that could influence or appear to influence their judgment or the quality of their services."[1] The IEEE code adds a duty to avoid real or perceived conflicts of interest whenever possible.[4]

A conflict of interest exists whenever an engineer's personal, financial or other business interests could bias their technical judgment, or appear to. For example, suppose a robotics integrator is hired to choose the best vision system for an inspection line, and the lead engineer secretly owns shares in one of the competing sensor start-ups. The engineer can no longer act as an impartial trustee for the client. Hiding the conflict, or ignoring it because the contract is small, breaks the disclosure rule that sits under Canon 4 (faithful agent) and risks a deceptive act under Canon 5.[1] The right response is to disclose the relationship in writing and step back from the vendor evaluation.

Similarly, Canon 2 obliges engineers to work only within their areas of competence.[1] An engineer trained only in computer-vision software should not sign off on the structural welds or the emergency-stop circuits of a heavy industrial manipulator without involving qualified mechanical and safety specialists.

05. Physical safety standards: ISO 10218:2025

Ethical duties are turned into concrete engineering requirements through international consensus standards. For industrial robots, the core standard is ISO 10218, revised in two parts in early 2025.[5][6] In the United States, ANSI and A3 published ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025, adapted from the two 2025 parts, in September 2025.[7]

  • ISO 10218-1:2025 (Industrial robots): Written for the robot manufacturer. It sets safety requirements for the industrial robot itself, treated as partly completed machinery, covering inherently safe design, protective measures and information for use.[5][8]
  • ISO 10218-2:2025 (Industrial robot applications and robot cells): Written for the integrator. It covers how the robot is integrated into an application and cell, and the cell's commissioning, operation, maintenance and decommissioning.[6][8]

A key idea in the 2025 revision is that collaboration belongs to the application, not to the robot alone. The terms "collaborative robot" and "collaborative operation" no longer appear in ISO 10218; the standard speaks of "collaborative applications" instead.[8][7] The revision also folded in the collaborative-application content of ISO/TS 15066, made functional safety requirements more explicit, and added cybersecurity requirements that relate to robot safety.[8][7] In practice, a robot arm with rounded edges and torque sensing can still be part of a dangerous application if it carries a sharp sheet of laser-cut steel or a cutting torch, so the whole application (robot, end-effector, workpiece and surroundings) must be risk-assessed.

06. Collaborative applications under ISO/TS 15066

Where people and robots share space without a fixed guard, OSHA's technical manual lists the technologies a collaborative application can use in automatic mode. Their detailed requirements were published in the Technical Specification ISO/TS 15066:2016, which supplements ISO 10218 and which ISO has now marked for revision.[2][9]

  • 1. Safety-rated monitored stop: The robot stops and is held at a monitored standstill while a person is in the collaborative space, then continues when the person leaves. OSHA notes that this function is used together with one of the other three. The 2025 revision of ISO 10218 renamed it "monitored standstill."[2][7]
  • 2. Hand guiding: The operator moves the robot directly by hand, using a hand-guiding control on the robot.[2]
  • 3. Speed and separation monitoring (SSM): The robot and the person move in the shared space at the same time. Sensors such as area scanners track the distance between them, and the robot slows down or stops to keep a protective separation distance.[2][7]
  • 4. Power and force limiting (PFL): Contact between robot and person is allowed, but the robot's speed, force and power are limited so that a contact stays below set biomechanical limits.[2][10]

07. Biomechanical thresholds for physical contact

To make power and force limiting measurable, ISO/TS 15066 gives a table of biomechanical limits. They come from a pain-onset study (project FP-0317) at the University of Mainz with 100 healthy adult volunteers, and they cover 29 specific body areas grouped into body regions.[10][11] For each body region the specification gives a maximum permissible force for quasi-static contact (clamping or crushing where a body part can be trapped); separate pressure limits (in N/cm²) are given for the specific body areas.[12][10]

Body region Maximum permissible quasi-static force (N)
Skull and forehead 130
Face 65
Chest 140
Upper arms and elbow joints 150
Lower arms and wrist joints 160
Hands and fingers 140
Thighs and knees 220

Source: ISO/TS 15066 body-region force limits as reproduced by Ghanbarzadeh and Najafi.[12] Notice the distinction between the two kinds of contact:[10]

  • Quasi-static contact: A clamping or crushing contact, where a body part is trapped between the moving robot and a fixed object such as a table edge, fixture or wall. Because the person cannot move away, the lower limits in the table apply (for example, 140 N on the chest).[10][12]
  • Transient contact: A short impact lasting less than 0.5 s, where the body is free to recoil. For most body regions the allowed force and pressure are set to at least twice the quasi-static values. No such increase applies to the skull, forehead and face.[10]

Researchers stress that many of these values are preliminary. Newer pain studies, such as the one by Behrens and colleagues at Fraunhofer IFF, propose their own pain-onset limits for 28 body locations.[11] Designers should therefore treat the table as a minimum check, not a guarantee of safety.

08. Industrial accident analysis: OSHA and NIOSH data

Despite robust standards, industrial robots remain heavy machines that can cause severe injury. The OSHA Technical Manual chapter on industrial robots summarizes typical hazards and accident cases.[2]

When do industrial robot accidents actually happen? Studies cited by OSHA, from Sweden and Japan, found that many robot accidents do not occur under normal operating conditions, but during programming, program touch-up and maintenance, and while workers are first exposed to a new application during installation and testing.[2] A NIOSH analysis of US data found 41 robot-related deaths at work from 1992 to 2017. In 78% of them a robot struck the worker while moving under its own power, and many of these strikes happened during robot maintenance.[13]

Key finding from industrial accident data

Most serious robot accidents happen during non-routine work (programming, maintenance, repair, setup and testing) rather than during normal automatic production, when guards and interlocks keep people out of the robot's reach.[2][13]

During normal production, perimeter guards, interlocked doors and presence-sensing devices such as light curtains keep people out of the robot's working space.[2] Injuries happen when people enter that space while the robot can still move.

One OSHA case shows how. A service worker cleaned the surface of a photobeam reflector (a sensor) while the cell was in automatic mode. When the cleaning finished, the sensor gave the robot system its signal to resume its programmed path, and the robot struck the worker. OSHA lists the causes as servicing in automatic mode without safeguarding, insufficient safeguarding, and failing to consider lockout/tagout.[2]

This is why OSHA expects hazardous energy to be controlled under its lockout/tagout (LOTO) rule, 29 CFR 1910.147 (or 1910.333 for electrical work).[2] Before a technician services equipment inside the robot's space, every energy source (electrical supply, compressed air and stored energy such as gravity on a raised axis) is isolated, locked and tagged.

09. The European Union Machinery Regulation 2023/1230

As robots add artificial intelligence, wireless connectivity and autonomous navigation, older machinery law has had to be updated.[14]

On 14 June 2023 the EU adopted Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 on machinery, published in the Official Journal on 29 June 2023. It repeals the Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC.[15] Unlike a directive, which each member state must transpose into national law, a regulation applies directly throughout the EU. It becomes fully applicable on 20 January 2027.[16][14]

The regulation adds several requirements that matter for modern robots:[16]

  • Autonomous mobile machinery: An autonomous machine may have a supervisor. The machine must send information and alerts to the supervisor, who must be able to stop it, restart it, or move it to a safe position, and the machine must operate safely within a defined working area.[16]
  • Cybersecurity: Protection against corruption covers external influences, accidental or intentional, that could lead to dangerous behaviour. Manufacturers must identify safety-critical software and data and keep records of interventions, and a failed communication link on a remote control must not create a dangerous situation.[16][14]
  • Self-evolving behaviour: When machine learning is used to ensure safety functions, machinery and safety components with self-evolving behaviour require third-party assessment by an independent designated organization, called a notified body. The risk assessment must cover how the machine may behave after it is placed on the market, and data on safety-related decisions must be recorded.[16][15]

The rules for AI in machinery changed again in 2026. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the "Digital Omnibus on AI," published 24 July 2026) moved the Machinery Regulation from Section A to Section B of Annex I of the EU AI Act. The AI Act's detailed high-risk requirements will instead be written into the Machinery Regulation through delegated acts, which the Commission must adopt by 2 August 2028 at the latest.[17]

10. Legal liability and professional engineering duty

When an automated cell causes severe injury or death, the law assigns responsibility mainly through product liability and negligence claims:[18][19]

  • Strict product liability: In US law, product liability is generally a strict liability matter: once a plaintiff shows the product was defective, the defendant is liable whether or not it took great care. Defects are usually grouped as design defects, manufacturing defects, and marketing defects (poor instructions or failure to warn).[18][19] For example, a collaborative cell whose pinch points exceed the ISO/TS 15066 limits could be argued to have a design defect.
  • Negligence: Negligence is a separate basis for a claim, in which the injured person argues that a person or company failed to take reasonable care.[18] Recognized standards such as ISO 10218 and ANSI/A3 R15.06 describe accepted good practice, so an engineer who skips the risk assessment they require is in a weak position.
  • EU product liability: The EU's new Product Liability Directive, (EU) 2024/2853, keeps no-fault liability, explicitly treats software as a product, and counts inadequate cybersecurity as a possible defect. Member states must transpose it by 9 December 2026.[20]
  • The duty to speak up: Under the NSPE code, if engineers' judgment is overruled in circumstances that endanger life or property, they "shall notify their employer or client and such other authority as may be appropriate," and they shall approve only engineering documents that conform to applicable standards.[1] The IEEE code likewise requires members to disclose promptly factors that might endanger the public.[4] In a US workplace, an appropriate authority can be OSHA.

11. Case study: production pressure versus safety sign-off

Consider the following hypothetical scenario, of a kind that integrators can meet in practice:

A robotics team has spent four months building an automated palletizing cell for a large distribution center. The contract's late-delivery penalties are severe: every day the cell is not in production costs the integration firm $25,000.

During final commissioning, the lead safety engineer finds an intermittent fault: when an autonomous pallet jack enters the collaborative zone, the laser area scanner sometimes fails to switch the robot into reduced speed, because of sunlight glinting off the polished concrete floor.

The project manager approaches the engineer: "The plant manager wants us live tomorrow morning. The glitch only happens two percent of the time, and the workers wear steel-toed boots anyway. Sign the CE declaration tonight, and we will fix the optical filter next month during scheduled maintenance."

Under the Fundamental Canons, the engineer must refuse.[1] Signing a safety compliance document for a cell with a known, unresolved sensing failure would breach Canon 1 (public safety), Canon 3 (truthful statements) and Canon 5 (deceptive acts), and the rule that engineers approve only documents that conform to applicable standards.[1] The $25,000 daily penalty is real, but the duty to protect human life comes first.

Try it
  1. Review the four collaborative technologies above. Why does Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM) need a separation distance that grows with the robot's speed, rather than one fixed distance?
  2. Explain why industrial accident studies find that most serious robot accidents happen during maintenance, programming, setup and clearing faults rather than normal production. What safety procedure prevents these accidents?
  3. Read the case study above. If you were the junior engineer on that project and your manager pressured you to sign off on the unsafe cell, what specific steps would you take under the NSPE and IEEE codes of ethics?
