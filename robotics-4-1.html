4.1 Ethics in Engineering

Gerald Lê

01. Why ethics is an engineering discipline

Engineering is not merely applied mathematics and physics. It is the practice of modifying the physical world to serve human needs. When an electrical engineer designs a power grid, a civil engineer blueprints a suspension bridge, or a robotics engineer programs a heavy industrial manipulator, their technical calculations directly decide whether human beings return home safely to their families at the end of the day.[1]

In robotics, the ethical stakes are physical and immediate. A single software bug or an improperly bypassed safety interlock can command a 500-kilogram articulated arm to swing at three meters per second, crushing an unsuspecting technician against a steel fixture. As autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) leave fenced factory enclosures to share hospital corridors, retail aisles, and public streets with ordinary pedestrians, ethical responsibility becomes central to every engineering decision.[2]

02. The NSPE Code of Ethics and the Fundamental Canons

To guide professional conduct, national and international engineering societies have established formal ethical codes. In the United States, the National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) publishes the NSPE Code of Ethics for Engineers.[1]

The foundation of the NSPE code is expressed in its six Fundamental Canons. Engineers, in the fulfillment of their professional duties, shall:[1]

  1. Hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public.
  2. Perform services only in areas of their competence.
  3. Issue public statements only in an objective and truthful manner.
  4. Act for each employer or client as faithful agents or trustees.
  5. Avoid deceptive acts.
  6. Conduct themselves honorably, responsibly, ethically, and lawfully so as to enhance the honor, reputation, and usefulness of the profession.

According to the NSPE Code of Ethics, what must engineers hold paramount? The answer is unambiguous: the safety, health and welfare of the public.[1] Notice the word "paramount." It does not mean safety is one priority among equals, to be balanced against quarterly company profits, executive bonuses, or tight product delivery deadlines. In any conflict between public safety and financial or schedule pressure, safety must always prevail.[1]

03. The IEEE Code of Ethics

The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) represents hundreds of thousands of electrical, computer, and robotics engineers worldwide. The IEEE Board of Directors approved an updated IEEE Code of Ethics in June 2020, organizing professional duties under three core sections:[2]

  • I. Integrity and Responsible Conduct: Reaffirms holding paramount public safety, health, and welfare; obligates engineers to protect the privacy of others; requires prompt disclosure of hazards; and introduces an explicit professional duty to foster public understanding of emerging technologies, including intelligent systems and autonomous robots.[2]
  • II. Fair Treatment: Mandates treating all persons fairly and with respect, strictly prohibiting harassment and unlawful discrimination in engineering teams.[2]
  • III. Upholding the Code: Obligates engineers to assist colleagues in professional development, follow ethical standards, and protect whistleblowers from retaliation when they report safety breaches.[2]

04. Conflicts of interest and professional competence

A central focus of engineering ethics is navigating professional integrity and conflicts of interest.[1] What should an engineer do about a conflict of interest? The ethical standard requires that the engineer disclose it immediately and completely.[1]

A conflict of interest occurs whenever an engineer's personal, financial, or secondary business interests could bias, or appear to an objective observer to bias, their technical judgment.[1] For example, if a robotics integrator is hired by a manufacturing client to select the best vision system for a quality inspection line, and the lead engineer secretly owns equity in one of the competing sensor startups, a direct conflict exists. The engineer cannot remain an impartial trustee for the client. Hiding the conflict or ignoring it because the contract is small violates Canon 4 and Canon 5. The engineer must disclose the relationship in writing to all stakeholders and recuse themselves from vendor evaluation.[1]

Similarly, Canon 2 obligates engineers to practice only within their areas of competence. An engineer trained purely in high-level computer vision algorithms must not sign off on the structural welding or electrical emergency-stop circuits of a multi-ton industrial manipulator without consulting qualified mechanical and safety specialists.

05. Physical safety standards: ISO 10218:2025

Ethical duties are translated into concrete engineering requirements through international consensus standards. In industrial robotics, the benchmark standard is ISO 10218 (published in updated editions as ISO 10218-1:2025 and ISO 10218-2:2025).[3]

  • ISO 10218-1:2025 (Robots): Sets requirements for the robot manufacturer, governing mechanical construction, protective stops, axis limiters, enabling devices on teach pendants, and fail-safe control software.[3]
  • ISO 10218-2:2025 (Robot Systems and Integration): Sets requirements for the systems integrator and factory owner. It governs how the arm is integrated into a complete cell with fixtures, tools, physical safety fencing, light curtains, interlocks, and risk assessments.[3]

A fundamental principle of ISO 10218:2025 is that there is no such thing as an inherently "safe" robot: there are only collaborative applications.[3] Even if a collaborative robot arm has soft rounded edges and motor torque sensors, if that robot is holding a razor-sharp sheet of laser-cut steel or an active plasma cutting torch, the application is highly hazardous. The entire application (robot, end-effector, workpiece, and physical environment) must undergo formal hazard identification and risk reduction per ISO 12100.[3]

06. Collaborative applications under ISO/TS 15066

Where humans and robots share space without physical safety cages, ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066:2016 recognize four distinct collaborative operational modes:[3][4]

  • 1. Safety-Rated Monitored Stop (SRMS): The robot operates automatically at high industrial speeds when no human is present. When a worker opens an access gate or steps across an optical laser scanner boundary into the collaborative workspace, the controller initiates a safety-rated category 2 stop. The motors remain powered and position is monitored, but all motion ceases immediately. As soon as the worker exits the zone, automatic production resumes without a manual reset.[4]
  • 2. Hand Guiding (Lead-Through): The operator enters the workspace and maneuvers the arm by grasping hand-guiding controls or an enabling device mounted near the end-effector. The robot's motion is governed entirely by manual inputs, operating under a reduced speed limit of 250 mm/s.[4]
  • 3. Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM): The robot and human can move within the shared workspace simultaneously. Safety laser area scanners or 3D optical cameras continuously track the exact physical separation distance \(S\) between the human and any moving robot link. As the worker approaches, the robot slows down. If the worker crosses the dynamic safety clearance threshold, the robot halts completely before contact can occur.[4]
  • 4. Power and Force Limiting (PFL): Physical contact between the robot mechanism and the human operator is intentionally permitted. The robot's speed, payload inertia, motor torques, and structural geometry are constrained so that any accidental impact remains strictly below human pain and injury thresholds.[4]

07. Biomechanical thresholds for physical contact

To make Power and Force Limiting mathematically rigorous, ISO/TS 15066 established an exhaustive scientific table of biomechanical thresholds.[4] Based on medical trauma research conducted at the University of Mainz, the standard defines the maximum permissible mechanical pressure (in N/cm²) and impact force (in Newtons) that can be applied to 29 distinct human body regions without causing pain onset or skin rupture:[4]

Body Region Max Pressure: Quasi-Static (N/cm²) Max Force: Quasi-Static (N) Max Force: Transient Dynamic (N)
Skull and Forehead 130 N/cm² 130 N Contact Not Permitted
Chest and Pectoral Muscle 140 N/cm² 140 N 280 N
Upper Arm and Deltoid 190 N/cm² 150 N 300 N
Forearm (Radial Side) 180 N/cm² 160 N 320 N
Back of Hand and Finger Joints 200 N/cm² 140 N 280 N
Thigh and Quadriceps 250 N/cm² 220 N 440 N

Notice the crucial distinction between Quasi-Static Contact and Transient Dynamic Contact:[4]

  • Quasi-Static Contact: A crushing or clamping scenario where a human limb or body part is trapped between the moving robot arm and a rigid obstacle (such as a table edge, fixture, or wall). Because the person cannot pull away, forces are strictly capped at lower thresholds (e.g., 140 N on the chest).
  • Transient Dynamic Contact: A momentary bumping collision where the human body is free to recoil or step backward, dissipating kinetic energy over a short duration (under 0.5 seconds). Transient contact permits up to twice the force of quasi-static contact. Contact with the head and neck is strictly prohibited in all collaborative modes.[4]

08. Industrial accident analysis: OSHA statistics

Despite robust standards, industrial robots remain heavy kinetic machines capable of causing severe trauma. In the United States, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) investigates workplace fatalities and publishes accident summaries in the OSHA Technical Manual.[5]

When do industrial robot accidents actually happen? Academic analyses of OSHA and international accident databases (including studies by Jiang and Geller, and Charpentier) reveal a striking pattern:[5][6]

Key Finding from Industrial Accident Data

Over 80% of all robot-related fatalities and critical injuries occur during non-routine operations: maintenance, programming, sensor calibration, mechanical troubleshooting, and clearing part jams. Less than 20% occur during normal automatic production.[5][6]

During normal production, physical perimeter fences, interlocked guard doors, and light curtains keep humans outside the robot's working volume. Fatalities happen when engineers, maintenance technicians, or line operators bypass protective interlocks to enter the live work envelope.

In classic OSHA case studies, workers enter a cell because an arm stopped moving mid-cycle. The worker assumes the power is off or the program has finished. In reality, the controller was simply paused waiting for an external digital input signal (such as a proximity switch on a conveyor). When the worker clears the jam or bumps the switch, the input goes HIGH, and the robot instantly resumes high-speed motion, pinning the worker against a steel post.[5]

This is why OSHA strictly enforces Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) under 29 CFR 1910.147.[5] Whenever a technician enters a robot's mechanical envelope for servicing, all energy sources (electrical main breakers, pneumatic pressure lines, and gravitational counterbalances) must be physically isolated, padlocked, and tagged.

09. The European Union Machinery Regulation 2023/1230

As robotics technology incorporates artificial intelligence, wireless connectivity, and autonomous mobile navigation, traditional machinery directives have required legislative modernization.

In June 2023, the European Union published Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 on Machinery, replacing the older Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC.[7] Unlike a directive, which requires individual national transposition laws, an EU regulation is directly binding in its entirety across all EU member states, entering into full mandatory application in January 2027.[7]

The 2023/1230 regulation introduces vital legal requirements for modern robotics:[7]

  • Autonomous Mobile Machinery: Establishes strict mandatory safety criteria for autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) operating in shared indoor and outdoor human environments.
  • Cybersecurity Protection: Recognizes that cybersecurity is a prerequisite for physical safety. Hardware interfaces and software configurations controlling physical robot motions must be cryptographically protected against unauthorized wireless hacking or malicious tampering.[7]
  • Self-Evolving AI Behavior: Regulates machinery driven by machine learning or neural networks. The regulation mandates that self-evolving AI systems must always operate within predetermined, risk-assessed safety envelopes. A machine learning model cannot be permitted to learn motions that override deterministic safety stops.[7]

10. Legal liability and professional engineering duty

When an automated robot cell causes severe injury or death, the legal system establishes accountability through tort law and product liability statutes:[8]

  • Strict Product Liability: Manufacturers and systems integrators are held strictly liable for physical harm caused by defective products, regardless of whether they exercised care. Defects are classified as design defects (e.g., designing a collaborative arm with pinch points that exceed ISO/TS 15066 limits), manufacturing defects, or failure-to-warn defects (inadequate safety signage or missing manuals).[8]
  • Negligence: In a negligence claim, injured parties must demonstrate that the engineer breached a duty of reasonable care. In court, consensus standards like ISO 10218, ISO/TS 15066, and RIA R15.06 define the standard of care. If an engineer deployed a robot cell without conducting a formal risk assessment or ignoring published force limits, negligence is readily established.[8]
  • The Duty of Whistleblowing: Both the NSPE and IEEE codes explicitly mandate that if an engineer's professional judgment is overruled by a client or supervisor in a way that endangers public safety, the engineer has a non-negotiable ethical obligation to notify their employer, refuse to sign off on the unsafe design, and, if uncorrected, report the condition to relevant safety regulators (such as OSHA).[1][2]

11. Case study: production pressure versus safety sign-off

Consider a real-world scenario frequently encountered in industrial systems integration:

A robotics engineering team has spent four months building an automated palletizing cell for a major logistics distribution center. Contractual delivery penalties are severe: every day the cell fails to enter commercial production costs the integration firm $25,000 in liquidated damages.

During final commissioning, the lead safety engineer discovers an intermittent fault: when an autonomous pallet jack enters the collaborative zone, the laser area scanner occasionally fails to trigger Reduced Mode because of ambient sunlight glinting off polished concrete floors.

The project manager approaches the engineer: "The plant manager is demanding we launch tomorrow morning. The optical glitch only happens two percent of the time, and our workers wear steel-toed boots anyway. Sign the final CE mark safety declaration tonight, and we will patch the optical filter next month during scheduled maintenance."

Under the Fundamental Canons of engineering ethics, the engineer's response must be an absolute refusal.[1] Signing a safety compliance document for a cell known to harbor unresolved optical sensing failures violates Canon 1 (public safety), Canon 3 (truthful statements), and Canon 5 (deceptive acts). The financial penalty of $25,000 per day is real, but an engineer's legal and ethical duty to human life holds absolute priority over contractual costs.

Try it
  1. Review the four collaborative modes under ISO/TS 15066. Why does Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM) require a dynamic, speed-dependent separation buffer rather than a static distance?
  2. Explain why industrial accident statistics show that over 80% of robot fatalities occur during maintenance, setup, and clearing part jams rather than normal production. What safety procedure prevents these accidents?
  3. Read the case study above. If you were the junior engineer on that project and your manager pressured you to sign off on the unsafe cell, what specific steps would you take under the NSPE and IEEE codes of ethics?
