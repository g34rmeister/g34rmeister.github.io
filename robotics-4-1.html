4.1 Ethics in Engineering

Gerald Lê

01. Ethics in engineering

Engineers build things other people depend on, so engineering ethics starts with the public. The National Society of Professional Engineers’ Code of Ethics puts it first: engineers shall hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public. Professional codes such as the NSPE code and the IEEE Code of Ethics (last revised in 2020) share a few core duties:

  • Put safety first: do not approve or ship work you believe is unsafe, and speak up when you see a risk.
  • Work within your competence: take on only tasks you are qualified for, and keep learning.
  • Be honest: report results, limitations and failures truthfully, including your own mistakes.
  • Avoid conflicts of interest: disclose anything that could bias your judgment.
  • Respect others: treat colleagues and the people affected by your work fairly.

02. The NSPE Fundamental Canons

The NSPE Code of Ethics (revised July 2019) opens with six Fundamental Canons. Engineers, in the fulfillment of their professional duties, shall:

  1. Hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public.
  2. Perform services only in areas of their competence.
  3. Issue public statements only in an objective and truthful manner.
  4. Act for each employer or client as faithful agents or trustees.
  5. Avoid deceptive acts.
  6. Conduct themselves honorably, responsibly, ethically, and lawfully so as to enhance the honor, reputation, and usefulness of the profession.

The rules that follow make these concrete. For example, engineers “shall undertake assignments only when qualified by education or experience” and “shall disclose all known or potential conflicts of interest that could influence or appear to influence their judgment.”

03. The IEEE Code of Ethics

IEEE is a professional organization for electrical, electronics and computer engineers, and many robotics engineers are members. Its board approved the current version in June 2020, organized under three principles:

  • I. Integrity and responsible conduct: including holding paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public, protecting the privacy of others, being honest about claims and estimates, and taking on technical work only when qualified or after disclosing your limits.
  • II. Fair treatment: treat all persons fairly and with respect, do not engage in harassment or discrimination, and avoid injuring others.
  • III. Upholding the code: help make sure colleagues follow it too, and do not retaliate against people who report violations.

The 2020 revision added privacy, anti-harassment and non-retaliation language and, notably for robotics, a duty to improve public understanding of “conventional and emerging technologies, including intelligent systems.”

04. Applying the codes in robotics

The codes are general, but each duty has a direct robotics meaning. “Hold paramount the safety of the public” is why robots need emergency stops and safety-rated limits. “Work within your competence” means not commissioning a robot cell without understanding its safety standards. “Be honest” means reporting a robot’s real limitations, not just its best demo. The Ethics in Robotics article covers these in hardware and software.

Try it
  1. Read one of the NSPE canons and describe a robotics situation where it would apply.
  2. Imagine your team is asked to ship a robot before a safety test is finished. Write down which duties from this lesson apply and what you would say.
