2.2 Pulse Width Modulation (PWM)

Gerald Lê Draft

01. What is PWM?

In digital electronics, everything is either on or off. Real robots need something in between: you rarely want a motor at exactly 0% or 100% power. Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) is the trick that makes a digital signal act like an analog one. The pin switches on and off very quickly, and by changing how long it stays on in each cycle, you change the average power delivered.

02. Duty cycle and frequency

Two numbers describe a PWM signal.

  • Duty cycle: the percentage of each cycle the signal is on. 0% is always off, 50% is on half the time, and 100% is always on.
  • Frequency: how many on/off cycles happen per second, in hertz (Hz). At low frequencies an LED visibly flickers and a motor can “chug”; at high frequencies the motor’s inertia smooths the pulses into steady motion.

The average voltage delivered is the duty cycle times the supply voltage: 

V_avg = D * V_cc

On an Arduino, analogWrite(pin, value) sets the duty cycle with a value from 0 (0%) to 255 (100%).

03. Three common uses

  • DC motor speed: a motor averages out the pulses, so a 25% duty cycle behaves roughly like 25% of the supply voltage: slower, with less torque.
  • Servo position: a standard hobby servo reads the width of a pulse repeated about 50 times a second. Around 1.0 ms is one end of travel, 1.5 ms is center and 2.0 ms is the other end. Exact ranges vary by servo.
  • LED brightness: pulsing an LED faster than the eye can follow makes it look dimmer. Doing this on red, green and blue channels mixes colors.

04. The H-bridge

A microcontroller can generate PWM, but it cannot supply the current a motor needs. An H-bridge motor driver (for example the L298N or DRV8833) takes the low-power PWM signal from the controller and uses it to switch high-power current from the battery to the motor. The “H” arrangement of switches also lets the driver reverse the motor’s direction.

05. Practical details

  • Audible whine: if the PWM frequency is in the range of human hearing, motor coils vibrate and whistle. Many motor controllers switch above about 20 kHz to stay silent, at the cost of more heat in the driver from switching.
  • Use hardware PWM: let the microcontroller’s timers generate the signal instead of toggling a pin by hand in code, so the signal stays steady while the program is busy.
  • Dead zone: most DC motors will not move at very low duty cycles because of friction in the gears. Find the minimum value that actually moves your motor and treat it as the floor.
  • Inductive kickback: a motor is an inductor, so cutting its power causes a voltage spike. Make sure the motor driver has flyback diodes to protect the electronics.

06. DC motor PWM vs. servo PWM

07. Example: a gripper

A gripper shows PWM and encoders working together. The duty cycle sets how hard the fingers squeeze, and an encoder reports how far they have closed. A simple strategy closes quickly at a high duty cycle, drops to a low duty cycle as the fingers near the object, and holds with just enough power to keep it from slipping. When the encoder stops changing while PWM is still applied, the fingers have stalled against the object, which means it has been grabbed. The next article covers encoders in detail.
