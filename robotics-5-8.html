5.8 Intro to Kalman Filters

Gerald Lê

01. Why robots require sensor fusion

In mobile robotics, state estimation is governed by a fundamental physical trade-off between two classes of sensors: proprioceptive motion sensors and exteroceptive environmental sensors.[1]

As demonstrated in Lesson 5.6, wheel odometry is smooth and responsive; Borenstein and Feng describe it as "simple, inexpensive, and easy to accomplish in real-time." However, odometry is an integration process: small mechanical errors, floor irregularities such as bumps and cracks, and wheel slip accumulate into the robot's pose estimate. In their words, "the disadvantage of odometry is its unbounded accumulation of errors."[1]

Conversely, exteroceptive sensors (such as ultrasonic distance sensors measuring a known wall, LiDAR scan matchers, or outdoor GPS receivers) do not accumulate integration drift. Their measurements are directly referenced to landmarks in the physical environment. However, such absolute measurements are usually noisier, slower or more costly to obtain, which is why mobile robots typically use odometry "in-between periodic absolute position measurements."[1]

Neither sensor modality is sufficient on its own. Sensor fusion is the mathematical process of combining measurements from multiple disparate sensors such that the resulting state estimate has lower uncertainty than either source alone (Section 04 shows this with numbers).[2]

The premier algorithmic framework for sensor fusion in robotics is the Kalman filter. Published in 1960 by Rudolf E. Kálmán in the Transactions of the ASME, Journal of Basic Engineering,[3] the filter is a recursive algorithm that keeps an estimate of a dynamic system's state together with an explicit measure of its uncertainty (the error covariance). Each new estimate is computed from the previous one and the newest measurement, rather than by reprocessing all past data.[2]

02. Historical origins: Rudolf Kálmán and Apollo navigation

Before 1960, optimal filtering was built on Norbert Wiener's theory. In his paper, Kálmán notes that Wiener's solution of the underlying integral equation covered only "the practically important special case of stationary statistics and rational spectra," that the optimal filter was specified by its impulse response, and that computing it was "often quite involved and poorly suited to machine computation."[3]

Kálmán instead re-examined the problem using the "state transition" method of describing dynamic systems, and his formulation applied to nonstationary as well as stationary statistics.[3] In the fall of 1960, Kálmán visited Stanley F. Schmidt at NASA Ames Research Center in California and presented his paper to Schmidt's Dynamics Analysis Branch, which was studying midcourse navigation and guidance for a circumlunar (Apollo) mission.[4]

Onboard computers of the time were tiny: the Apollo Guidance Computer had 36,864 words of fixed (read-only rope) memory and 2,048 words of erasable core memory, with a word length of 15 bits plus parity.[5] A recursive filter suits such a machine because it needs only the latest estimate and its covariance, not a stored history of measurements.[2] The Ames group's key change was to relinearize the non-linear navigation equations about the current estimated state, a modification that "has come to be known as the 'extended Kalman filter'" (the Extended Kalman Filter, EKF). Potter then devised the first square-root implementation of the filter, which was used for the Apollo system because it worked well on small-word-size onboard computers.[4]

03. The recursive Predict-Update architecture

The Kalman filter operates as a continuous, two-stage recursive loop: the Predict (Time Update) step and the Update (Measurement Update) step. Welch and Bishop call these the "predictor" and "corrector" equations.[2]

Consider a linear discrete-time physical system modeled in state space:

\[\mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{w}_k\]

\[\mathbf{z}_k = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{v}_k\]

Here, \(\mathbf{x}_k\) is the state vector at time step \(k\), \(\mathbf{A}\) is the state transition matrix, \(\mathbf{B}\) is the control input matrix, and \(\mathbf{u}_k\) represents control commands (such as wheel velocity). The term \(\mathbf{w}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{Q})\) represents zero-mean Gaussian process noise with covariance matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\). The sensor measurement vector \(\mathbf{z}_k\) is linked to state space via observation matrix \(\mathbf{H}\), corrupted by zero-mean Gaussian measurement noise \(\mathbf{v}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{R})\) with covariance matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). The two noise sources are assumed to be independent, white and normally distributed.[2]

The two operational stages execute sequentially:

  • Predict Step (Time Update): The filter projects the state estimate forward in time using the mathematical motion model:

    \[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^- = \mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_k\]

    \[\mathbf{P}_k^- = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{k-1}\mathbf{A}^T + \mathbf{Q}\]

    Because real physical motion is inherently imperfect (tires slip, terrain varies), adding the process noise covariance \(\mathbf{Q}\) causes estimation uncertainty \(\mathbf{P}_k^-\) to grow during prediction.[2]
  • Update Step (Measurement Update): When a sensor reading arrives, the filter computes the difference between the physical measurement \(\mathbf{z}_k\) and what the state model expected:

    \[\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_k = \mathbf{z}_k - \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^-\]

    This residual difference \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_k\) is termed the innovation (or measurement residual).[2] Labbe's open textbook calls it simply the residual.[6] The filter computes the optimal Kalman Gain \(\mathbf{K}_k\), which balances trust between prediction and measurement:

    \[\mathbf{K}_k = \mathbf{P}_k^-\mathbf{H}^T (\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_k^-\mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R})^{-1}\]

    The state estimate and its error covariance matrix are updated:

    \[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^- + \mathbf{K}_k \tilde{\mathbf{y}}_k\]

    \[\mathbf{P}_k = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}_k^-\]

    [2] Because new observational data was incorporated, the updated error covariance \(\mathbf{P}_k\) shrinks, indicating greater certainty.

04. One-dimensional scalar worked example

This section is the lesson's own worked example; the numbers are chosen for illustration, and the equations are the standard ones from Section 03 reduced to one dimension.[2][6] To develop concrete numerical intuition, examine a one-dimensional robot driving along a hallway. Its state is a single scalar \(x\), representing position along the hallway in meters. The variance of its position estimate is denoted by scalar \(P\).

In one-dimensional scalar form (\(A = 1, B = 1, H = 1\)), the governing equations simplify to:

Predict:

\[x_{pred} = x + u\]

\[P_{pred} = P + Q\]

Update:

\[K = \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred} + R}\]

\[x = x_{pred} + K \times (z - x_{pred})\]

\[P = (1 - K) \times P_{pred}\]

Suppose the robot begins at estimated position \(x = 2.00\text{ m}\) with variance \(P = 0.04\text{ m}^2\) (standard deviation \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20\text{ m}\)). Its wheel encoders report that the robot drove forward by \(u = 0.50\text{ m}\). We assign a process noise variance of \(Q = 0.01\text{ m}^2\) to account for potential wheel slip during the maneuver.

An ultrasonic range sensor then pings a known corridor wall and reports absolute position \(z = 2.60\text{ m}\). Suppose that bench testing of this sensor gave a measurement noise variance of \(R = 0.05\text{ m}^2\).

We execute the filter calculations step by step:

  1. Predict Position: \[x_{pred} = 2.00 + 0.50 = 2.50\text{ m}\]
  2. Predict Variance: \[P_{pred} = 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.05\text{ m}^2\]
  3. Compute Kalman Gain \(K\): \[K = \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred} + R} = \frac{0.05}{0.05 + 0.05} = \frac{0.05}{0.10} = 0.50\]
  4. Compute State Innovation: \[\text{innovation} = z - x_{pred} = 2.60 - 2.50 = 0.10\text{ m}\]
  5. Update Position Estimate: \[x = x_{pred} + K \times (z - x_{pred}) = 2.50 + 0.50 \times (0.10) = 2.55\text{ m}\]
  6. Update Error Variance: \[P = (1 - K) \times P_{pred} = (1 - 0.50) \times 0.05 = 0.025\text{ m}^2\]

Two vital insights emerge from these calculations:

First, because the predicted state uncertainty (\(P_{pred} = 0.05\)) and the sensor measurement noise (\(R = 0.05\)) were identical, the Kalman gain was exactly \(K = 0.50\). The updated position landed precisely halfway between the kinematic prediction (\(2.50\text{ m}\)) and the sensor measurement (\(2.60\text{ m}\)), at \(2.55\text{ m}\). When the prediction and the measurement are equally accurate, the update is just the average of the two.[6]

Second, the resulting variance \(P = 0.025\text{ m}^2\) is strictly smaller than both the prediction variance (\(0.05\)) and the sensor noise (\(0.05\)). By fusing two uncertain sources of information, the Kalman filter produced an estimate that is more certain than either source alone. In one dimension this always holds, because \(P = \frac{P_{pred} R}{P_{pred} + R}\), which is smaller than both \(P_{pred}\) and \(R\).[6]

The Kalman filter simulator needs JavaScript. With it on, you can step the hallway robot from section 04 and tune Q and R.

05. Embedded C++ implementation

The listing below is the lesson's own example. On an embedded microcontroller, a one-dimensional Kalman filter can be written in a dozen lines of non-blocking C++ (readWheelOdometry() and readUltrasonicDistance() stand for your own sensor functions): 

class KalmanFilter1D {
public:
  float x; // State estimate (position in meters)
  float P; // Estimate error variance
  float Q; // Process noise covariance (motion uncertainty)
  float R; // Measurement noise covariance (sensor noise)

  KalmanFilter1D(float initX, float initP, float processNoiseQ, float sensorNoiseR)
    : x(initX), P(initP), Q(processNoiseQ), R(sensorNoiseR) {}

  // Predict step: call on every odometry update
  void predict(float u) {
    x = x + u;
    P = P + Q;
  }

  // Update step: call when valid sensor measurement arrives
  void update(float z) {
    float K = P / (P + R);
    x = x + K * (z - x);
    P = (1.0f - K) * P;
  }
};

KalmanFilter1D filter(0.0f, 1.0f, 0.01f, 0.05f);

void handleSensors() {
  float deltaDistance = readWheelOdometry();
  filter.predict(deltaDistance);

  float rangeReading = readUltrasonicDistance();
  // Reject sensor dropouts (e.g. out of range or timeout)
  if (rangeReading > 0.02f && rangeReading < 4.00f) {
    filter.update(rangeReading);
  }
}

Notice the crucial architectural pattern in handleSensors(): predict() is called continuously at the high odometry rate (e.g. 50 Hz). The update() routine is called only when a fresh, valid sensor reading is acquired. If the ultrasonic sensor drops an echo or times out, the code simply skips the update step. The filter continues projecting forward using the motion model, and variance \(P\) grows to reflect the lack of external correction. Production filters reject bad readings statistically instead: PX4's flight-controller EKF performs innovation consistency checks and ignores a sensor reading whose innovation is too large compared with the predicted uncertainty.[7] The robot_localization package offers similar rejection thresholds, expressed as Mahalanobis distances.[8] For desktop work in Python, the FilterPy library implements the same predict and update steps in its KalmanFilter class.[9]

06. Tuning process noise Q and measurement noise R

In practical engineering, the performance of a Kalman filter depends critically on properly selecting the noise covariances \(Q\) and \(R\):

  • Measurement Noise Covariance \(R\): Can usually be measured before the filter runs, because sample measurements can be taken off-line.[2] Park the robot stationary on the floor, collect several thousand static readings from the range sensor, and compute the mathematical variance of the sample set: \[R = \sigma_z^2 = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (z_i - \bar{z})^2\]
  • Process Noise Covariance \(Q\): Represents physical disturbances not captured in the kinematic model. It is harder to determine than \(R\) because the process usually cannot be observed directly, so \(Q\) is normally tuned, often off-line.[2] Setting \(Q\) too large signals that the motion model is untrustworthy, forcing the filter to track noisy sensor readings aggressively (producing jitter).[8][10] Setting \(Q\) too small causes the filter to over-trust its kinematic model, responding sluggishly to genuine sensor corrections. Such a filter can become “smug”: its covariance \(P\) keeps shrinking, reporting ever-better accuracy, while the estimate actually drifts away from the truth.[10]

The mathematical behavior of the Kalman gain \(K = \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred} + R}\) demonstrates two limiting regimes: as \(R\) approaches zero the gain weights the residual more heavily, and as the predicted covariance approaches zero it weights the residual less heavily.[2]

  • Small Sensor Noise (\(R \ll P_{pred}\)): When sensor noise \(R\) is small compared to predicted uncertainty \(P_{pred}\), the ratio approaches 1: \[K \to \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred}} = 1.0\] The state update becomes \(x \approx x_{pred} + 1.0 \times (z - x_{pred}) = z\). The filter disregards its kinematic prediction and follows the sensor reading directly.[2]
  • Large Sensor Noise (\(R \gg P_{pred}\)): When sensor noise \(R\) is huge compared to predicted uncertainty, the denominator is dominated by \(R\): \[K \to \frac{P_{pred}}{R} \approx 0.0\] The state update becomes \(x \approx x_{pred} + 0.0 \times (z - x_{pred}) = x_{pred}\). The filter ignores the noisy sensor and relies entirely on its motion model. (Strictly, Welch and Bishop state the second case in terms of the predicted covariance approaching zero; a very large \(R\) has the same effect on \(K\).)[2]

07. Non-linear state estimation: EKF versus UKF

The standard Kalman filter assumes that the system dynamics and observation models are linear; the extended Kalman filter exists for the non-linear case.[2] However, mobile robot kinematics are non-linear because of the trigonometric terms in the motion equations (such as \(\dot{x} = v \cos\theta\) and \(\dot{y} = v \sin\theta\)).

When a Gaussian probability distribution passes through a non-linear transformation, the output distribution is distorted and is no longer Gaussian.[2][11] Two non-linear algorithms address this challenge:

  • Extended Kalman Filter (EKF): Linearizes non-linear functions around the current state estimate, "in something akin to a Taylor series," using matrices of partial derivatives (Jacobians).[2] It was one of the first techniques used for non-linear problems, and it remains the most common one.[11] This requires computing Jacobian matrices of partial derivatives at each time step: \[\mathbf{F}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \right|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}}, \quad \mathbf{H}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \right|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^-}\] For the differential-drive model, the heading column of the Jacobian \(\mathbf{F}\) holds \(-v\,\Delta t \sin\theta\) in the \(x\) row and \(v\,\Delta t \cos\theta\) in the \(y\) row, which come from differentiating the cosine and sine terms of the motion model.[12] The EKF is only an approximation. Julier and Uhlmann note that linearization "can produce highly unstable filters" when the assumption of local linearity is violated, that deriving the Jacobians can be difficult, and that the filter can underestimate its covariance, "raising the possibility that the filter will diverge."[13]
  • Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF): Presented in 1997 by Simon Julier and Jeffrey Uhlmann, this approach rests on the idea that "it is easier to approximate a Gaussian distribution than it is to approximate an arbitrary nonlinear function or transformation." Rather than calculating Jacobians, it chooses a deterministic set of \(2n+1\) weighted sample points (now called sigma points) around the state mean, where \(n\) is the state dimension. The points are passed through the exact non-linear equations, and the new mean and covariance are recomputed as weighted sums. The authors show that the transformed mean and covariance are correct to second order, with a more accurate mean than the EKF, and no derivatives are needed.[13] The UKF is often more accurate than the EKF, especially when the problem is highly non-linear, but it is not guaranteed to do better every time. Most UKF code today, including FilterPy, uses Van der Merwe's “scaled” sigma points, a refinement of Julier and Uhlmann's original choice.[9][11]

08. ROS 2 state estimation: the robot_localization dual-EKF architecture

In ROS and ROS 2, a widely used package for multi-sensor state estimation is robot_localization, presented by Tom Moore and Daniel Stouch of Charles River Analytics.[14] It provides an EKF node and a UKF node, and both track "a 15-D vector" of 3D position, orientation (roll, pitch, yaw), their velocities, and linear acceleration:[8]

\[\mathbf{x} = [x, y, z, \text{roll}, \text{pitch}, \text{yaw}, \dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{z}, \dot{\text{roll}}, \dot{\text{pitch}}, \dot{\text{yaw}}, \ddot{x}, \ddot{y}, \ddot{z}]^T\]

The package documentation describes how to split estimation between two filter instances. An instance that fuses only continuous data (wheel odometry, visual odometry, IMU) uses the odom frame as its world frame. An instance that also fuses global absolute data such as GPS uses the map frame, and something else (normally the first instance) must provide the odom → base_link transform. Both nodes publish their estimate on odometry/filtered by default, so the two instances are usually remapped to different topic names at launch.[8] The package's GPS guide explains the reason: GPS data is subject to discrete jumps, which makes a position estimate that includes it unfit for navigation modules, so it recommends one instance that fuses only continuous data such as odometry and IMU in the odom frame and a second instance that fuses all sources, including GPS, in the map frame.[15] These frames follow ROS REP 105, in which the pose in the odom frame is continuous but can drift without bound, while the map frame is not continuous and can change in discrete jumps.[16] This dual-EKF arrangement separates smooth motor control from absolute global positioning:

Filter Instance Sensor Inputs Coordinate Frame & Output Behavioral Guarantee
Local EKF Continuous sources only: wheel odometry and IMU gyroscopes and accelerometers. odom → base_link transform; publishes a nav_msgs/Odometry estimate. Continuous and smooth. No GPS jumps, so it suits closed-loop motor controllers and local obstacle avoidance, although it slowly drifts.
Global EKF The same continuous sources plus absolute global references: GPS (converted by navsat_transform_node) or landmark observations. map → odom transform; publishes a second, globally referenced estimate. Globally referenced. Corrects cumulative drift. The documentation warns that the map frame "is subject to discrete jumps, e.g., due to the fusion of GPS data," so those jumps appear in the map → odom transform instead of in the local estimate used for control.[8]

09. Complementary filters for microcontrollers

On low-cost 8-bit microcontrollers without floating-point hardware, the matrix operations of a full EKF can be slow. For orientation estimation (such as measuring the tilt angle of a self-balancing robot from a 6-axis IMU), a common lightweight alternative is the complementary filter.[17]

A complementary filter exploits the complementary frequency characteristics of the IMU: the gyroscope responds quickly but drifts over time, whereas the accelerometer gives a drift-free tilt from gravity, but only on average, because vibration and linear accelerations corrupt each sample. Its tilt reading is therefore trustworthy only at low frequencies.[18] The filter therefore takes the high-frequency part of its estimate from the gyroscope and the low-frequency part from the accelerometer, using fixed weights.[17] The Fusion AHRS library describes its own algorithm in the same terms, as a complementary filter that combines high-pass filtered gyroscope measurements with low-pass filtered measurements from other sensors.[18] The update is:

\[\theta_k = \alpha \times (\theta_{k-1} + \omega \Delta t) + (1 - \alpha) \times \theta_{accel}\]

With \(\alpha = 0.98\) (the value used in the CENG4480 example code[17]), the filter derives 98% of its angular estimate from integrated gyroscope velocity, using the accelerometer's gravitational reading for the remaining 2% to gently cancel accumulated gyro drift on every pass without running matrix algebra. Raising \(\alpha\) trusts the gyroscope more, which gives smoother output but slower drift correction; lowering it corrects drift faster but lets more vibration through.[18] Real systems often use both kinds of filter: PX4's EKF uses a complementary filter to carry its states forward from its delayed fusion time to the current time using buffered IMU data.[7]
