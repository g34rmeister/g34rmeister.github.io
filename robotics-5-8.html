5.8 Intro to Kalman Filters

Gerald Lê

01. Why robots require sensor fusion

In mobile robotics, state estimation is governed by a fundamental physical trade-off between two classes of sensors: proprioceptive motion sensors and exteroceptive environmental sensors.[1]

As demonstrated in Lesson 5.6, wheel odometry is smooth, responsive, and available at high sampling rates (100 Hz to 1000 Hz). However, odometry is an integration process: small mechanical errors, floor irregularities, and wheel slip accumulate continuously into the robot's pose estimate. Without external references, odometric position drift grows without bound over time.

Conversely, exteroceptive sensors (such as ultrasonic distance sensors measuring a known wall, LiDAR scan matchers, or outdoor GPS receivers) do not accumulate integration drift. Their measurements are directly referenced to landmarks in the physical environment. However, exteroceptive sensors suffer from high high-frequency measurement noise, electronic jitter, acoustic multipath, and intermittent signal outages.

Neither sensor modality is sufficient on its own. Sensor fusion is the mathematical process of combining measurements from multiple disparate sensors such that the resulting state estimate possesses lower statistical uncertainty than any single sensor operating in isolation.[2]

The premier algorithmic framework for sensor fusion in robotics is the Kalman filter. Published in 1960 by mathematician Rudolf Emil Kálmán, the filter is an optimal recursive data processing algorithm that maintains a continuous estimate of a dynamic system's state alongside an explicit mathematical measure of its statistical uncertainty.[1]

02. Historical origins: Rudolf Kálmán and Apollo navigation

Prior to 1960, optimal filtering was dominated by Norbert Wiener's frequency-domain filtering theory developed during World War II. While mathematically elegant, Wiener filters operated in the frequency domain, assumed infinite stationary signal histories, and required solving complex integral equations that exceeded the capabilities of early digital computers.

Rudolf E. Kálmán formulated state estimation directly in the time domain using state-space representations and linear matrix equations.[1] In autumn 1960, Kálmán visited Stanley F. Schmidt at NASA Ames Research Center in California. Schmidt's engineering team was tasked with developing midcourse navigation and trajectory estimation algorithms for the upcoming Apollo lunar missions.[3]

The Apollo Guidance Computer (AGC) possessed only 36 kilobytes of read-only core rope memory and 2 kilobytes of magnetic core RAM. Storing complete sensor histories was physically impossible. Schmidt recognized that Kálmán's recursive algebraic formulation, which required storing only the current state estimate and its error covariance matrix, was the ideal computational solution. Schmidt modified Kálmán's linear equations to handle non-linear orbital celestial mechanics, developing what became known as the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF).[3] A square-root formulation of the filter subsequently flew aboard the Apollo spacecraft, guiding humans to the lunar surface and back.

03. The recursive Predict-Update architecture

The Kalman filter operates as a continuous, two-stage recursive loop: the Predict (Time Update) step and the Update (Measurement Update) step.[4]

Consider a linear discrete-time physical system modeled in state space:

\[\mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{k-1} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{w}_k\]

\[\mathbf{z}_k = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{v}_k\]

Here, \(\mathbf{x}_k\) is the state vector at time step \(k\), \(\mathbf{A}\) is the state transition matrix, \(\mathbf{B}\) is the control input matrix, and \(\mathbf{u}_k\) represents control commands (such as wheel velocity). The term \(\mathbf{w}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{Q})\) represents zero-mean Gaussian process noise with covariance matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\). The sensor measurement vector \(\mathbf{z}_k\) is linked to state space via observation matrix \(\mathbf{H}\), corrupted by zero-mean Gaussian measurement noise \(\mathbf{v}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{R})\) with covariance matrix \(\mathbf{R}\).

The two operational stages execute sequentially:

  • Predict Step (Time Update): The filter projects the state estimate forward in time using the mathematical motion model:

    \[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^- = \mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_k\]

    \[\mathbf{P}_k^- = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{k-1}\mathbf{A}^T + \mathbf{Q}\]

    Because real physical motion is inherently imperfect (tires slip, terrain varies), adding the process noise covariance \(\mathbf{Q}\) causes estimation uncertainty \(\mathbf{P}_k^-\) to grow during prediction.
  • Update Step (Measurement Update): When a sensor reading arrives, the filter computes the difference between the physical measurement \(\mathbf{z}_k\) and what the state model expected:

    \[\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_k = \mathbf{z}_k - \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^-\]

    This residual difference \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_k\) is termed the innovation. The filter computes the optimal Kalman Gain \(\mathbf{K}_k\), which balances trust between prediction and measurement:

    \[\mathbf{K}_k = \mathbf{P}_k^-\mathbf{H}^T (\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_k^-\mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R})^{-1}\]

    The state estimate and its error covariance matrix are updated:

    \[\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^- + \mathbf{K}_k \tilde{\mathbf{y}}_k\]

    \[\mathbf{P}_k = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_k \mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}_k^-\]

    Because new observational data was incorporated, the updated error covariance \(\mathbf{P}_k\) shrinks, indicating greater certainty.

04. One-dimensional scalar worked example

To develop concrete numerical intuition, examine a one-dimensional robot driving along a hallway. Its state is a single scalar \(x\), representing position along the hallway in meters. The variance of its position estimate is denoted by scalar \(P\).

In one-dimensional scalar form (\(A = 1, B = 1, H = 1\)), the governing equations simplify to:

Predict:

\[x_{pred} = x + u\]

\[P_{pred} = P + Q\]

Update:

\[K = \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred} + R}\]

\[x = x_{pred} + K \times (z - x_{pred})\]

\[P = (1 - K) \times P_{pred}\]

Suppose the robot begins at estimated position \(x = 2.00\text{ m}\) with variance \(P = 0.04\text{ m}^2\) (standard deviation \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20\text{ m}\)). Its wheel encoders report that the robot drove forward by \(u = 0.50\text{ m}\). We assign a process noise variance of \(Q = 0.01\text{ m}^2\) to account for potential wheel slip during the maneuver.

An ultrasonic range sensor then pings a known corridor wall and reports absolute position \(z = 2.60\text{ m}\). Laboratory testing has shown this sensor has a measurement noise variance of \(R = 0.05\text{ m}^2\).

We execute the filter calculations step by step:

  1. Predict Position: \[x_{pred} = 2.00 + 0.50 = 2.50\text{ m}\]
  2. Predict Variance: \[P_{pred} = 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.05\text{ m}^2\]
  3. Compute Kalman Gain \(K\): \[K = \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred} + R} = \frac{0.05}{0.05 + 0.05} = \frac{0.05}{0.10} = 0.50\]
  4. Compute State Innovation: \[\text{innovation} = z - x_{pred} = 2.60 - 2.50 = 0.10\text{ m}\]
  5. Update Position Estimate: \[x = x_{pred} + K \times (z - x_{pred}) = 2.50 + 0.50 \times (0.10) = 2.55\text{ m}\]
  6. Update Error Variance: \[P = (1 - K) \times P_{pred} = (1 - 0.50) \times 0.05 = 0.025\text{ m}^2\]

Two vital insights emerge from these calculations:

First, because the predicted state uncertainty (\(P_{pred} = 0.05\)) and the sensor measurement noise (\(R = 0.05\)) were identical, the Kalman gain was exactly \(K = 0.50\). The updated position landed precisely halfway between the kinematic prediction (\(2.50\text{ m}\)) and the sensor measurement (\(2.60\text{ m}\)), at \(2.55\text{ m}\).

Second, the resulting variance \(P = 0.025\text{ m}^2\) is strictly smaller than both the prediction variance (\(0.05\)) and the sensor noise (\(0.05\)). By mathematically fusing two uncertain sources of information, the Kalman filter produced an estimate that is substantially more certain than either source alone!

05. Embedded C++ implementation

On an embedded microcontroller, a one-dimensional Kalman filter can be written in a dozen lines of non-blocking C++: 

class KalmanFilter1D {
public:
  float x; // State estimate (position in meters)
  float P; // Estimate error variance
  float Q; // Process noise covariance (motion uncertainty)
  float R; // Measurement noise covariance (sensor noise)

  KalmanFilter1D(float initX, float initP, float processNoiseQ, float sensorNoiseR)
    : x(initX), P(initP), Q(processNoiseQ), R(sensorNoiseR) {}

  // Predict step: call on every odometry update
  void predict(float u) {
    x = x + u;
    P = P + Q;
  }

  // Update step: call when valid sensor measurement arrives
  void update(float z) {
    float K = P / (P + R);
    x = x + K * (z - x);
    P = (1.0f - K) * P;
  }
};

KalmanFilter1D filter(0.0f, 1.0f, 0.01f, 0.05f);

void handleSensors() {
  float deltaDistance = readWheelOdometry();
  filter.predict(deltaDistance);

  float rangeReading = readUltrasonicDistance();
  // Reject sensor dropouts (e.g. out of range or timeout)
  if (rangeReading > 0.02f && rangeReading < 4.00f) {
    filter.update(rangeReading);
  }
}

Notice the crucial architectural pattern in handleSensors(): predict() is called continuously at the high odometry rate (e.g. 50 Hz). The update() routine is called only when a fresh, valid sensor reading is acquired. If the ultrasonic sensor drops an echo or times out, the code simply skips the update step. The filter continues projecting forward using the motion model, and variance \(P\) grows to reflect the lack of external correction.

06. Tuning process noise Q and measurement noise R

In practical engineering, the performance of a Kalman filter depends critically on properly selecting the noise covariances \(Q\) and \(R\):

  • Measurement Noise Covariance \(R\): Can be measured experimentally. Park the robot stationary on the floor, collect several thousand static readings from the range sensor, and compute the mathematical variance of the sample set: \[R = \sigma_z^2 = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (z_i - \bar{z})^2\]
  • Process Noise Covariance \(Q\): Represents physical disturbances not captured in the kinematic model. Because wheel slip, floor roughness, and tire deflection cannot be observed directly, \(Q\) is tuned empirically. Setting \(Q\) too large signals that the motion model is untrustworthy, forcing the filter to track noisy sensor readings aggressively (producing jitter). Setting \(Q\) too small causes the filter to over-trust its kinematic model, responding sluggishly to genuine sensor corrections.

The mathematical behavior of the Kalman gain \(K = \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred} + R}\) demonstrates two fundamental limiting regimes:

  • Small Sensor Noise (\(R \ll P_{pred}\)): When sensor noise \(R\) is small compared to predicted uncertainty \(P_{pred}\), the ratio approaches 1: \[K \to \frac{P_{pred}}{P_{pred}} = 1.0\] The state update becomes \(x \approx x_{pred} + 1.0 \times (z - x_{pred}) = z\). The filter disregards its kinematic prediction and follows the sensor reading directly.
  • Large Sensor Noise (\(R \gg P_{pred}\)): When sensor noise \(R\) is huge compared to predicted uncertainty, the denominator is dominated by \(R\): \[K \to \frac{P_{pred}}{R} \approx 0.0\] The state update becomes \(x \approx x_{pred} + 0.0 \times (z - x_{pred}) = x_{pred}\). The filter ignores the noisy sensor and relies entirely on its motion model.

07. Non-linear state estimation: EKF versus UKF

The standard Kalman filter assumes that system dynamics and observation models are strictly linear. However, mobile robot kinematics are inherently non-linear due to trigonometric rotation equations (such as \(\dot{x} = v \cos\theta\) and \(\dot{y} = v \sin\theta\)).

When a Gaussian probability distribution passes through a non-linear transformation, the output distribution is distorted and ceases to be strictly Gaussian. Two non-linear algorithms address this challenge:

  • Extended Kalman Filter (EKF): Linearizes non-linear functions locally around the current state estimate using first-order Taylor series approximations.[4] This requires computing Jacobian matrices of partial derivatives at each time step: \[\mathbf{F}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \right|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}}, \quad \mathbf{H}_k = \left. \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \right|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^-}\] While computationally efficient, the EKF discards higher-order terms. Under severe non-linearities or rapid angular rotations, the linearized approximation breaks down, leading to filter divergence.
  • Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF): Formulated in 1997 by Jeffrey Uhlmann and Simon Julier, the UKF operates on the premise that "it is easier to approximate a probability distribution than an arbitrary non-linear function."[5] Rather than calculating analytical Jacobians, the UKF selects a deterministic set of \(2n+1\) sample points (called Sigma Points) centered on the state mean. These points are propagated directly through the exact non-linear equations, and the posterior mean and covariance are recomputed via weighted summation. The UKF achieves third-order Taylor series accuracy for Gaussian inputs without computing derivatives.

08. ROS 2 state estimation: the robot_localization dual-EKF architecture

In ROS 2, multi-sensor state estimation is standardized via the robot_localization package, developed by Tom Moore and Daniel Stouch.[6] The package provides continuous 15-dimensional state estimation tracking 3D pose, linear velocity, angular velocity, and linear acceleration:

\[\mathbf{x} = [x, y, z, \text{roll}, \text{pitch}, \text{yaw}, \dot{x}, \dot{y}, \dot{z}, \dot{\text{roll}}, \dot{\text{pitch}}, \dot{\text{yaw}}, \ddot{x}, \ddot{y}, \ddot{z}]^T\]

In professional autonomous ground vehicle deployments, robot_localization implements a Dual-EKF Architecture to resolve the conflict between smooth motor control and absolute global positioning:

Filter Instance Sensor Inputs Coordinate Frame & Output Behavioral Guarantee
Local EKF Wheel odometry (/wheel/odometry), IMU gyroscopes and accelerometers (/imu/data). odom → base_link transform; publishes /odometry/filtered. Continuous and smooth. Zero discrete position jumps. Ideal for high-frequency closed-loop motor controllers and local path obstacle avoidance.
Global EKF Local fused odometry plus absolute global references: GPS (via navsat_transform_node) or visual landmark SLAM. map → odom transform; publishes /odometry/filtered_global. Globally referenced. Eliminates cumulative drift. When GPS satellite signals fix or drop, discrete coordinate adjustments are absorbed into the map → odom transform without destabilizing local wheel velocity control loops.

09. Complementary filters for microcontrollers

On low-cost 8-bit microcontrollers lacking floating-point hardware units, executing matrix inversions for an EKF can strain CPU limits. For orientation estimation (such as measuring the tilt angle of a self-balancing robot from a 6-axis IMU), engineers frequently deploy a complementary filter.[4]

A complementary filter exploits the complementary frequency characteristics of the IMU: the gyroscope is highly accurate over short timeframes (high frequency) but drifts over time, whereas the accelerometer reliably senses the gravity vector over long periods (low frequency) but is corrupted by vibration in the short term. The filter blends both sources using fixed weighting coefficients:

\[\theta_k = \alpha \times (\theta_{k-1} + \omega \Delta t) + (1 - \alpha) \times \theta_{accel}\]

With \(\alpha = 0.98\), the filter derives 98% of its angular estimate from integrated gyroscope velocity, using the accelerometer's gravitational reading for the remaining 2% to gently cancel accumulated gyro drift on every pass without running matrix algebra.
