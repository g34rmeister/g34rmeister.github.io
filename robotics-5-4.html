5.4 Making a Robot Cell Safe

Gerald Lê

01. From principles to a working safety plan

Lesson 5.1 introduced the standards behind industrial robot safety (ISO 10218:2025, ISO/TS 15066 and the EU Machinery Regulation) and the accident data that explain why they exist. This lesson is the practical sequel. It follows the steps an integrator takes to turn an unguarded robot on a shop floor into a cell that people can run, teach, clear and repair without being hurt.

In the United States the governing robot standard is now ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025, announced in September 2025. It is the U.S. adoption of ISO 10218-1:2025 for robots and ISO 10218-2:2025 for robot applications and cells, and it is a revision of ANSI/RIA R15.06-2012.[1][2] For integrators, one important change is a shift away from a blanket requirement of Performance Level d (PLd) toward performance levels chosen by risk assessment. The 2025 standard also asks for a more precise description of each safety function, for validation, and for a justification whenever a design deviates from the standard.[3] SICK recommends that integrators and end users coordinate on the risk assessment early in a project.[3]

02. The ISO 12100 three-step method

ISO 12100 is the general machinery standard for risk assessment and risk reduction, and robot standards build on it. Its core is a three-step method applied in a fixed order:[4][5]

  • Step 1, inherently safe design: Remove the hazard, or lower its risk, through design choices. It is the only step that can remove a hazard, and Keyence calls it the most effective way to reduce risk.[4][5] For a robot cell this might mean a layout that keeps the loading point outside the robot's reach.
  • Step 2, safeguarding and complementary protective measures: Used when Step 1 cannot remove the hazard or reduce the risk enough. This is where fences, interlocked doors, light curtains and emergency stops come in.[4][5]
  • Step 3, information for use: Warnings, signs, manuals and training that tell users about the risks that remain after Steps 1 and 2.[4][5]

What does ISO 12100 say about the order of the three steps? The steps are applied in order, starting with inherently safe design, and information for use must not replace the other risk-reduction measures.[4][5]

Practitioners add two habits. First, after applying a measure, run hazard identification again, because measures can create new hazards. Second, record each Step 1 option you considered and rejected, together with the reason.[6] The incident in Section 10 shows why the first habit matters: a pole installed as a safeguard became the pinch point that killed a worker.

03. Task-based risk assessment with S, E and A

The three-step method tells you how to reduce risk, but first you have to find and rank the hazards. In North America, the RIA technical report TR R15.306 (first published in 2014 and updated in 2015 and 2016) supplements R15.06-2012 with a task-based method.[7][8][9] It lists every task that people perform at the cell and the hazards of each task.

Each task and hazard pair is scored on three factors, which combine into an initial risk level:[7][8]

  • Severity (S): S3 serious, S2 moderate, S1 minor.
  • Exposure (E): E2 high, E1 low, E0 prevented.
  • Avoidance (A): A3 not possible, A2 not likely, A1 likely.

The older R15.06-1999 used only two levels per factor. TR R15.306 moved to three, and the "prevented" exposure level (E0) was reportedly added after some users skipped steps.[9] The report also links the factors to the hierarchy of controls. Elimination drops all three factors to their lowest ratings, while substitution lowers only severity. According to the same article, the 2014 edition said that exposure and avoidance did not affect severity, and the 2016 edition changed that.[7]

The workflow is: initial risk, then selection of risk-reduction measures, then residual risk, which is the risk that remains after the measures are applied. One author argues that once the initial risk and the chosen measures are fixed, the residual risk is effectively determined.[8] If the residual risk is not acceptable, you add or change measures.

The real TR R15.306 matrix that maps S, E and A to a risk level is in the purchased report. For classroom work, this lesson uses the simplified scale below. It is illustrative only: it is not the TR R15.306 table, and it must not be used to sign off a real cell.

Illustrative teaching scale (not from TR R15.306) Rule
Score Add the numbers: S (1 to 3) + E (0 to 2) + A (1 to 3).
Negligible Any pair rated E0, because exposure is prevented.
Low Score of 4 or less.
Medium Score of 5 or 6. Reduce the risk further if you reasonably can.
High Score of 7 or 8. Not acceptable: apply Step 1 or Step 2 measures and score again.

Why insist on a task-based list? Because the dangerous moments are rarely normal production. As lesson 5.1 showed, a NIOSH study of 41 robot-related deaths in the United States from 1992 to 2017 found that 78% involved a robot striking a worker, often during maintenance.[10] An assessment that covers only automatic running misses jam clearing, teaching, tool changes, cleaning and repair.

04. Fences and interlocked doors

Once the hazards are ranked, the integrator chooses safeguards for every point where a person could reach the robot. A useful question for each opening is: does anyone need to go through here, and how often? If nobody needs access, use a fixed fence. If access is needed occasionally, use an interlocked door. If access is frequent, as at a loading station, a presence-sensing device such as a light curtain is often the better choice (Section 05).

Fences. The height of a fence and its distance from the hazard are set by ISO 13857, whose current edition (2019) was reconfirmed in 2025. It covers people aged 14 and older; the 5th-percentile stature of a 14-year-old is about 1400 mm.[11] The fence maker Troax advises that protective structures lower than 1400 mm should not be used without additional safety measures, and that reach-over distances come from ISO 13857 Table 2, which relates the hazard height, the fence height and the horizontal distance between them.[12] Openings in the fence must be sized under the same standard, so that fingers, arms or a whole body cannot reach through.

Interlocked doors. A door in the fence is fitted with an interlocking device under ISO 14119, so that opening the door stops the robot. ISO 14119 classifies interlocks by type. A Type 4 interlock uses a coded actuator such as an RFID tag or a coded magnet, while a switch with an uncoded actuator, such as an uncoded magnet, is Type 3. Coding levels are low, medium and high, and high coding means more than 1,000 code variations. ISO 14119 sets additional measures against defeat that depend on the device type.[13][14]

Why does coding matter? The common way to defeat an interlock is with a spare actuator held in the switch while the guard is open.[15] ISO 14119 asks designers to minimize the motivation to defeat a guard: a door that costs an operator several minutes on every jam invites a workaround.[16]

Robots do not stop instantly. Guard locking holds the door shut until a time delay covering the worst-case run-down has passed, or until a standstill monitor releases it. Note also that an interlock is not energy isolation, so maintenance still needs lockout/tagout (Section 09).[16]

05. Light curtains and the safety distance S = K × T + C

A light curtain is a row of infrared beams. When a hand or body breaks the beams, it sends a stop signal. It lets an operator reach in without opening a door, but it only works if it is mounted far enough from the hazard that the robot has stopped before the hand arrives. ISO 13855 gives that minimum distance as:[17][18] 

S = K × T + C

S = minimum safety distance from the detection zone to the hazard (mm)
K = approach speed of the body (mm/s)
T = total stopping time: sensor response time + machine stopping time (s)
C = intrusion allowance, how far a body part can pass before detection (mm)

For a light curtain with a resolution d of 40 mm or less, K = 2000 mm/s and C = 8 × (d − 14) mm, with C never below 0. This version is valid for S up to 500 mm. If S calculated with K = 2000 comes out above 500 mm, you recalculate with K = 1600 mm/s, but S must stay at least 500 mm.[17][18]

Worked example 1 (Keyence). A light curtain has a response time of 0.0157 s and the machine's stopping time is 0.1 s, so T = 0.1157 s. With C = 88 mm, S = 2000 × 0.1157 + 88 = 319.4 mm. That is under 500 mm, so no recalculation is needed.[17]

Worked example 2 (this lesson's own arithmetic, using the rules above). A 14 mm curtain gives C = 8 × (14 − 14) = 0. A large robot with a total stopping time of T = 0.3 s gives S = 2000 × 0.3 = 600 mm. That is over 500 mm, so recalculate with K = 1600: S = 1600 × 0.3 = 480 mm. Because S may not drop below 500 mm, the curtain goes at 500 mm.

The same rules fit in a few lines of Python. This is an illustrative study aid for the fine-resolution case only, so check its results against the standard and the device manual before using them on a real cell: 

def safety_distance_mm(T_s, d_mm):
    """ISO 13855 safety distance for a light curtain with d <= 40 mm (illustrative)."""
    C = max(0.0, 8 * (d_mm - 14))
    S = 2000 * T_s + C
    if S > 500:
        S = max(500.0, 1600 * T_s + C)
    return S

print(safety_distance_mm(0.1157, 25))  # 319.4  (worked example 1, C = 88 mm)
print(safety_distance_mm(0.3, 14))     # 500.0  (worked example 2)

For coarser curtains (resolutions from 40 to 70 mm), Datalogic gives K = 1600 mm/s and a reach-over allowance of C = 850 mm, with the highest beam at 900 mm and the lowest beam at 300 mm.[18] ReeR gives the same K and C for resolutions of 50 to 90 mm.[19] These rules and examples follow the long-used 2010 edition of ISO 13855, as presented in vendor guides. The current edition, ISO 13855:2024, which replaced the 2010 edition, restates the formula as S = (K × T) + DDS + Z, where DDS takes the place of C and Z is an application-dependent supplement, and it adds new cases such as mobile applications. Check the current text before you position a real safeguard.[20][21]

In the ISO 13855 formula S = K × T + C, what happens to the safety distance if the robot's stopping time T increases? S increases, so the light curtain must be mounted farther from the hazard. This links the guard layout directly to the robot's measured stopping performance.

06. Emergency stops and teach mode

Every cell needs emergency stop buttons, but they play a specific role. The emergency stop standard ISO 13850 says an emergency stop "shall not be applied as a substitute for safeguarding measures."[22][23] It is a complementary protective measure under Step 2 of ISO 12100, a last line of defense for when something has already gone wrong.

Stops are grouped into categories:[24][25]

  • Stop category 0: An uncontrolled stop that removes power from the actuators immediately.
  • Stop category 1: A controlled stop, with power kept until the machine has stopped and then removed.
  • Stop category 2: A controlled stop that keeps power on, which is usually not suitable for an emergency stop.

Which category to use is decided by the risk assessment.[24][25]

Teach mode is a special case, because a programmer must stand near a powered robot to jog it and record points. Two measures protect them. The first is reduced speed. In the Universal Robots manual, the speed of the tool center point and of the elbow both start limited to 250 mm/s in teach or program mode; the speed slider can raise the limit, but activating the enabling device resets it to 250 mm/s.[26] The second is a three-position enabling device on the pendant. On UR15, UR20 and UR30 robots, manual-mode motion requires holding the enabling button in its center position. Releasing it, or squeezing it fully in a panic grip, stops the robot immediately; UR classes this as a category 2 stop. UR says this three-position-enabling pendant is mandatory for those models under ISO 10218-1:2025.[27] The UR manual also recommends pairing the enabling device with a mode switch wired as a safety input.[26]

07. Functional safety in brief: Performance Levels and categories

A guard is only as good as the circuit that reads it. If a door switch is wired through a single relay that can weld shut, opening the door may not stop the robot. ISO 13849-1 deals with this by treating each safety function, such as "door opened, robot stops", as something that must reach a required Performance Level (PLr).

The scale runs from PL a, the lowest, to PL e, the highest: the higher the risk, the higher the level needed.[28] PLr is found from a risk graph with three parameters:[28][29]

  • Severity: S1 slight (reversible) injury, or S2 serious (irreversible) injury or death.
  • Frequency or duration of exposure: F2 means exposure more often than once every 15 minutes. F1 means not more than once every 15 minutes, with accumulated exposure of no more than 1/20 of the operating time.
  • Possibility of avoidance: P1 means avoidance is possible under specific conditions; P2 means it is scarcely possible.

A combination of S2, F2 and P2 gives PLr e, the highest level.[6] Consider a door on a cell that operators enter several times an hour (F2), where a fast robot (P2) could cause serious injury (S2): the door's safety function would need PL e. The speed at which the hazardous situation arises is one of the factors for P, so measures such as reduced speed may justify a lower avoidance rating, and therefore a lower PLr.[30] S, F and P choices are judgment calls, so record the reasoning for each one.

The designer then has to show that the safety function as built achieves at least the PLr. The architecture Category (B, 1, 2, 3 or 4) is part of that proof, together with the reliability of the components (MTTFD) and the diagnostic coverage (DC). Categories are not inputs to the risk graph. The 2015 revision of the standard also lets PLr drop by one level if a low probability of occurrence is proven.[29][31] Note that ISO 13849-1:2023 is now the current edition, and it reworks the Annex A guidance on finding PLr, so check which edition your project has to follow.[32][30] In practice, door switches, light curtains and emergency stops are wired into safety-rated inputs on the robot controller or a safety PLC, often with two channels, so that a single fault does not hide a stop demand.

08. Safety functions inside the robot controller

Modern robot controllers include certified safety software that limits where and how fast the robot can move. By shrinking the space the robot can enter, they can bring the fence closer.

  • FANUC Dual Check Safety (DCS): Joint and Cartesian Position Check define three-dimensional operating zones. Joint and Cartesian Speed Check limit speed and are used to slow the robot when an operator enters the cell.[33][34][35]
  • ABB SafeMove: Programmable safe zones, safe speed limits and safe standstill monitoring. ABB's SafeMove application manual states that SafeMove, together with the safety module and the robot controller, complies with PL d under ISO 13849-1, equivalent to Category 3. The PL e certification mentioned in a 2017 SafeMove2 overview belongs to a safety digital input module, not to the whole safety function, so check the documentation for the rating of each function you use.[36][37][38]
  • Universal Robots safety configuration: Joint position and speed limits, safety planes, tool orientation limits, and limits on tool center point speed and force.[39]

ISO 10218-2:2025 makes functional safety requirements "more explicit rather than implied,"[40][41] and adds robot classifications with matching functional safety requirements and test methods.[40] The same revision integrated the collaborative requirements from ISO/TS 15066 and merged guidance on end-effectors and manual load stations from the former TR (Technical Report) 20218-1 and -2.[40][42] The four collaborative modes are described in lesson 5.1. Here the practical point is that they are built from the controller functions above: FANUC's speed check slowing the robot when a person enters is a speed-and-separation use,[34] and UR's speed and force limits are the basis of power and force limiting.[39]

A "cobot" is not automatically safe: its gripper, the part it carries and any clamping point belong in the risk assessment too.

09. Lockout/tagout: when the robot must have no energy at all

Teaching needs a powered robot, so it relies on reduced speed and the enabling device. Mechanical repair does not need power, so it relies on lockout/tagout (LOTO). In the United States, OSHA's rule 29 CFR 1910.147 applies to servicing and maintenance where unexpected energization, start-up or release of stored energy could injure someone, and its exception for minor servicing is narrow.[43] A3 notes that discussions of robot maintenance quickly turn to LOTO, which removes the hazardous energy source before work begins.[44]

Paragraph (c) of the rule requires documented energy-control procedures, training, and periodic inspections at least once a year. The most-cited provision is (c)(4), documented procedures. Paragraph (d) sets the order in which the procedure is applied, and citations often involve steps that were missed or done out of order.[45]

Is closing an interlocked door, or pressing the emergency stop, enough before a technician climbs into the cell to change a gripper? No. An interlock or emergency stop is not energy isolation, so maintenance still needs lockout/tagout.[16]

The checklist below shows the kind of points a robot-cell LOTO procedure covers. It is illustrative only, based on general practice rather than on the text of 1910.147 or a robot maker's manual. A real procedure must be written for the specific cell and follow your site's energy-control program.

  1. Tell the operators and anyone affected that the cell is going down for service.
  2. Stop the robot and the cell with the normal stop sequence.
  3. Isolate every energy source: the robot controller's main disconnect, the conveyors and feeders that share the cell, and the pneumatic supply through its lockable dump valve.
  4. Each worker applies a personal lock and tag to each isolation point.
  5. Release or block stored energy: bleed the air lines, lower or support any gravity-loaded axis or load on the end-of-arm tool.
  6. Verify zero energy before entry, for example by trying the start controls and checking pressure gauges.

A Nebraska case shows why every person counts. A carousel operator was crushed by a robotic platform that had not been locked out; the operator had stepped onto a conveyor to watch a technician's repair. The recommendations were to lock out and tag out before maintenance, and to keep uninvolved people out of the maintenance area.[46]

10. Case study: NIOSH FACE 8420

On 21 July 1984, a 34-year-old die-cast operator with 15 years' experience was pinned between the right rear of a hydraulic robot and a safety pole, and went into cardiopulmonary arrest. The robot took castings from a die-cast machine, dipped them in a quench tank and loaded a trim press. NIOSH investigated the death in its Fatality Assessment and Control Evaluation (FACE) report 8420.[47][48]

The cell was safeguarded by a safety rail with an interlocked gate on two sides of the robot's work envelope. However, openings were present, and the operator probably got in by climbing over, under or around the rail. The pole, intended as a safeguard, created a pinch point. NIOSH's recommendations covered ergonomic and safety-system design, training and supervision, and NIOSH also named the victim's behavior as a major contributing factor.[47][48]

Seen through the tools in this lesson, each layer had a gap:

  • Perimeter: Openings that a person can walk through fail the purpose of an ISO 13857 guard. An interlocked gate protects nobody if people can walk around it.
  • Presence sensing: A presence-sensing device across the openings, or a complete perimeter, would have detected the entry and stopped the robot.
  • Re-assessment: The pole shows that a "safeguard" can add a hazard, which is why each measure must be checked for new hazards after it is added (Section 02).
  • Behavior: NIOSH pointed to the worker's actions, but design shapes behavior. ISO 14119's principle of minimizing the motivation to defeat guards (Section 04) applies equally to gaps that make a shortcut easy.
