5.4 PID Control

Gerald Lê

01. The challenge of closed-loop motion control

In real-world robotic systems, commanding actuators is never as simple as setting a fixed PWM duty cycle. If a mobile robot is commanded to drive forward at a constant 60% duty cycle, its speed will drop when it transitions from smooth concrete to carpet, veer off-course if one wheel motor has slightly higher internal friction, and accelerate uncontrollably down an incline. Similarly, if an articulated robotic arm attempts to hold its joints at fixed motor voltages, the downward pull of gravity will cause the arm to sag toward the floor. Even the battery voltage sags under load, so the same command gives a different result as the robot works harder.[1] Feedback exists to cope with exactly these disturbances.[2]

Rather than commanding raw actuator power, roboticists command a target setpoint (such as maintaining a velocity of 0.50 m/s or rotating an elbow joint to exactly 45.0 degrees). To achieve and maintain this target despite changing loads and disturbances, the robot must measure its actual state using feedback sensors (such as quadrature encoders), compute the tracking error, and automatically adjust its actuator commands. The most widely used algorithm for this job is the Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controller: Åström and Murray state that more than 95% of all industrial control problems are solved by PID control, many of them with the derivative term left out (PI control).[2]

02. Historical origins: Minorsky and the USS New Mexico

The first mathematical treatment of what we now call three-term (PID) control was published by the Russian-born engineer Nicolas Minorsky, who later worked in the United States. His 1922 paper, “Directional Stability of Automatically Steered Bodies,” appeared in the Journal of the American Society of Naval Engineers and analysed the automatic steering of ships.[3][4]

Minorsky started from the way a skilled helmsman keeps a ship on course: the helmsman does not only react to how far the ship is off course, but times the rudder to meet and oppose the ship's swing.[4] Read in today's terms, that behaviour maps onto the three PID actions:

  • Present error: When the ship is off course, turn the rudder in proportion to the heading error.
  • Past error: If a steady crosswind or current keeps pushing the ship to one side, hold a small extra rudder offset that builds up until the persistent drift is gone.
  • Future trend: If the ship is swinging quickly back toward the heading, apply opposite rudder early so it does not overshoot.

Minorsky's electrical steering controller (built from potentiometers, a differential gear and a motor-generator set) was tested aboard the battleship USS New Mexico in 1923. The trials kept the ship close to its course, but the equipment was removed afterwards and the US Navy set automatic steering aside until the 1930s, largely because the ship's operating crews opposed it.[4]

03. The three control actions: present, past, and future

At any instant in time \(t\), the tracking error \(e(t)\) is defined as the difference between the desired target setpoint \(r(t)\) and the measured process variable \(y(t)\):[2]

\[e(t) = r(t) - y(t)\]

A PID controller computes its corrective output \(u(t)\) by synthesizing three distinct mathematical actions:[2]

  • Proportional Term (Present Error): \(P(t) = K_p \cdot e(t)\). The proportional action generates a restoring force directly proportional to the magnitude of the current error: “the farther the system is from the target, the harder the motor pushes.” Increasing the proportional gain \(K_p\) accelerates system response and shortens rise time. However, pure proportional control inherently leaves a steady-state error (droop/offset). As the robot approaches the setpoint, the error shrinks toward zero, causing the proportional push to become too weak to overcome opposing physical forces such as gearbox friction or gravity.[2][5]
  • Integral Term (Past Error Accumulation): \(I(t) = K_i \int_0^t e(\tau) \, d\tau\). The integral action accumulates tracking errors over time. Even a microscopic residual steady-state error that lingers over time causes the mathematical integral to steadily grow, progressively increasing the control output until the residual error is driven strictly to zero. However, because integration introduces phase lag into the feedback loop, excessive integral gain \(K_i\) causes severe overshoot and resonant ringing.[2][5]
  • Derivative Term (Future Error Trend): \(D(t) = K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt}\). The derivative action measures the instantaneous rate of change of error. If the robot is rapidly closing the gap toward the target, the derivative of error is strongly negative, generating an immediate opposing braking force that damps kinetic momentum and prevents overshooting. While derivative action stabilizes dynamic oscillations, it inherently acts as a high-pass filter, amplifying high-frequency electrical sensor noise.[2]

The “past, present and future” reading is a helpful picture, but modern control texts often describe the terms differently: P drives the position error to zero, D drives the velocity error to zero, and I drives the accumulated error to zero.[5]

04. Block diagram and continuous mathematical formulation

The standard continuous-time ideal PID equation is formulated as:[2]

\[u(t) = K_p \, e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau) \, d\tau + K_d \, \frac{de(t)}{dt}\]

In industrial process automation, the controller is frequently expressed in standard form:[6][7]

\[u(t) = K_p \left( e(t) + \frac{1}{T_i} \int_0^t e(\tau) \, d\tau + T_d \, \frac{de(t)}{dt} \right)\]

where \(T_i = K_p / K_i\) is the integral reset time constant (in seconds) and \(T_d = K_d / K_p\) is the derivative time constant (in seconds).[6][7]

05. Discrete-time implementation in robot software

Digital microcontrollers execute software cyclically at discrete sampling intervals \(\Delta t\) (for example, every 10 milliseconds, or 100 Hz). In discrete time, the continuous integral becomes a numerical running summation, and the continuous derivative becomes a backward difference:[2][6] 

float Kp = 1.2, Ki = 0.5, Kd = 0.1;
float lastError = 0.0;
float integral = 0.0;

float updatePID(float setpoint, float measured, float dt) {
    float error = setpoint - measured;
    
    // Discrete numerical integration (backward Euler: right-rectangle sum using the current error)
    integral += error * dt;
    
    // Discrete numerical differentiation (backward difference)
    float derivative = (error - lastError) / dt;
    lastError = error;
    
    // Sum three terms to yield command output
    float output = (Kp * error) + (Ki * integral) + (Kd * derivative);
    return output;
}

In real-time software, the PID calculation works best when it runs at a regular interval. If the loop timing jitters, the integral sum and the derivative slope are computed with the wrong \(\Delta t\), so the effective gains change from one pass to the next. Robot loops are therefore usually driven by a hardware timer interrupt, or they measure the elapsed time on every pass.[6][8] WPILib's PID controller, for example, assumes it is called at its configured period (20 ms by default) and warns that calling it irregularly gives unintended loop behaviour.[9] Libraries such as Beauregard's Arduino PID library fold the sample time into the gains once (\(K_i \Delta t\) and \(K_d / \Delta t\)), which is only valid if the loop really runs at that fixed interval.[10]

06. Classical tuning methods: Ziegler–Nichols and Cohen–Coon

Choosing the gains \(K_p\), \(K_i\), and \(K_d\) determines whether an actuator tracks smoothly or violently oscillates. In 1942, John G. Ziegler and Nathaniel B. Nichols, engineers at Taylor Instrument Companies, published their empirical tuning rules in Transactions of the ASME.[11]

In the Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop tuning method, how is the ultimate gain \(K_u\) determined? The answer: by setting \(K_i\) and \(K_d\) to zero and increasing \(K_p\) until the output sustains continuous, steady oscillations.[11]

The procedure follows four steps:[11]

  1. Set integral gain \(K_i = 0\) and derivative gain \(K_d = 0\).
  2. Gradually raise proportional gain \(K_p\) while introducing small disturbance steps until the system reaches marginal stability: the output oscillates continuously at constant amplitude without growing or decaying.
  3. Record this critical proportional value as the ultimate gain \(K_u\).
  4. Measure the period between oscillation peaks in seconds as the ultimate period \(P_u\).

Ziegler and Nichols then gave rule-of-thumb settings in terms of \(K_u\) and \(P_u\). Their paper used the instrument terms of the day (“sensitivity”, “reset rate” and “pre-act time”); the table below converts them to the parallel gains used in this lesson, using \(K_i = K_p / T_i\) and \(K_d = K_p T_d\):[11]

Controller Type Proportional Gain (\(K_p\)) Integral Gain (\(K_i\)) Derivative Gain (\(K_d\))
P Controller \(0.50 \cdot K_u\) N/A N/A
PI Controller \(0.45 \cdot K_u\) \(0.54 \cdot \frac{K_u}{P_u}\) N/A
Full PID Controller \(0.60 \cdot K_u\) \(1.20 \cdot \frac{K_u}{P_u}\) \(0.075 \cdot K_u \cdot P_u\)

In 1953, G. H. Cohen and G. A. Coon published the Cohen–Coon open-loop method.[12] Instead of pushing the closed loop to the edge of oscillation, you put the controller in manual, step its output by a few percent, and read three numbers off the process response: the process gain \(K\), the dead time \(L\) before the response starts, and the time constant \(\tau\) (the time to reach 63% of the total change). These describe the plant as a First-Order Plus Dead Time (FOPDT) model, and the tuning rules turn them into gains. The method suits self-regulating processes such as flow, temperature, pressure and speed, but not integrating ones such as tank level, and the original rules give a fast but quite oscillatory response, so practitioners often reduce the gain they recommend.[13]

Servo-drive makers often give their own practical tuning recipes. ODrive's is: set the integrator to zero, raise the velocity gain about 30% at a time until the motor vibrates, back it off to 50% of that value, then tune the position gain until overshoot just disappears.[14]

08. Critical practical modifications: anti-windup, derivative kick, and noise filtering

Textbook PID equations assume ideal components. Physical actuators have real limits (saturation, finite update rates and electrical noise) that call for a few standard modifications:[2][6]

  • Integral Windup: A robot is blocked by an immovable barrier, its integral term keeps growing, and it lurches violently when released. What is this called? Integral windup. When an actuator reaches physical saturation (such as maximum 100% PWM), the error cannot be reduced immediately. The integral term continues accumulating past physical limits. When the obstacle is removed, the controller remains saturated until the accumulated integral slowly unwinds, causing massive overshoot. Robot software should include an anti-windup measure, such as integrator clamping (bounding the integral term) or conditional integration (halting integration whenever the commanded output saturates).[2][6][8] WPILib's PID controller clamps the integrator by default,[9] and its tuning guide adds another option: reset the integrator whenever the error is large (an “I-zone”).[1] ROS 2's ros2_control PID offers a third anti-windup option, back-calculation, which bleeds the integrator off in proportion to how far the output exceeds its limit.[15] Many robotics practitioners avoid integral gain altogether and remove steady-state error with feedforward instead.[9]
  • Derivative Kick: When an operator commands an instantaneous step change in target setpoint \(r(t)\), the error jumps in a single sample, so the derivative of error \(\frac{d(r - y)}{dt}\) produces a huge one-sample spike in the output. This “derivative kick” jolts motor drives and gearboxes. How do industrial servo controllers prevent derivative kick? By computing the derivative on the process measurement rather than on the error. While the setpoint is constant, the derivative of the error is just the negative derivative of the measurement, so nothing else changes:[8]

    \[D[k] = -K_d \, \frac{y[k] - y[k-1]}{\Delta t}\]

    Because a setpoint change does not make the measurement \(y(t)\) jump, the setpoint step no longer produces a kick.[8] Common PID libraries do this as standard: Beauregard's Arduino PID library always takes the derivative on the measurement, and the Python simple-pid library does so by default.[10][16]
  • High-Frequency Sensor Noise: Differentiating discrete sensor data (such as encoder tick quantization) amplifies high-frequency noise. Practical controllers therefore filter the derivative term, for example with a first-order low-pass filter:[2][6]

    \[D_{filtered}[k] = \alpha \cdot D_{filtered}[k-1] + (1 - \alpha) \cdot D[k]\]

    where filter coefficient \(\alpha\) attenuates high-frequency jitter while preserving true mechanical motion.

09. How industrial servo drives nest their control loops

Servo drives for robot joints and machine axes rarely run a single PID loop from joint position straight to motor voltage. Instead they use a cascade of nested loops, each feeding the setpoint of the faster loop inside it. One drive maker, Synapticon, documents its scheme as follows:[17]

  • 1. Inner Torque/Current Loop (16 kHz): The fastest loop. It turns the torque command into PWM signals for the motor phases using Field-Oriented Control (FOC).[17]
  • 2. Middle Velocity Loop (4 kHz): Turns the speed error into a torque command for the current loop, with anti-windup limiting its integrator.[17]
  • 3. Outer Position Loop (4 kHz): Turns the position error into a target speed for the velocity loop. In the recommended “PI-P” setting the position loop is PI and the velocity loop is P only.[17]

Exact loop rates differ between products, so treat these numbers as one real example rather than a universal rule. The ODrive motor controller, for instance, also cascades position, velocity and current loops, but uses a P position loop and a PI velocity loop, the reverse of Synapticon's recommended setting.[14] The PX4 drone autopilot uses the same cascaded idea, with a mix of P and PID controllers.[18] Robot arm controllers can also add model-based feed-forward computed from the rigid-body equations of motion, a technique covered in robotics textbooks under motion control:[19]

\[\tau_{cmd} = \tau_{PID} + M(q)\ddot{q}_{ref} + C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}_{ref} + g(q)\]

Rather than waiting for a feedback error to develop, the model predicts the joint torque needed to accelerate the arm's mass matrix \(M(q)\), cancel Coriolis and centrifugal effects \(C(q,\dot{q})\), and hold the arm against gravity \(g(q)\). The better the model, the less work is left for the feedback PID loop, which then mainly corrects modelling errors and unmodelled friction.[19]

Try it
  1. In the PID simulator above, set Ki and Kd to zero. Increase Kp until the step response begins sustained oscillations, noting the ultimate gain Ku. Calculate the Ziegler–Nichols PID gains using the formulas in section 06 and test them in the simulator.
  2. Simulate a heavy external load disturbance by applying a continuous offset in the simulator. Compare how a pure P controller settles with permanent steady-state droop versus how adding Ki eliminates the offset.
  3. In your robot firmware, implement integrator clamping by restricting the integral term between -100 and +100 PWM units. Test with a physically stalled motor and observe how clamping prevents the robot from lurching when released.
