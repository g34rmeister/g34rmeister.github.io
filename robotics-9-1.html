      9.1 Preventive Maintenance

      Gerald Lê
      Chapter 9, lesson 1 of 6 ← Previous: Chapter 9 overview Next: 9.2 A Troubleshooting Method →

      01. Maintenance strategies in automated manufacturing

      In Lesson 1.7 The Industrial Robot Cell, we examined how robots coordinate within production cells alongside conveyors, fixtures, and supervisory controllers. When the robot stops, the whole cell stops, so plants plan how they look after their machines.

      The simplest approach is run-to-failure servicing: run equipment until it fails. Reactive maintenance is maintenance done after equipment has failed or stopped.[1] Nothing is done until something breaks.

      It is still common. On average, 45.7 percent of manufacturing machinery maintenance is reactive maintenance.[1]

      Manufacturing establishments relying most heavily on reactive maintenance experience 3.3 times more downtime than those relying on it least.[1]

      Preventive maintenance is maintenance that is scheduled, timed, or based on an operating cycle.[1] The work is done on schedule whether or not the machine shows a problem.

      Predictive maintenance is initiated based on predictions of failure made using observed data such as temperature, noise, and vibration.[1] Instead of a calendar, the trigger is a measurement that suggests a part is wearing out.

      Among plants relying primarily on planned maintenance, leading adopters of predictive maintenance achieved fifteen percent less downtime and an eighty-seven percent lower defect rate.[1]

      02. Balancing schedules, sensor data, and maintenance records

      Choosing the interval is a trade-off. Preventive maintenance can cause unnecessary cost and downtime if performed too frequently, or failures if not performed frequently enough.[2]

      Manufacturers generate maintenance records documenting equipment faults, failures, root causes, and repairs to speed up troubleshooting and improve failure prediction.[2] A maintenance log for each robot (what was seen, what was done, what the cause turned out to be) lets the next technician check whether the same symptom has happened before.

      Adding sensors is not enough on its own. Installed sensors offer limited predictive insight if they do not capture data relevant to the specific failure mode.[2]

      Failure prediction success remains low unless failure data is systematically collected and labeled alongside sensor data.[2] Keep the maintenance log and the sensor data together, so that someone can see which readings came before which failures.

      03. Routine service intervals and safety system testing

      Universal Robots recommends a service check at one of its regional service centers “at least bi-annually”.[3] The page does not say whether that means twice a year or every two years, so ask your distributor.

      Spare robots in storage need attention too. A robot must not be kept in storage for more than 6 months without performing a visual inspection.[3]

      Universal Robots recommends testing robot safety functions monthly to verify correct operation.[3] Lesson 5.4 Making a Robot Cell Safe covers what those safety functions are.

      04. Robot arm inspection, cleaning, and joint lubrication

      Cleaning must follow the manufacturer's instructions. Compressed air must never be used to clean the robot arm because it can damage internal components.[3]

      Instead, dust, dirt and oil can be wiped off with a cloth and water, isopropyl alcohol, 10% ethanol alcohol or 10% naphtha. Bleach must not be used, even diluted.[3]

      While cleaning, look at the joints. The gearboxes inside them (see 1.6 Robot Anatomy and Coordinate Frames) are greased. Small amounts of grease occasionally visible at a robot joint do not affect the function or durability of the joint.[4]

      05. Control cabinet maintenance and electrostatic discharge protection

      The control box needs cleaning too. Accumulated dirt or dust inside the control box must be cleaned using an electrostatic discharge vacuum cleaner.[3]

      An ESD vacuum is built to avoid causing electrostatic discharge (ESD), a static spark that can damage electronics.

      The controller log can also point to maintenance needs. An incorrect date and clock in the controller log indicates that the CMOS battery is low.[3] A wrong clock also makes the log harder to use, because the time stamps no longer match what happened in the plant.

      Technicians must wear an intact ESD wristband and prepare an ESD bag before replacing electrostatic-sensitive parts.[4]

      06. A worked checklist: UR's inspection plan

      Universal Robots publishes an inspection plan for its cobots. If a part is not in an acceptable state, UR says to fix it immediately.[3]

      Visual inspection of the arm:[3]

      1. Move the arm to its ZERO position if possible, turn it off and disconnect the power cable from the control box.
      2. Inspect the cable between the control box and the arm for damage.
      3. Check that the base mounting bolts and the tool flange bolts are properly tightened.
      4. Inspect the flat rings (the seals between joints) for wear and damage. Because they all see the same environment, UR recommends replacing all of them if one is worn.
      5. Inspect the blue joint lids for cracks and their screws for tightness. The lid screws are set to 0.4 Nm (±0.05 Nm) with a calibrated tool.

      Functional and safety checks, which UR recommends monthly:[3]

      1. Press the teach pendant's emergency stop and check that the robot stops and cuts power to the joints.
      2. Freedrive (pulling the arm into position by hand, see 2.1): set the correct TCP, payload and center of gravity. Next, move the arm to a stretched-out horizontal position and check that it holds its position.
      3. Backdrive: test it on all joints. It lets you move the arm to a safe position, for example when it is close to colliding with something.
      4. Check that the safety settings still match the cell's risk assessment and that the extra safety inputs and outputs still work.
      5. Check the date and clock in the log. The CMOS battery that keeps the clock has a long shelf life of up to 10 years.
      6. Back up the robot's data (see 9.5).

      Control box:[3]

      1. With the power cable disconnected, check that the control board terminals are properly inserted and no wires are loose.
      2. Clean dust with an ESD vacuum. The box has two filters, one on each side; clean them with low-pressure air or replace them if they are very dirty or damaged.
      3. Listen to the fan while it runs. If it sounds abnormal, contact the service provider or replace it.

      Each check, its date and anything found or fixed goes into the robot's maintenance log, so the next inspection can compare against it.

      07. Structural fasteners, collisions, and torque verification

      Robot arm mounting bolts and tool mounting bolts must be functionally inspected after any heavy collision.[4]

      A collision sends a shock through the arm, the tool and their bolts (2.5 End-of-Arm Tooling and I/O covers how the tool is mounted).

      Looking at a bolt does not tell you whether it is tight enough. A functional inspection requires a calibrated torque wrench to confirm that robot arm bolts and tooling fasteners are properly torqued.[4] The torque values come from the service manual.

      08. Check your understanding

      • How do reactive, preventive and predictive maintenance differ, and what did NIST find about downtime in plants that rely most on reactive maintenance?
        Show answer

        Reactive maintenance is done after equipment has failed or stopped. Preventive maintenance is scheduled, timed, or based on an operating cycle. Predictive maintenance is initiated by predictions of failure made from observed data such as temperature, noise and vibration. Establishments relying most heavily on reactive maintenance experienced 3.3 times more downtime than those relying on it least. (Section 01)

      • Why is compressed air prohibited when cleaning a robot arm, and what type of vacuum cleaner must be used to clean dirt and dust inside the control box?
        Show answer

        Compressed air must never be used to clean the robot arm because it can damage internal components. Dirt and dust inside the control box must be cleaned with an electrostatic discharge (ESD) vacuum cleaner. (Sections 04 and 05)

      • When must robot arm mounting bolts and tool mounting bolts undergo functional inspection, and what instrument must technicians use to verify that they are properly tightened?
        Show answer

        They must be functionally inspected after any heavy collision. Technicians must use a calibrated torque wrench to confirm they are properly torqued. (Section 07)

      1. Label each case reactive, preventive or predictive. Case one: a gripper is replaced after it fails in the middle of a shift. Case two: grease is changed every 12 months whatever the condition. Case three: a joint is serviced because its motor current has been rising for a month.
      2. For the predictive case, name the record this lesson says must be kept next to the sensor data for the prediction to be useful.
      3. Write a four-line cleaning and inspection checklist for a robot arm and its control box, using only rules from this lesson.
