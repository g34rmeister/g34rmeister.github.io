5.6 Odometry

Gerald Lê

01. Dead reckoning and mobile robot pose

Before an autonomous mobile robot can plan a collision-free path or navigate toward a target room, it must answer the most fundamental question in robotics: Where am I? In outdoor environments, GPS receivers provide periodic global coordinates. However, GPS signals cannot penetrate concrete buildings, warehouse metal roofs, or sub-surface tunnels, and their consumer accuracy (two to five meters) is far too coarse for indoor navigation.[1]

Indoor mobile robots rely on odometry: the mathematical process of estimating current position and orientation by integrating the rotational motion of the vehicle's wheels over time.[1] Odometry is a direct implementation of dead reckoning, a maritime navigation technique where an explorer advances a known starting location using estimated speed, elapsed time, and heading course. In robotics, the inputs are proprioceptive measurements collected from digital wheel encoders.[1]

On a flat two-dimensional floor, the spatial configuration of a mobile robot is described by its pose \(\mathbf{p}\) in a fixed global Cartesian coordinate frame:[1]

\[\mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \theta \end{bmatrix}\]

where \(x\) and \(y\) represent the physical position of the robot's kinematic center in meters, and \(\theta\) denotes the heading angle in radians, measured counter-clockwise from the positive \(X\)-axis.[1]

02. Differential-drive forward kinematics

The most widely deployed kinematic architecture for indoor mobile robotics is the differential-drive robot.[1] A differential platform features two independently actuated coaxial drive wheels mounted on a common lateral axis, complemented by one or two passive caster wheels that support vertical weight while rolling freely in any direction.[1]

The physical geometry of the platform is defined by two mechanical constants:[1]

  • Wheel diameter (\(D\)): The outer nominal diameter of the left and right drive wheels in meters (with wheel radius \(r = D / 2\)).
  • Wheelbase / Track width (\(b\)): The physical lateral distance between the center contact patches of the two drive wheels on the floor surface.

Over a discrete sampling interval \(\Delta t\) (such as 20 milliseconds), optical encoders attached to each wheel shaft register count increments \(\Delta\text{ticks}_L\) and \(\Delta\text{ticks}_R\). Using the Counts Per Revolution (CPR) from 4x quadrature decoding, the linear ground displacement traveled by each wheel's contact patch is:[1]

\[d_L = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}_L}{\text{CPR}}\right) \times (\pi \cdot D), \quad d_R = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}_R}{\text{CPR}}\right) \times (\pi \cdot D)\]

03. The kinematic update equations: displacement and rotation

Because the two drive wheels are rigidly linked to the chassis along a common axle, their individual forward displacements dictate the net motion of the robot's midpoint. The linear displacement of the robot's kinematic center point \(d_{center}\) is simply the average of the two wheel distances:[1]

\[d_{center} = \frac{d_R + d_L}{2}\]

The difference between the two wheel displacements forces the chassis to rotate about its vertical yaw axis. The incremental angular change in heading \(d\theta\) (in radians) is given by:[1]

\[d\theta = \frac{d_R - d_L}{b}\]

Analyzing this equation reveals the fundamental behaviors of a differential drive:

  • Straight-Line Driving: When both wheels travel the exact same distance (\(d_R = d_L\)), the numerator is zero: \(d\theta = 0\). The heading remains unchanged, and the robot drives in a straight line.
  • Leftward Counter-Clockwise Turn: When the right wheel travels farther than the left wheel (\(d_R > d_L\)), \(d\theta\) is positive, causing the robot to turn toward its left.
  • Rightward Clockwise Turn: When the left wheel travels farther than the right wheel (\(d_L > d_R\)), \(d\theta\) is negative, causing the robot to turn toward its right.
  • Spinning in Place: When the wheels spin in equal and opposite directions (\(d_R = -d_L\)), the center displacement is zero (\(d_{center} = 0\)). The robot rotates in place about its kinematic center with zero net translation.

Consider a practical numerical example: in one update the left wheel travels 0.10 m and the right wheel 0.14 m on a robot with a 0.20 m wheelbase. What is \(d\theta\)? Applying the kinematic equation yields:[1]

\[d\theta = \frac{d_R - d_L}{b} = \frac{0.14\text{ m} - 0.10\text{ m}}{0.20\text{ m}} = \frac{0.04\text{ m}}{0.20\text{ m}} = 0.20\text{ radians}\]

Because the right wheel traveled farther than the left, \(d\theta = +0.20\text{ rad}\), representing a counter-clockwise left turn.

04. Numerical integration: Euler forward versus midpoint Runge-Kutta

Once the incremental center displacement \(d_{center}\) and heading change \(d\theta\) are computed, the robot must project this local motion into the fixed global coordinate frame.[1][2]

The simplest approach is Euler forward integration, which assumes the heading remains constant at its initial value \(\theta[k]\) throughout the entire time step:[2]

\[x[k+1] = x[k] + d_{center} \cos(\theta[k])\]

\[y[k+1] = y[k] + d_{center} \sin(\theta[k])\]

\[\theta[k+1] = \theta[k] + d\theta\]

However, when a robot travels along a curved arc, its heading is continuously turning during the sampling interval. Assuming the heading is frozen at the starting value introduces a systematic mathematical chord error on every curve.[1]

A far superior method is midpoint integration (second-order Runge-Kutta), standardly presented in textbooks by Roland Siegwart and colleagues.[1] Midpoint integration evaluates the trigonometry using the average heading halfway through the step:[1]

\[x[k+1] = x[k] + d_{center} \cos\left(\theta[k] + \frac{d\theta}{2}\right)\]

\[y[k+1] = y[k] + d_{center} \sin\left(\theta[k] + \frac{d\theta}{2}\right)\]

\[\theta[k+1] = \theta[k] + d\theta\]

By incorporating the \(\frac{d\theta}{2}\) midpoint term, the algorithm models the circular trajectory traced by a differential drive during coordinated turns, substantially reducing numerical truncation errors.[1] Furthermore, because \(\theta\) continuously accumulates, software must normalize the heading into the canonical interval \((-\pi, \pi]\) radians on every iteration using the standard library atan2 function:[1]

\[\theta = \text{atan2}(\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\]

05. Embedded odometry implementation in C++

The following Arduino C++ implementation demonstrates real-time, non-blocking odometry execution. It uses atomic interrupt locking to read volatile encoder counts safely without racing against high-speed ISR updates:[1] 

const float WHEEL_DIAMETER = 0.065;   // Wheel diameter in meters
const float WHEELBASE      = 0.150;   // Track width in meters
const long  CPR            = 1440;    // Decoded counts per wheel revolution
const float M_PER_TICK     = PI * WHEEL_DIAMETER / CPR;

volatile long leftTicks = 0;          // Updated by hardware encoder ISR
volatile long rightTicks = 0;         // Updated by hardware encoder ISR

long prevLeft = 0;
long prevRight = 0;
float x = 0.0, y = 0.0, theta = 0.0;
unsigned long lastUpdate = 0;

void updateOdometry() {
    // Atomically snapshot volatile tick counts
    noInterrupts();
    long currentL = leftTicks;
    long currentR = rightTicks;
    interrupts();

    // Calculate incremental distance traveled since last update
    float dL = (currentL - prevLeft)  * M_PER_TICK;
    float dR = (currentR - prevRight) * M_PER_TICK;
    prevLeft = currentL;
    prevRight = currentR;

    // Compute center displacement and heading change
    float dCenter = (dL + dR) / 2.0;
    float dTheta  = (dR - dL) / WHEELBASE;

    // Second-order Runge-Kutta midpoint integration
    x += dCenter * cos(theta + dTheta / 2.0);
    y += dCenter * sin(theta + dTheta / 2.0);
    theta += dTheta;

    // Normalize heading angle into (-pi, +pi]
    theta = atan2(sin(theta), cos(theta));
}

void loop() {
    // Execute odometry at a deterministic 50 Hz rate (every 20 ms)
    if (millis() - lastUpdate >= 20) {
        lastUpdate += 20;
        updateOdometry();
    }
    // High-level navigation and motor control logic runs concurrently
}

06. Error propagation: why heading error dominates position error

The Achilles' heel of dead reckoning is cumulative error growth. Because each step updates the pose relative to the previous estimate, any error introduced during a step is permanently locked into future positions. There is no external reference to pull the estimate back to truth.[1][3]

Crucially, angular heading errors cause vastly more position drift than linear wheel distance errors. Why does a 1-degree heading error cause far more position drift than a 1-centimeter wheel diameter calibration error over a 50-meter trip? The answer lies in coordinate geometry: heading errors rotate the entire subsequent coordinate frame, causing lateral position error to grow proportionally to total distance traveled.[1][3]

If an encoder calibration error causes wheel distance to be miscalculated by 1%, the resulting longitudinal position error is simply 1% of distance traveled (0.50 meters after 50 meters). However, if an early turn leaves the robot's heading estimate with an uncorrected error of \(\epsilon_\theta = 1.0^\circ\) (0.0175 radians), every future forward step projects distance along the wrong angle:[1][4]

\[\Delta y_{lateral}(s) \approx s \times \sin(\epsilon_\theta) \approx s \times \epsilon_\theta\]

After traveling \(s = 50\text{ meters}\), that tiny 1-degree heading offset causes a massive lateral cross-track position error of \(50 \times 0.0175 = 0.875\text{ meters}\) (nearly a full meter off target), even if the wheels rolled with absolute millimeter perfection![4]

07. Borenstein and Feng's error taxonomy

In their benchmark 1996 research at the University of Michigan, roboticists Johann Borenstein and Liqiang Feng published a comprehensive scientific taxonomy of mobile robot odometry errors in IEEE Transactions on Robotics and Automation.[4] They established that odometry errors fall into two distinct physical categories:[4]

Error Category Underlying Physical Mechanism Predictability and Calibration
Systematic Errors Inherent physical vehicle imperfections: unequal wheel diameters (\(D_R \neq D_L\)), uncertainty in effective wheelbase (\(b_{actual} \neq b_{nominal}\)), wheel wobble/misalignment, finite encoder sampling resolution.[4] Deterministic and repeatable. Occur consistently on every run. Can be measured experimentally and calibrated away in software.[4]
Non-Systematic Errors Random, unpredictable physical interactions between wheels and the terrain: wheel slip on slippery floors, driving over surface cracks or cables, carpet pile resistance, wheel bounce, external human collisions.[4] Stochastic and non-repeatable. Vary randomly from run to run. Cannot be calibrated away; can only be bounded through multi-sensor fusion.[4]

Borenstein and Feng identified that the two dominant systematic errors in all differential-drive robots are unequal wheel diameters (which causes a robot to trace an unintentional curved trajectory when commanded straight) and wheelbase uncertainty (which causes consistent over-rotation or under-rotation during turns).[4]

08. Calibration in practice: the UMBmark benchmark

To systematically measure and eliminate deterministic vehicle errors, Borenstein and Feng created the University of Michigan Benchmark (UMBmark).[4]

The UMBmark procedure decouples wheel diameter error from wheelbase error through bidirectional testing:[4]

  1. The robot drives a precise 4-meter by 4-meter square path five times in a Clockwise (CW) direction, returning to the starting point.
  2. The robot drives the identical 4-meter by 4-meter square path five times in a Counter-Clockwise (CCW) direction.
  3. The physical stopping coordinates of the robot chassis are measured relative to the origin on every run.

By comparing the centroid of CW stopping errors versus CCW stopping errors, the test mathematically separates the two errors: unequal wheel diameters curve the vehicle in the same physical direction regardless of turn direction, whereas wheelbase errors cause consistent turn-angle errors that manifest symmetrically. The resulting calibration yields precise scaling constants that reduce systematic odometry drift by more than an order of magnitude.[4]

09. Multi-sensor fusion: eliminating slip with IMU gyroscopes

While systematic errors can be calibrated away, non-systematic errors (especially wheel slip on low-friction flooring) remain inevitable. When a robot accelerates hard or turns on slick linoleum, the drive wheels slip. The encoders continue registering rotation, but the robot chassis fails to turn. Odometry assumes a massive turn occurred, permanently corrupting the pose estimate.[4][5]

To defeat wheel slip, modern robots combine wheel odometry with an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) gyroscope.[1] A MEMS gyroscope measures angular yaw rate (\(\omega_z\)) directly through Coriolis acceleration acting on vibrating silicon proof masses. The gyroscope reading is completely independent of wheel-floor friction.[1]

In simple embedded firmware, the heading update from wheel difference is replaced by direct gyroscope integration:[1]

\[d\theta_{gyro} = \omega_z \times \Delta t\]

If the wheels spin out on ice, the gyroscope measures zero angular velocity, correctly reporting that no rotation occurred. In professional robotics systems running ROS 2, sensory data from wheel encoders, IMU gyroscopes, and visual odometry are fused probabilistically using an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) via packages like robot_localization, dynamically weighting each sensor by its mathematical covariance. This foundational sensor fusion architecture is explored in Lesson 5.8.[1][3]

Try it
  1. Calculate the updated pose \((x, y, \theta)\) of a differential-drive robot starting at pose \((0, 0, 0)\) with wheelbase \(b = 0.20\text{ m}\), if the left wheel moves \(d_L = 0.10\text{ m}\) and the right wheel moves \(d_R = 0.14\text{ m}\). Use second-order midpoint integration.
  2. Command a mobile robot to drive forward 2.0 meters along a straight taped line on a gymnasium floor. Measure the physical stop position versus the odometry estimate. Calculate the diameter correction factor \(c = \text{actual} / \text{reported}\).
  3. Spin the robot in place 10 full revolutions (3600 degrees). Compare the physical stopping heading with the odometry heading to determine the effective wheelbase calibration factor \(b_{calibrated} = b_{nominal} \cdot (\theta_{reported} / \theta_{actual})\).
