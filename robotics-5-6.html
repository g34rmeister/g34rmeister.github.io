5.6 Odometry

Gerald Lê

01. Dead reckoning and mobile robot pose

Before an autonomous mobile robot can plan a collision-free path or drive to a target room, it must answer a basic question: Where am I? Outdoors, a GPS receiver can supply global coordinates, but GPS is a poor fit for indoor robots. The U.S. government's GPS site reports that GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within a 4.9 m radius under open sky, that accuracy worsens near buildings, bridges and trees, and that signal blockage and indoor or underground use are common causes of wrong positions.[1] An error of several meters is far too coarse for driving down a hallway.

Indoor mobile robots therefore rely heavily on odometry: estimating the current position and orientation by adding up the motion of the wheels over time, as counted by wheel encoders.[2] Odometry is a form of dead reckoning, a term Borenstein and colleagues trace to the “deduced reckoning” of sailing days: a simple procedure for working out a vessel's present location from a known starting point, its speed, heading and elapsed time.[2] Their survey notes that the vast majority of land-based mobile robots rely on dead reckoning as the backbone of their navigation, because odometry is totally self-contained and needs no outside signals.[2]

On a flat floor, the robot's position and orientation together are called its pose \(\mathbf{p}\), written in a fixed global (world) coordinate frame:[2][3]

\[\mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \theta \end{bmatrix}\]

Here \(x\) and \(y\) are the position of the point midway between the drive wheels, in meters, and \(\theta\) is the heading angle in radians. In this lesson \(\theta\) is measured counter-clockwise from the positive \(X\)-axis, which is the usual mathematical convention.

02. Differential-drive forward kinematics

This lesson uses the differential-drive robot, the design assumed in the odometry equations of Borenstein and colleagues and in common course notes on odometry.[2][4] A differential-drive robot has two independently driven wheels on a common axle, with an encoder on each drive motor counting wheel revolutions.[2] Most small robots of this kind also have one or two passive caster wheels that carry weight and roll freely; they do not appear in the odometry equations.

The geometry that matters for odometry is captured by two constants:[2]

  • Wheel diameter (\(D\)): The nominal outer diameter of the drive wheels in meters (with wheel radius \(r = D / 2\)).
  • Wheelbase / Track width (\(b\)): The distance between the points where the two drive wheels touch the floor.

Over each short sampling interval \(\Delta t\) (for example 20 milliseconds), the encoders register count increments \(\Delta\text{ticks}_L\) and \(\Delta\text{ticks}_R\). Borenstein and colleagues convert pulses to distance with a conversion factor built from the nominal wheel diameter, the encoder resolution and the gear ratio.[2] If CPR is the number of counts per wheel revolution (after any gearing and after quadrature decoding, which raises the resolution of an incremental encoder[2]), the distance rolled by each wheel is:

\[d_L = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}_L}{\text{CPR}}\right) \times (\pi \cdot D), \quad d_R = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}_R}{\text{CPR}}\right) \times (\pi \cdot D)\]

03. The kinematic update equations: displacement and rotation

Because both wheels are fixed to the same chassis, their two distances determine how the robot's midpoint moves. The distance traveled by the midpoint, \(d_{center}\), is the average of the two wheel distances:[2][4]

\[d_{center} = \frac{d_R + d_L}{2}\]

The difference between the two wheel distances turns the chassis about its vertical axis. The change in heading \(d\theta\) (in radians) is:[2][4]

\[d\theta = \frac{d_R - d_L}{b}\]

(Some course notes write the denominator as \(2L\), where \(L\) is half the wheelbase; this is the same formula.[4]) Reading this equation shows the basic behaviors of a differential drive:

  • Straight-Line Driving: When both wheels travel the exact same distance (\(d_R = d_L\)), the numerator is zero: \(d\theta = 0\). The heading remains unchanged, and the robot drives in a straight line.
  • Leftward Counter-Clockwise Turn: When the right wheel travels farther than the left wheel (\(d_R > d_L\)), \(d\theta\) is positive, causing the robot to turn toward its left.
  • Rightward Clockwise Turn: When the left wheel travels farther than the right wheel (\(d_L > d_R\)), \(d\theta\) is negative, causing the robot to turn toward its right.
  • Spinning in Place: When the wheels spin in equal and opposite directions (\(d_R = -d_L\)), the center displacement is zero (\(d_{center} = 0\)). The robot rotates in place about its midpoint with zero net translation.

Worked example (this lesson's own numbers): in one update the left wheel travels 0.10 m and the right wheel 0.14 m on a robot with a 0.20 m wheelbase. What is \(d\theta\)? Applying the equation gives:

\[d\theta = \frac{d_R - d_L}{b} = \frac{0.14\text{ m} - 0.10\text{ m}}{0.20\text{ m}} = \frac{0.04\text{ m}}{0.20\text{ m}} = 0.20\text{ radians}\]

Because the right wheel traveled farther than the left, \(d\theta = +0.20\text{ rad}\), representing a counter-clockwise left turn.

04. Numerical integration: Euler forward versus midpoint Runge-Kutta

Once \(d_{center}\) and \(d\theta\) are known for a step, the robot must turn this local motion into a change of \(x\) and \(y\) in the global frame.[2][4]

The simplest approach is Euler forward integration, which assumes the heading stays at its starting value \(\theta[k]\) for the whole step:

\[x[k+1] = x[k] + d_{center} \cos(\theta[k])\]

\[y[k+1] = y[k] + d_{center} \sin(\theta[k])\]

\[\theta[k+1] = \theta[k] + d\theta\]

(Borenstein and colleagues' report uses the closely related form that projects the step along the new heading \(\theta[k+1]\) instead.[2]) When the robot drives along a curve, however, its heading changes during the step, so using only the start (or only the end) heading puts each step slightly off the true arc. The error is small for short steps but adds up on every curve.

A better method is midpoint integration (second-order Runge-Kutta), the form given in Siegwart and colleagues' textbook and in course notes built on it.[3][4] Midpoint integration evaluates the trigonometry at the average heading halfway through the step:[4]

\[x[k+1] = x[k] + d_{center} \cos\left(\theta[k] + \frac{d\theta}{2}\right)\]

\[y[k+1] = y[k] + d_{center} \sin\left(\theta[k] + \frac{d\theta}{2}\right)\]

\[\theta[k+1] = \theta[k] + d\theta\]

The \(\frac{d\theta}{2}\) term points each step along the chord of the arc the robot actually drove, which is why the midpoint form is more accurate on turns. Because \(\theta\) keeps accumulating, it is also common practice to wrap the heading back into the range \(-\pi\) to \(\pi\) radians after each update, for example with the standard atan2 function:

\[\theta = \text{atan2}(\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\]

05. Embedded odometry implementation in C++

The following Arduino C++ example (written for this lesson; the constants are sample values, not a specific robot) runs odometry on a fixed timer without blocking. It briefly disables interrupts while copying the encoder counts, so an encoder interrupt cannot change a count halfway through the read: 

const float WHEEL_DIAMETER = 0.065;   // Wheel diameter in meters
const float WHEELBASE      = 0.150;   // Track width in meters
const long  CPR            = 1440;    // Decoded counts per wheel revolution
const float M_PER_TICK     = PI * WHEEL_DIAMETER / CPR;

volatile long leftTicks = 0;          // Updated by hardware encoder ISR
volatile long rightTicks = 0;         // Updated by hardware encoder ISR

long prevLeft = 0;
long prevRight = 0;
float x = 0.0, y = 0.0, theta = 0.0;
unsigned long lastUpdate = 0;

void updateOdometry() {
    // Atomically snapshot volatile tick counts
    noInterrupts();
    long currentL = leftTicks;
    long currentR = rightTicks;
    interrupts();

    // Calculate incremental distance traveled since last update
    float dL = (currentL - prevLeft)  * M_PER_TICK;
    float dR = (currentR - prevRight) * M_PER_TICK;
    prevLeft = currentL;
    prevRight = currentR;

    // Compute center displacement and heading change
    float dCenter = (dL + dR) / 2.0;
    float dTheta  = (dR - dL) / WHEELBASE;

    // Second-order Runge-Kutta midpoint integration
    x += dCenter * cos(theta + dTheta / 2.0);
    y += dCenter * sin(theta + dTheta / 2.0);
    theta += dTheta;

    // Normalize heading angle into (-pi, +pi]
    theta = atan2(sin(theta), cos(theta));
}

void loop() {
    // Execute odometry at a deterministic 50 Hz rate (every 20 ms)
    if (millis() - lastUpdate >= 20) {
        lastUpdate += 20;
        updateOdometry();
    }
    // High-level navigation and motor control logic runs concurrently
}

06. Error propagation: why heading error dominates position error

The main weakness of dead reckoning is cumulative error growth. Each step updates the pose relative to the previous estimate, so any error made in one step is carried into every later position. With nothing outside the robot to pull the estimate back to the truth, the position error of odometry grows without bound.[2][4]

Heading errors are especially harmful. Why does a 1-degree heading error cause far more position drift than a 1-centimeter wheel diameter calibration error over a 50-meter trip? Because heading errors rotate the entire subsequent coordinate frame, causing lateral position error to grow proportionally to total distance traveled. Borenstein and Feng describe orientation errors as the main concern because, once incurred, they grow without bound into lateral position errors, and the “Where am I?” report notes that any small momentary orientation error causes a constantly growing lateral position error.[2][5] Course notes make the same point: errors perpendicular to the direction of travel grow much larger.[4]

Worked example (this lesson's own numbers): if a calibration error makes the computed wheel distance 1% too large, the resulting error along the direction of travel is simply 1% of the distance (0.50 m after 50 m). Now suppose instead that an early turn leaves the heading estimate off by \(\epsilon_\theta = 1.0^\circ\) (0.0175 radians). Every later forward step is then projected along the wrong angle:

\[\Delta y_{lateral}(s) \approx s \times \sin(\epsilon_\theta) \approx s \times \epsilon_\theta\]

After \(s = 50\text{ meters}\), that 1-degree offset produces a sideways (cross-track) error of about \(50 \times 0.0175 = 0.875\text{ meters}\), nearly a full meter, even if every wheel distance after the turn was measured perfectly. And unlike the 1% distance error, a heading error usually keeps growing, because each later turn can add a little more.

07. Borenstein and Feng's error taxonomy

In a 1996 paper in IEEE Transactions on Robotics and Automation, Johann Borenstein and Liqiang Feng of the University of Michigan sorted odometry errors into two categories:[5]

Error Category Sources listed by Borenstein and Feng Predictability and Calibration
Systematic Errors Unequal wheel diameters (\(D_R \neq D_L\)); average wheel diameter differing from the nominal value; wheel misalignment; uncertainty about the effective wheelbase (\(b_{actual} \neq b_{nominal}\)); limited encoder resolution; limited encoder sampling rate.[5] Repeatable. They come from the robot itself and act the same way on every run, so they can be measured and corrected in software.[5]
Non-Systematic Errors Travel over uneven floors; travel over unexpected objects on the floor; wheel slippage from slippery floors, over-acceleration, fast turning (skidding), or external and internal forces; non-point wheel contact with the floor.[5] Unpredictable. They come from the interaction with the environment and vary from run to run, so calibration cannot remove them; other sensors are needed to limit their effect.[2][5]

Borenstein and Feng single out unequal wheel diameters and uncertainty about the effective wheelbase as the two most notorious systematic error sources for differential-drive robots.[5] Unequal diameters make a robot that is commanded to drive straight follow a slight curve, and a wrong wheelbase value makes the robot over-rotate or under-rotate during turns.[5]

08. Calibration in practice: the UMBmark benchmark

To measure and correct these systematic errors, Borenstein and Feng created the University of Michigan Benchmark (UMBmark).[5]

The UMBmark procedure is a bidirectional square-path test:[5]

  1. The robot drives a 4 m by 4 m square Clockwise (CW), making four on-the-spot 90° turns and returning to the start. This run is repeated five times.[5]
  2. The robot drives the same 4 m by 4 m square five times Counter-Clockwise (CCW).[5]
  3. After every run, the robot's actual stopping position is measured and compared with the position its odometry reports.[5]

Running in both directions separates the two dominant errors. Borenstein and Feng call a wheelbase error a Type A error: it reduces (or increases) the total amount of rotation in both the CW and CCW runs. Unequal wheel diameters cause a Type B error: the robot's slight curve adds to its turns in one direction and subtracts from them in the other.[5] Comparing the CW and CCW results therefore lets each error be estimated and corrected with scaling factors. In their experiments, the correction improved odometric accuracy with respect to systematic errors by at least one order of magnitude; in the main example the largest systematic error fell from 317 mm to 21 mm, about 15 times smaller.[5]

09. Multi-sensor fusion: limiting slip with IMU gyroscopes

Systematic errors can be calibrated away, but non-systematic errors such as wheel slip on smooth floors cannot.[5] Encoders count wheel rotation, not chassis motion, so when a wheel slips, for example during hard acceleration or a fast turn, the encoders still register rotation and odometry records motion that did not fully happen. Because the error is locked into every later pose, one bad slip during a turn can spoil the estimate for the rest of the run.[2][5]

For this reason robots often pair wheel odometry with other sensors. Borenstein and colleagues' survey of positioning methods lists odometry and inertial navigation as its first two groups of techniques, and their report describes heading sensors such as gyroscopes as able to help compensate for the foremost weakness of odometry.[2][6] A MEMS gyroscope, often part of an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), measures angular rate (\(\omega_z\)) using the Coriolis acceleration acting on a tiny resonating mass, so its reading does not depend on how well the wheels grip the floor.[7]

In simple embedded firmware, the heading change from the wheel difference can be replaced by integrating the gyroscope rate over the step:

\[d\theta_{gyro} = \omega_z \times \Delta t\]

If the wheels spin on a slick patch but the chassis does not turn, the gyroscope reports (close to) zero rotation, while the wheel-based estimate would report a turn. In practice the gyroscope is combined with wheel odometry rather than used alone. In ROS 2 systems, the robot_localization package can fuse wheel odometry and IMU data with an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) (ekf_node) or an Unscented Kalman Filter; the Nav2 setup guide notes that a usual robot has at least wheel encoders and an IMU as odometry sources.[8] Sensor fusion is explored further in Lesson 5.8.

Try it
  1. Calculate the updated pose \((x, y, \theta)\) of a differential-drive robot starting at pose \((0, 0, 0)\) with wheelbase \(b = 0.20\text{ m}\), if the left wheel moves \(d_L = 0.10\text{ m}\) and the right wheel moves \(d_R = 0.14\text{ m}\). Use second-order midpoint integration.
  2. Command a mobile robot to drive forward 2.0 meters along a straight taped line on a gymnasium floor. Measure the physical stop position versus the odometry estimate. Calculate the diameter correction factor \(c = \text{actual} / \text{reported}\).
  3. Spin the robot in place 10 full revolutions (3600 degrees). Compare the physical stopping heading with the odometry heading to determine the effective wheelbase calibration factor \(b_{calibrated} = b_{nominal} \cdot (\theta_{reported} / \theta_{actual})\).
