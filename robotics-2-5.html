2.5 End-of-Arm Tooling and I/O

Gerald Lê

01. What the robot holds, and how it is told to hold it

On its own, a robot arm only carries a flange through space. The work is done by the end-of-arm tooling (EOAT), also called the end effector: a gripper, suction cups, a torch or a screwdriver. In lesson 2.3 you wrote pick-and-place programs in which a single line set a digital output to close a gripper and another line read an input to check for a part. This lesson looks at what sits behind those lines.

Three questions run through the lesson. What can physically hold this part? What does the controller need to know about the tool that is bolted to it? And how does the program find out whether the tool actually did its job? The answers involve some mechanics, some configuration and a good deal of input/output (I/O) programming in URScript and ABB RAPID.

02. Choosing a gripper family

Gripper selection starts from the part: its material, porosity, mass, shape and fragility. It also depends on the utilities the cell has. Some cells have a compressed-air supply; others have only 24 V electrical power. A practical rule of thumb for a first choice:

  • Vacuum (suction cups): Flat, smooth, non-porous parts such as sheet, panels and sealed boxes.
  • Parallel finger grippers: Rigid parts such as machined components. Pneumatic fingers are inexpensive, fast and strong, but are usually either fully open or fully closed. Electric fingers offer programmable force and position, can report their state back to the controller, and need no compressed air.
  • Magnetic grippers: Ferrous (steel) sheet and parts.
  • Soft grippers: Food and irregular or fragile items that rigid fingers would bruise or crush.
  • Tool changers: Cells that must handle several part types, each needing a different tool.

This list is a starting heuristic, not a standard. A real selection checks the candidate gripper's datasheet against the actual part, and it pays to note which version of the datasheet a number came from. For example, Robotiq's 2019 Hand-E instruction manual and its current Hand-E product page give different payload figures for the same gripper.[1][2] This lesson concentrates on vacuum and finger grippers, which you are most likely to meet first.

03. Vacuum grippers: sizing the cups

A suction cup holds a part because the air pressure outside the cup is higher than the partial vacuum inside it. The holding force is the pressure difference multiplied by the effective suction area of the cup, before any safety factor is applied.[3] For a round cup of diameter D, the area is A = π(D/2)².[4] Schmalz's sizing method runs this backwards: from the part's mass, a safety factor, the vacuum level and the number of cups, it gives the cup diameter needed, with a friction coefficient added when the cup face is vertical and the part hangs on friction alone.[3]

The force a cup must supply is larger than the part's weight, because the robot accelerates. Robotiq gives the required holding force as FH = m × (g + a) × S for a horizontal cup lifting straight up (the part is picked from above), FH = m × (g + a/μ) × S for a horizontal cup when the force is horizontal (the part is moved sideways and held by friction), and FH = (m/μ) × (g + a) × S for a vertical cup. Here m is the part mass, g is gravitational acceleration, a is the robot's acceleration, μ is the friction coefficient between cup and part, and S is the safety factor.[5] Robotiq recommends a safety factor of at least 1.5 for smooth parts, 2.0 for porous or oily parts, up to 4.0 when the cup face is vertical, and 2.5 or more for fast swinging or swivelling moves.[5]

Worked example. A 2 kg box is picked from above with a horizontal cup. The robot accelerates upward at a = 5 m/s² and the box is smooth, so S = 1.5. Using Robotiq's formula for a horizontal cup lifting straight up, FH = 2 × (9.81 + 5) × 1.5 = 2 × 14.81 × 1.5 ≈ 44 N.[5] To turn that into a cup size, assume, for this exercise only, that the vacuum generator holds a pressure difference of 60 kPa (a real design takes this from the generator's datasheet). The total cup area must then be at least 44 N ÷ 60,000 Pa ≈ 7.4 × 10-4 m², or about 740 mm². Split over two cups, that is about 370 mm² each, and solving A = π(D/2)² gives D ≈ 22 mm per cup.

The vacuum itself comes either from an electric pump or from an ejector (a venturi device that creates vacuum from a flow of compressed air). Piab, an ejector maker, says ejectors generate no heat or vibration and reduce noise, while conceding that they consume a lot of compressed air.[6] How the ejector is built matters for that air bill. In a stamping study at DaimlerChrysler, multistage ejectors used 1.1 SCFM (standard cubic feet per minute, an industrial measure of compressed-air volumetric flow rate) per cycle, up to 78% less than single-stage generators, and an air-saving check valve that shuts off the air once vacuum is reached reduced consumption further.[7] Piab also claims up to 50% lower compressed-air cost for its compact P2010 multistage ejector, which runs from a 26 psi feed.[8] Treat these as vendor figures: no independent comparison of ejectors and pumps was found for this lesson.

04. Finger grippers and soft grippers

Finger grippers hold a part either by friction (squeezing it) or by form (the fingers wrap around a shape so the part cannot slip out). Datasheets therefore often quote two payloads. The OnRobot RG2 electric gripper, for example, is rated for 2 kg with a force-fit grip and 5 kg with a form-fit grip.[9] Its other datasheet figures show the trade-offs an electric gripper exposes to the programmer:[9]

  • Stroke and force: 110 mm total stroke; grip force adjustable from 3 to 40 N (±25%).
  • Speed: 38 to 127 mm/s, linearly proportional to the force setting, so a gentle grip is also a slower one.
  • Repeatability and build: 0.2 mm repeatability, IP54, 0.78 kg, powered from 24 V at 70 to 600 mA.
  • Power loss: The gripper keeps holding the workpiece if power is lost.

Electric grippers also differ in how they talk to the controller. Zimmer's GEP2000 series comes in three sizes, with 10, 13 and 16 mm of stroke per jaw and up to 200, 360 and 500 N of grip force, and each size is offered with plain digital I/O, with a 0-10 V position-feedback signal, or with IO-Link (a standardized digital point-to-point communication protocol connecting sensors and actuators to industrial controllers).[10] A GEP2000-B variant adds detection of a lost workpiece that is reported to the controller.[11] Vention sells a Universal Robots package for the 16 mm Zimmer gripper (125 to 500 N, 24 V, 2 A maximum, 0.02 mm repeatability) aimed at sites with no compressed air.[12] The choice of interface decides what your program can know: a digital-only gripper reports at most "open" or "closed", while an analog or IO-Link version can report where the jaws actually stopped.

A pneumatic finger gripper is driven by a solenoid valve: the robot switches a digital output, the valve sends air to one side of the gripper's piston, and the fingers move. Grip force is set mechanically, by the air pressure, rather than in the program. That makes them cheap and robust, but they tell the controller little unless sensors are added.

Soft grippers use flexible materials that conform to the item. The OnRobot Soft Gripper, for example, has three interchangeable silicone cups, handles small items under 2.2 kg with a grip size of 10 to 118 mm depending on the cup, needs no external air supply, and is certified for food contact (FDA 21 CFR for non-fatty foods and EC 1935/2004).[13] Its capacity depends on the item's shape, softness and friction.[13] Inflatable soft grippers can handle varied food shapes with forces that do not bruise, although one patent notes that few industrial soft-robotics designs suit washdown or food-contact environments.[14]

05. Mounting: the ISO 9409-1 flange

Grippers bolt to the robot through a standardised mechanical interface defined in ISO 9409-1. The designation encodes the pattern: ISO 9409-1-50-4-M6 means a 50 mm pitch circle with four M6 screws.[15][16] Accessory makers sell adapters for this pattern; Schmalz, for example, offers a 50 mm / M6 flange set described as compatible with Universal Robots arms.[17] It is not the only size. Epson's tool-adapter documentation lists a smaller ISO pattern, a 31.5 mm pitch circle with four M5 screws (31.5-4-M5), alongside the 50 mm, 4 × M6 pattern.[16] Gripper makers often supply a coupling plate for a given pattern; Robotiq's 2019 Hand-E manual, for instance, lists a coupling (GRP-CPL-062) for ISO 9409-1-50-4-M6.[1]

Compare the gripper's coupling with the flange drawing in your robot's manual, and remember that every adapter plate moves the tool further out, which changes the tool center point (TCP) offset.

06. Telling the controller about the tool: payload and center of gravity

The controller does not see the gripper; it has to be told what it carries. Two groups of numbers matter: the TCP (where the working point of the tool is, covered in lesson 2.3) and the payload, meaning the mass of the tool, and later of the tool plus the part, together with the position of its center of gravity (CoG). The controller uses these numbers in its model of the arm's dynamics. If they are wrong, that model is wrong: path accuracy suffers, and on a force-limited cobot the robot may stop for a collision that never happened, or press harder than expected before it reacts to a real one. So a careful program updates the payload at the pick and restores it at the place.

On a Universal Robots arm, UR's own open-source driver script shows the two calls in use. It calls set_payload(mass, cog), with cog given as [x, y, z], and on PolyScope 5.10.0 or newer it calls set_target_payload(mass, cog, inertia, transition_time) instead. On older software it falls back to set_payload and logs "PolyScope < 5.10.0. Inertia and transition_time ignored."[18] The e-Series script manual lists set_payload(m, cog), set_payload_mass(m) and set_target_payload(m, cog, inertia=[0,0,0,0,0,0], transition_time=0).[19]

In ABB RAPID, the tool's load is part of the tooldata record. Its components are robhold (TRUE when the robot holds the tool), tframe (the TCP as a translation and a quaternion) and tload (mass, center of gravity, axes of moment aom, and the inertia values ix, iy, iz).[20] One ABB community example defines a dual gripper of 8.5 kg with its CoG at [10, 0, 80] mm.[21] The RobotStudio API documentation models a load as a 5 kg point mass at (0.023, 0, 0.075) m.[22] The part being carried is handled separately: GripLoad sets the payload of the gripped part relative to the active TCP, whereas the optional \TLoad argument of a move instruction gives the total load of tool and part relative to the wrist flange.[23] The declarations below are an illustrative example with made-up values, not a measured tool: 

! Tool: held by robot, TCP 150 mm along tool Z, 1.2 kg with CoG 60 mm out from the flange
PERS tooldata tGripper := [TRUE, [[0, 0, 150], [1, 0, 0, 0]], [1.2, [0, 0, 60], [1, 0, 0, 0], 0, 0, 0]];
! Part: 0.5 kg, CoG 60 mm from the TCP (used with GripLoad after the grip)
PERS loaddata loPart := [0.5, [0, 0, 60], [1, 0, 0, 0], 0, 0, 0];

07. The I/O on a UR and an ABB controller

A digital input (DI) tells the program whether something is true, such as a part sensor seeing a part. A digital output (DO) switches something, such as a gripper valve. Analog inputs and outputs (AI and AO) carry a value in a range, such as a jaw position.

The Universal Robots e-Series control box provides 16 digital inputs, 16 digital outputs, 2 analog inputs, 2 analog outputs and 4 quadrature digital inputs, powered by a 24 V, 2 A I/O supply.[24] UR describes the digital channels as 8 dedicated (standard) inputs and outputs plus 8 configurable ones, with two more of each at the tool flange; more I/O can be added through a Modbus I/O block.[25] UR's official ROS message definition gives the pin numbering that drivers use:[26]

Group Pin numbers (ROS SetIO) Where it is
Standard digital out 0 to 7 (PIN_DOUT0 to PIN_DOUT7) Control box
Configurable digital out 8 to 15 (PIN_CONF_OUT0 to PIN_CONF_OUT7) Control box
Tool digital out 16 and 17 (PIN_TOOL_DOUT0, PIN_TOOL_DOUT1) Tool flange
Analog out 0 and 1 (PIN_AOUT0, PIN_AOUT1) Control box
Tool voltage 0, 12 or 24 V Tool flange

The tool connector matters most for grippers, because it saves running cables along the arm. A UR community wiki written for the CB3 generation, which has the same tool I/O counts, describes an M8 connector with 2 digital inputs, 2 digital outputs and 2 analog inputs. Signals and tool power are at 12 V or 24 V, switched together, with 600 mA available in total. Because the tool voltage is generated locally, an external power supply does not raise that limit.[27] Check the figures for your controller generation in its own manual. The tool outputs can be set to sink current (NPN), source current (PNP) or push-pull with set_tool_digital_output_mode(n, mode), using mode 1, 2 or 3 respectively.[28] Use the mode your gripper's manual specifies.

On an ABB IRC5, I/O usually comes from fieldbus units. The DSQC 652 is a DeviceNet unit with 16 digital inputs and 16 digital outputs; on the IRC5 Compact these come out on connector XS7 with 24 V, and a forum reply gives 500 mA per output.[29] The usual way to get more is a second DSQC 652 at an unused DeviceNet address, within DeviceNet's limit of 20 I/O devices per controller.[30] In RAPID, signals are not addressed by pin number: each one is given a name in the controller's I/O configuration, and the program uses that name.[31]

08. Reading and writing I/O in code

URScript has separate functions for the control-box I/O and for the tool I/O. The current script manual's Interfaces module lists get_tool_digital_in(n), set_tool_digital_out(n, b) and set_tool_digital_output_mode(n, mode) for the tool connector.[32] For the standard control-box channels, the same module lists get_standard_digital_in(n) and set_standard_digital_out(n, b).[32] A UR forum reply describes the older generic input function as deprecated in favour of these names, so prefer them to the generic get_digital_in and set_digital_out used in lesson 2.3.[33] Tool power is switched with set_tool_voltage, which UR's own driver script calls.[18] 

set_tool_voltage(24)               # power the tool connector at 24 V
set_tool_digital_out(0, True)      # tool output 0 on
set_standard_digital_out(3, False) # control-box output 3 off
if get_standard_digital_in(0):     # control-box input 0
  textmsg("Part present")
end

URScript has no single "wait for input with timeout" instruction in the sources used here, so a timed wait is written as a loop. UR's own driver script uses sync() inside its control loops and reads the control-cycle length with get_steptime(), treating 0.008 s as a CB3 controller and 0.002 s as an e-Series.[18] Calling sync() in a polling loop ends the current control cycle, so the loop checks the input once per cycle instead of spinning and starving the controller. Adding get_steptime() to a counter each time round gives elapsed time.

RAPID uses named signals and dedicated instructions. SetDO sets an output defined in the I/O configuration, and WaitDI waits until an input reaches a value, as in WaitDI di1, 1;.[31][34] A timeout is an optional argument: WaitDI sig, value \MaxTime:=t \TimeFlag:=flag;. With \TimeFlag, an expired timeout sets the Boolean flag to TRUE and execution continues. Without it, the error ERR_WAIT_MAXTIME is raised, which an ERROR handler can catch.[35][36] One user reported \TimeFlag behaving differently in Auto mode, which was not resolved, so test your timeout handling in the mode you will run in.[35] For momentary signals, such as a "cycle done" pulse to a PLC, there is PulseDO, used as PulseDO do1;.[34]

Task URScript RAPID
Set an output set_standard_digital_out(n, b) or set_tool_digital_out(n, b) SetDO doName, 1; (Reset doName; to clear)
Read an input get_standard_digital_in(n) or get_tool_digital_in(n) Signal name used directly, e.g. IF diName = 1 THEN
Wait for an input with timeout Loop with sync() and a counter WaitDI diName, 1 \MaxTime:=t;
Signal identity Pin number Name from the I/O configuration

09. Confirming the grip and handling failures

Setting an output only tells the gripper what to do. It does not prove that the part is held. The rule for a reliable cell is simple: never move away from the pick until the grip is confirmed, and never wait for that confirmation forever.

For suction tools, confirmation usually comes from a vacuum switch wired to a digital input. In one RobotStudio program discussed on ABB's forum, the robot turns the vacuum on with SetDO doVacuum,1; and then waits with WaitDI diVacuum,1; before it moves.[31] In RobotStudio simulation, Smart Components (such as a simulated gripper) can set such inputs in place of real sensors.[37]

Electric grippers can report more. With the Robotiq URCap installed on a UR, rq_is_object_detected() returns True if an object was picked and False otherwise.[38] Underneath, Robotiq grippers report object detection through a status register called gOBJ, but sources disagree about what some of its values mean, so read the manual for your exact gripper and firmware before relying on a particular code.[39][40] The Zimmer GEP2000 can report jaw position as a 0-10 V signal or over IO-Link, and its -B variant reports a lost workpiece.[10][11]

One trap is worth remembering. For a finger gripper, "fully closed" often means the part was missed: the fingers travelled their whole stroke because nothing was between them. Treat a jaw position at full close as a failure, not a success. Also know what your tool does on power loss: the RG2, for example, keeps holding the workpiece.[9]

The error-handling pattern to use in every pick is:

  1. Command: Set the output that closes the gripper or turns on the vacuum.
  2. Confirm: Wait for the confirmation input, with a timeout.
  3. On failure: Open the gripper, retreat to the approach pose, then raise a fault or retry once.
  4. Keep watching: Re-check the grip or vacuum signal before the place move, since a part can slip during transport.

10. Worked example: one pick, two languages

The two programs below put the pieces together. They are this lesson's own illustrative examples and have not been run on a controller, so test them in a simulator (URSim or RobotStudio) before using them on hardware. They assume the following wiring, which you would change to match your gripper: tool output 0 closes the gripper and tool output 1 opens it; standard input 0 is a part-present sensor; tool input 0 is a gripper-closed-on-part (or vacuum-OK) signal. 

def pick_with_io():
  home_q   = [0.0, -1.57, 1.57, -1.57, -1.57, 0.0]       # joint angles (rad)
  pick_p   = p[0.350, 0.250, 0.150, 0.0, 3.14159, 0.0]    # pick pose (m, rad)
  approach_p = pose_add(pick_p, p[0, 0, 0.100, 0, 0, 0])  # 10 cm above

  set_tool_voltage(24)
  set_tool_digital_out(0, False)            # close off
  set_tool_digital_out(1, True)             # open on
  movej(home_q, a=1.2, v=0.5)

  # Wait for part-present, with a 10 s timeout
  t = 0
  while not get_standard_digital_in(0):
    sync()
    t = t + get_steptime()
    if t > 10:
      popup("No part after 10 s", error=True)
      halt
    end
  end

  movel(approach_p, a=0.5, v=0.25)
  movel(pick_p, a=0.3, v=0.05)
  set_tool_digital_out(1, False)            # open off
  set_tool_digital_out(0, True)             # close on
  sleep(0.3)                                # let the fingers settle

  if not get_tool_digital_in(0):            # grip not confirmed
    set_tool_digital_out(0, False)
    set_tool_digital_out(1, True)           # open again
    movel(approach_p, a=0.5, v=0.25)        # retreat
    popup("Grip failed", error=True)
    halt
  end

  set_target_payload(0.5, [0, 0, 0.06], [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0], 0.1)  # tool + part
  movel(approach_p, a=0.5, v=0.25)
  # ... move to the place pose, release, then restore the empty-tool payload
end

The same pick in RAPID, with the signals doGripClose, diPartPresent and diGripClosed defined in the controller's I/O configuration and the tGripper and loPart data from section 06: 

PROC Pick()
    Reset doGripClose;                          ! gripper open
    MoveJ pHome, v1000, z50, tGripper;
    WaitDI diPartPresent, 1 \MaxTime:=10;       ! wait for a part, 10 s max
    MoveL Offs(pPick, 0, 0, 100), v500, z10, tGripper;
    MoveL pPick, v100, fine, tGripper;
    SetDO doGripClose, 1;                       ! close
    WaitDI diGripClosed, 1 \MaxTime:=2;         ! confirm the grip, 2 s max
    GripLoad loPart;                            ! robot now carries the part
    MoveL Offs(pPick, 0, 0, 100), v500, z10, tGripper;
ERROR
    IF ERRNO = ERR_WAIT_MAXTIME THEN
        Reset doGripClose;                      ! open again
        TPWrite "Timeout: part or grip";
        Stop;
    ENDIF
ENDPROC

Compare the two. In URScript the timeout is a loop you write yourself, the signals are pin numbers, and the failure branch sits inline after the check. In RAPID the timeout is one optional argument to WaitDI, the signals have names, and both timeouts land in the same ERROR handler through ERR_WAIT_MAXTIME.[35] That handler cannot tell which wait failed, and it stops without retreating; the exercise below fixes both. After the place move, GripLoad load0; returns to the empty-tool load.

11. Handshaking with a PLC

In a production cell, the robot is rarely in charge. A programmable logic controller (PLC) usually owns the sequence, and the robot acts as a device that exchanges ready, busy and done bits with it. In the common pattern, permissives such as "E-stop OK" and "guard door closed" come from a safety PLC, safety relays or a safety protocol, not from ordinary I/O. The PLC holds cycle-start on until the robot acknowledges; the robot reports busy and then complete; the PLC acknowledges, and the bits reset.[41] The PLC side is often written as a state machine: a start button and part-present together begin the cycle, and the next state is entered only after the robot reports, for example, position OK and gripper closed. Typical causes of failure are differing PLC and robot cycle times, hard-coded timing delays, and missing checks that both controllers agree on the current state.[42]

The table below is an illustrative sequence for the pick in section 10. Signal names and polarity vary between vendors and integrators; Fanuc's PalletTool, for example, uses signals such as cycle-ready, pallet-ready and abort.[43]

Step Signal Meaning
1Safety OK (safety circuit)E-stop and guards are healthy
2Robot → PLC: RobotAtHome = 1Robot is ready at home
3PLC → Robot: PartPresent = 1A part is waiting
4PLC → Robot: CycleStart = 1 (held)Request to run one cycle
5Robot → PLC: Busy = 1Acknowledged; the PLC now drops CycleStart
6Gripper → Robot: GripClosed = 1Grip confirmed, otherwise the robot faults
7Robot → PLC: CycleDone = 1Part placed, robot back home
8PLC → Robot: DoneAck = 1PLC has seen the result
9Robot clears Busy and CycleDoneReady for the next cycle

Every request stays on until it is acknowledged, so a slow scan cannot miss it. Discrete wiring like this works for a handful of bits. When a cell needs many signals, they travel over a fieldbus or industrial Ethernet on one cable instead: the DSQC 652 already sits on DeviceNet,[30] and UR controllers can add I/O over Modbus.[25]

Try it
  1. In URSim, start from your pick-and-place script from lesson 2.3. Replace get_digital_in and set_digital_out with get_standard_digital_in and set_standard_digital_out, then add gripper open and close on tool outputs 0 and 1 with set_tool_digital_out, as in section 10.
  2. Add a "wait for part-present" loop on standard input 0 with a 10 s timeout using sync() and get_steptime(). Run it once with the input off and confirm the popup appears after about 10 s; then toggle the input in the simulator's I/O tab and confirm the robot continues.
  3. Add the grip check on get_tool_digital_in(0) so that a failed grip opens the gripper, retreats to the approach pose and stops.
  4. In RobotStudio, write the RAPID version with SetDO and WaitDI ... \MaxTime. Then change it to use \TimeFlag on both waits so the program reports which wait timed out, and add a retreat move before Stop. Write two or three sentences comparing how each language expresses the timeout and the failure path.
  5. Size the suction cups for a 1.5 kg smooth box picked from above at a = 4 m/s² with μ = 0.5, using Robotiq's horizontal formula and a safety factor of 1.5.
