1.5 Common Applications of Robotics

Gerald Lê

01. Robots at work: the big picture

Robots are no longer rare machines found in a few car plants. Each year the International Federation of Robotics (IFR) counts the industrial robots installed worldwide in its World Robotics report. The 2026 edition, released on September 24, 2026, covers the year 2025:[1]

  • New robots in 2025: 603,307 industrial robots were installed, 11% more than in 2024 and 9% above the old record of 552,946 set in 2022.[1][2]
  • Robots in operation: the operational stock, all industrial robots still working, grew 9% to 5,079,078. For the first time, more than five million robots work in factories.[1][2]
  • By region: Asia installed 457,315 robots (about 76%), Europe 73,054 (about 12%, down 14%) and the Americas a record 57,044 (about 9%).[2]

This is a long trend, not a one-year blip. The 2024 total of about 542,000 was more than double the figure ten years earlier.[3] IFR expects about 655,000 installations in 2026 and about 806,000 in 2029, with the operational stock passing six million in 2028.[2]

02. Where the robots are: countries and robot density

China is by far the largest market. In 2025 it installed 354,226 robots (up 20%), about 59% of the world total, and since 2024 more than half of them have come from Chinese manufacturers.[2]

The two rankings tell different stories. China has about two million robots in operation, roughly 4.5 times as many as Japan in second place, yet ranks only 22nd for density because its factories also employ a very large number of people.[7] When someone calls a country “the most automated,” ask whether they mean total robots or robots per worker.

03. What factory robots do: tasks and industries

Production is still where most robots work. Industrial arms handle heavy, fast and repetitive work, while collaborative robots take on tasks next to human workers. IFR groups their jobs by application:

  • Handling: picking, placing, packing and palletizing parts and loading other machines. This is the largest group: in 2023, the latest year with a public split, about 280,000 handling robots were installed, roughly half of all industrial robots installed that year.[8]
  • Welding: spot welding of car bodies and arc welding of seams, about 92,000 robots in 2023.[8]
  • Assembly, dispensing and processing: fitting parts together; painting, gluing and sealing; and machining, grinding and polishing.
  • Cleanroom work: handling silicon wafers and display panels, where people would bring in too much dust.

By customer industry, the biggest buyers in 2025 were electrical and electronics (142,235 robots, about 24%), automotive (138,429, about 23%, a new peak) and metal and machinery (108,571, 18%, up 22%).[2]

Robots are spreading beyond car plants. In Germany, the automotive industry took 40% of new robots in 2020 but only 23% in 2025, with metalworking close behind at 21%.[6] In the United States, car makers are still the biggest customer at about 35% of installations, but food and beverage installations jumped 30% to about 2,900.[4]

AI already helps in factories, but in specific jobs. IFR calls machine vision for finding and guiding parts, predictive maintenance (spotting a failing part before it breaks) and AI-based inspection “industry-grade.” General-purpose “physical AI” is not there yet.[2]

04. Case studies: warehouses and car plants

Amazon. Amazon started its robotics program in 2012 with one type of robot. On July 1, 2025 it deployed its one millionth robot, at a fulfillment center in Japan. The fleet includes Hercules, which can lift up to 1,250 lb (about 570 kg), and Proteus, Amazon’s first fully autonomous mobile robot. Amazon also launched DeepFleet, a generative AI model that coordinates how the robots move around a building, and says it improves the fleet’s travel time by 10%.[9]

Not every robot makes it. In October 2025 Amazon showed Blue Jay, a cell with several arms that picks and stows items and could handle about 75% of the item types at a South Carolina site. In February 2026 Amazon said it “is no longer utilizing Blue Jay in operations.”[10] Pilots are experiments, and some of them end.

BMW and Figure. At BMW’s plant in Spartanburg, South Carolina, a Figure 02 humanoid robot worked in the body shop in 2025, placing sheet-metal parts for welding with millimetre precision. Over ten months of ten-hour weekday shifts it logged about 1,250 operating hours, moved more than 90,000 parts, walked about 1.2 million steps and helped build more than 30,000 BMW X3 cars.[11] Hard numbers like these are rare for humanoid pilots.

05. Robots in medicine

The best-known medical robot is Intuitive’s da Vinci surgical system. It is teleoperated: a surgeon at a console controls the instruments and the robot follows. It does not operate on its own, and most payers, including Medicare, treat it as a tool and do not pay more because a robot helped.[12]

  • Procedures: about 3,153,000 da Vinci procedures in 2025, up 18% from 2024,[13] with growth of about 14% in the third quarter of 2026.[14]
  • Systems in hospitals: 12,094 da Vinci systems installed by September 30, 2026.[14]
  • Typical operations: gallbladder removal, hernia repair, prostate removal and hysterectomy. More than half of da Vinci procedures treat benign (non-cancerous) conditions.[12]

IFR also reports that sales of exoskeletons, wearable robots that support a person’s movement, rose substantially in 2025.[15]

  • Sidewalk delivery: by April 2025 Starship Technologies had completed more than 8 million autonomous deliveries with a fleet of more than 2,000 robots in 6 countries.[16]
  • Milking robots: Lely introduced its Astronaut milking robot commercially in 1995 and installed its 50,000th in June 2024. Lely says they milk more than 2.5 million cows a day in more than 50 countries.[17]

Robots that work around people need predictable behavior. A hospitality robot in a hotel is often organized as a state machine (a model where a robot switches between distinct operational states based on inputs), such as Idle (waiting for a wake word), Listening and Responding, with a rule that the robot slows or stops whenever a person comes too close.

07. In scientific exploration

Robots go where people cannot easily follow. In orbit, the Canadian Space Agency’s Canadarm2 has worked on the International Space Station since its launch on April 19, 2001. It is 17 m long and has seven degrees of freedom, like a human arm: three at the shoulder, one at the elbow and three at the wrist.[18]

Under the sea, remotely operated and autonomous underwater vehicles map the deep sea floor far beyond the reach of divers. The Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution’s autonomous vehicle Sentry, for example, can dive to 5,000 meters without a tether, mapping the sea floor with multibeam sonar.[19]

08. A closer look: robots on Mars

NASA’s Mars rovers have driven across another planet while scientists on Earth planned their routes and experiments:

  • Sojourner: landed with Mars Pathfinder on July 4, 1997.[20]
  • Spirit and Opportunity: landed in January 2004. Spirit’s mission ended in 2010; Opportunity last contacted Earth on June 10, 2018.[21]
  • Curiosity: landed in Gale Crater on August 5, 2012 (Pacific time).[22]
  • Perseverance: landed on February 18, 2021, carrying the Ingenuity helicopter.[23][24]

A radio signal can take more than 20 minutes to travel one way between Earth and Mars, and NASA puts the longest one-way delay at about 22 minutes.[25] That is why rovers cannot be driven with a joystick. Teams plan routes from orbital images and send commands that the rover carries out the next day.[26] Perseverance uses a self-driving system called AutoNav, which builds a 3D map of the terrain, finds hazards and plans around them while its wheels are turning. NASA gives its possible top speed as up to about 120 meters per hour, compared with roughly 20 meters per hour for Curiosity.[26] During landing, rovers relay data to Earth through orbiters such as Mars Odyssey and the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.[27]

Self-driving pays off. By October 11, 2026, Perseverance had driven 45.22 km, passing Opportunity’s record of 45.16 km for the farthest drive on another world. Opportunity needed almost 15 years; Perseverance took about five and a half. It has also collected more than two dozen rock samples.[23]

Ingenuity, a small helicopter, was planned for up to five test flights over 30 days. It flew 72 times, for more than two hours in total. On its last flight, on January 18, 2024, sand ripples with no features confused its navigation system and its rotor blades were damaged on landing.[28] It is a good example of engineering margin: a demonstrator that kept working far past its design life.

09. In protecting human lives

Some of the most important robots exist to keep people out of danger:

  • Bomb disposal: police and military teams use remotely operated robots to inspect and disarm explosive devices from a safe distance.[29]
  • Search and rescue: after the World Trade Center collapse in 2001, small robots were sent into rubble too tight or unstable for rescuers (section 10).[30]
  • Nuclear and hazardous sites: after the 2011 Fukushima Daiichi accident, iRobot PackBots entered the reactor buildings on April 17–18, 2011[31][32] to measure radiation and record video, and the Japanese robot Quince followed in July 2011 (section 11).[33]
  • Dangerous industrial work: robots take over welding, painting and heavy lifting, which reduces workers’ exposure to fumes, injuries and repetitive strain.

10. Case study: rescue robots at the World Trade Center

After the 2001 attacks, the Center for Robot-Assisted Search and Rescue (CRASAR), then at the University of South Florida, brought small robots to Ground Zero. According to Robin Murphy’s account, only three models were used on the rubble pile between September 11 and 21: the Inuktun micro-Tracs, the Inuktun micro-VGTV and the Foster-Miller Solem. The robots were called out eight times, and were used on four shifts to search eight voids.[30]

They were shoebox-sized machines. The tethered Inuktun robots weighed about 4.5 kg and measured about 17 × 32 × 6 cm. Most voids were less than a metre across, and the robots went 5 to 20 m into the rubble, compared with about 2 m for a camera on a pole.[30]

The robots found no survivors, but they did find several sets of human remains, some of which the people watching live missed and spotted only later on the recorded video.[30] A robot’s sensors are only as useful as the people and software interpreting them. The deployment was the first major test of rescue robots, and Murphy’s numbers show why field robotics is hard:

  • Tethers: the tether had to be pulled free about 7.75 times per drop into a void.[30]
  • Getting stuck: the robots were stuck or high-centred 8.9% of the time.[30]
  • Poor view: the camera view was blocked for about 18% of each run.[30]
  • Lost robot: the wireless Solem was lost when its radio link dropped out and its safety rope broke.[30]

11. Case study: Fukushima Daiichi

After the 2011 earthquake and tsunami, radiation inside the Fukushima Daiichi reactor buildings was too high for workers. On April 17–18, 2011, iRobot PackBots[31][32] entered the reactor buildings, on the first floors of Units 1 to 3. Quince, a tracked robot of about 50 kg built by the Chiba Institute of Technology, Tohoku University and the International Rescue System Institute, followed, and on July 8, 2011 it reached the third floor of Unit 2.[33]

Other survey robots, decontamination robots and remote-controlled construction machines were also used. The robots were designed to keep working in radiation far beyond what a person can safely be exposed to.[33]

The hardest job is reaching the melted fuel, mixed with metal and concrete, called fuel debris. It has taken robots more than a decade:

  • February 2017, surveying: the Scorpion robot from Toshiba and IRID (the International Research Institute for Nuclear Decommissioning) entered the Unit 2 containment vessel, but deposits blocked its path to the area under the reactor.[34]
  • July 2017, finding: a small Toshiba and IRID submersible entered the flooded Unit 3 containment vessel, and TEPCO reported it had seen what was possibly melted fuel.[35]
  • 2019, touching: a Toshiba device about 30 cm long touched the Unit 2 deposits for the first time.[36]
  • November 2024, retrieving: an extendable “telesco” robot brought out a piece of fuel debris about 5 mm across from Unit 2, the first sample retrieved.[37]

IRID estimates that about 880 tonnes of fuel debris remain in the three reactors.[37] Thirteen years of robot work have retrieved a few grams, which shows both what robots make possible and how far they still have to go.

12. What is new: humanoids and AI foundation models

Humanoid robots get a lot of attention, but they are a small, early application. IFR estimates that about 6,900 humanoids were sold in 2025, from 171 known manufacturers,[15] about 1% of the 603,307 industrial robots installed that year. IFR says “most humanoids are still prototypes or pilot-stage” and that conventional industrial robots are currently economically superior for production work.[2]

Pilots continue. Besides BMW and Figure, Agility Robotics signed a multi-year agreement with the logistics company GXO in June 2024 to rent out its humanoid, Digit, which moves totes onto conveyors.[38] By November 2025 Digit had moved more than 100,000 totes at one GXO site.[39]

A foundation model is a large AI model trained on huge amounts of data that can be adapted to many tasks. In 2025 the first vision-language-action (VLA) models for robots appeared: they take in camera images and an instruction and output robot actions. Examples are Google DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics (March 2025)[40] and NVIDIA’s Isaac GR00T N1, an “open foundation model for generalist humanoid robots.”[41] Amazon’s DeepFleet applies the same idea to a whole fleet of robots.[9]

Keep both halves of the picture in mind. Most automated work in 2025 was still done by conventional arms and mobile robots running carefully engineered programs, and that is what this course teaches you to build.
