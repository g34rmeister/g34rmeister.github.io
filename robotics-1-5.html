1.5 Common Applications of Robotics

Gerald Lê

01. Robots at work: the big picture

Robots are no longer rare machines locked away in a few car plants. Every year the International Federation of Robotics (IFR) counts the industrial robots installed around the world and publishes the totals in its World Robotics report. The 2026 edition, released on September 24, 2026, covers the year 2025.

  • New robots in 2025: factories installed 603,307 industrial robots, 11% more than in 2024. That beat the old record of 552,946 robots, set in 2022, by 9%.
  • Robots in operation: the operational stock, meaning all industrial robots still working, grew 9% to 5,079,078. For the first time, more than five million robots work in factories worldwide.
  • Market value: the robots installed in 2025 were worth about US$18.6 billion.
  • By region: Asia installed 457,315 robots (about 76% of the world total, up 14%), Europe 73,054 (about 12%, down 14%) and the Americas a record 57,044 (about 9%, up 14%).

The growth is not a one-year blip. In 2024 the world installed about 542,000 robots, more than double the number ten years earlier, and that was the fourth year in a row above half a million. IFR expects about 655,000 installations in 2026 and about 806,000 in 2029, roughly 7% growth a year, with the operational stock passing six million in 2028.

02. Where the robots are: countries and robot density

China is by far the largest robot market. In 2025 it installed 354,226 industrial robots, up 20%, which is about 59% of all robots installed worldwide. Since 2024, more than half of the robots installed in China have come from Chinese manufacturers. The next four markets were much smaller:

The two rankings tell different stories. China has about two million industrial robots in operation, roughly 4.5 times as many as Japan in second place, yet IFR ranks it only 22nd for density, because Chinese manufacturing also employs a very large number of people. Korea and Singapore have far fewer robots in total but many more per worker. When you read a claim that one country is “the most automated,” check whether it means total robots or robots per worker.

03. What factory robots do: tasks and industries

Production is still where most robots work. Industrial arms handle heavy, fast and repetitive work, while collaborative robots take on tasks next to human workers. IFR sorts the jobs robots do into application groups:

  • Handling: picking, placing, packing and palletizing parts, and loading and unloading other machines. This is the largest group. In 2023, the latest year with a public split, about 280,000 robots were installed for handling, roughly half of the 541,000 installed that year.
  • Welding: spot welding of car bodies and arc welding of seams. About 92,000 welding robots were installed in 2023.
  • Assembly: fitting, screwing and joining parts, for example in electronics.
  • Dispensing: painting, gluing and sealing, where the robot lays down an even layer along a path.
  • Processing: machining, grinding, polishing and cutting.
  • Cleanroom work: handling silicon wafers and flat-panel displays, where people would bring in too much dust.

IFR also sorts robots by the industry that buys them. In 2025 the three largest customers were:

  • Electrical and electronics: 142,235 robots (about 24% of the total, up 10%).
  • Automotive: 138,429 robots (about 23%, up 10%, a new peak).
  • Metal and machinery: 108,571 robots (18%, up 22%).

Robots are spreading beyond car plants. In Germany, the automotive industry took 40% of new robots in 2020 but only 23% (about 5,800 robots) in 2025, with metalworking close behind at 21% (5,300) and chemicals and plastics at 12% (3,000). In the United States, car makers are still the biggest customer, with about 13,500 robots or 35% of US installations, but food and beverage installations jumped 30% to about 2,900. IFR names food, warehousing, logistics and medical work as growth areas in the US.

Artificial intelligence already helps in factories, but in specific jobs. IFR describes machine vision for finding and guiding parts, predictive maintenance (spotting a failing part before it breaks) and AI-based inspection as “industry-grade.” General-purpose “physical AI,” a robot that can learn almost any task, is not yet at that level.

04. Case studies: warehouses and car plants

Amazon. Amazon started its robotics program in 2012 with a single type of robot. On July 1, 2025 it deployed its one millionth robot, at a fulfillment center in Japan. Its fleet includes Hercules, a mobile robot that can lift up to 1,250 lb (about 570 kg), Pegasus, and Proteus, Amazon’s first fully autonomous mobile robot. At the same time Amazon launched DeepFleet, a generative AI model that coordinates how robots move around a building. Amazon says it improves the travel time of its robot fleet by 10%. The company also says it has trained more than 700,000 employees in new skills since 2019.

Not every robot makes it. In October 2025 Amazon showed Blue Jay, a cell with several arms that picks, stows and consolidates items. It went from idea to production in just over a year and could handle about 75% of the types of items at a site in South Carolina. In February 2026 Amazon updated its announcement to say it “is no longer utilizing Blue Jay in operations.” Pilots are experiments, and some of them end.

BMW and Figure. At BMW’s plant in Spartanburg, South Carolina, a Figure 02 humanoid robot worked in the body shop in 2025. Its job was to take sheet-metal parts and place them for welding with millimetre precision. Over ten months of ten-hour shifts, Monday to Friday, it logged about 1,250 operating hours, moved more than 90,000 parts, walked about 1.2 million steps and helped build more than 30,000 BMW X3 cars. BMW’s next step is to evaluate the newer Figure 03. Numbers like these are rare, because most humanoid pilots publish very little data.

05. Robots in medicine

The best-known medical robot is Intuitive’s da Vinci surgical system. It is a teleoperated robot: a surgeon sits at a console and controls the instruments, and the robot follows those motions. It does not operate on its own. Most payers, including Medicare in the US, treat it as a tool and do not pay more for a surgery because a robot helped.

  • Procedures: surgeons performed about 3,153,000 da Vinci procedures in 2025, up 18% from about 2,683,000 in 2024. Procedures grew about 14% year over year in the third quarter of 2026.
  • Systems in hospitals: the installed base reached 12,094 da Vinci systems on September 30, 2026. In that quarter Intuitive placed 546 systems, 315 of them the newer da Vinci 5.
  • Typical operations: gallbladder removal (cholecystectomy), hernia repair, prostate removal (prostatectomy) and hysterectomy. More than half of da Vinci procedures treat benign (non-cancerous) conditions.

IFR reports that surgical robots were a key driver of medical robot sales in 2025 and that sales of exoskeletons, wearable robots that support or strengthen a person’s movement, rose substantially.

06. Service robots and human interaction

A service robot does useful work for people outside classic factory production. IFR reports that sales of professional service robots (not counting medical robots) grew 24% in 2025. Approximate sales by application, read from IFR’s chart:

Transport and logistics robots make up “nearly every second” professional service robot. More companies also rent robots instead of buying them, a model called Robotics-as-a-Service (RaaS), which IFR says grew at a high double-digit rate.

  • Sidewalk delivery: by April 2025, Starship Technologies’ delivery robots had completed more than 8 million autonomous deliveries. Its fleet of more than 2,000 robots crossed about 125,000 roads and driveways a day in 6 countries and more than 150 locations.
  • Milking robots: Lely introduced its Astronaut milking robot commercially in 1995 and installed its 50,000th unit in June 2024, in Northern Ireland. Lely says its robots milk more than 2.5 million cows a day in more than 50 countries.
  • Hospitality: robots in hotels interact with guests directly, the second-largest group in the table above.

Robots that work around people need predictable behavior. A hospitality robot’s behavior is often organized as a state machine, such as Idle (waiting for a wake word), Listening and Responding, with a rule that the robot slows or stops whenever a person comes too close. You will build state machines like this later in the course.

07. In scientific exploration

Robots go where people cannot easily follow. NASA’s Mars rovers, from Sojourner in 1997 through Spirit and Opportunity, Curiosity and Perseverance, have driven across another planet while scientists on Earth planned their routes and experiments. A radio signal takes roughly 3 to 22 minutes to travel one way between Earth and Mars, so rovers cannot be driven with a joystick. Perseverance uses a self-driving system called AutoNav to plan its own path around hazards.

In orbit, the Canadian Space Agency’s Canadarm2 has worked on the International Space Station since it was launched on the Space Shuttle Endeavour on April 19, 2001. It is 17 m long, has a mass of 1,497 kg and has seven degrees of freedom, like a human arm: three at the shoulder, one at the elbow and three at the wrist.

On Earth, remotely operated and autonomous underwater vehicles map the deep sea floor and study ocean life far beyond the reach of divers. The Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution’s autonomous vehicle Sentry, for example, can dive to 5,000 meters with no tether. It carries multibeam sonar to map the sea floor, a CTD sensor (conductivity, temperature and depth), an optical backscatter sensor, two magnetometers and a sensor for the chemistry of hydrothermal vents, and it navigates with inertial sensors and underwater acoustic beacons.

08. A closer look: robots on Mars

  • Sojourner: landed with Mars Pathfinder on July 4, 1997.
  • Spirit and Opportunity: landed in January 2004. Spirit’s mission ended in 2010; Opportunity last contacted Earth on June 10, 2018.
  • Curiosity: landed in Gale Crater on August 5, 2012 (Pacific time).
  • Perseverance: landed on February 18, 2021, carrying the Ingenuity helicopter.

With signals taking minutes each way, rover teams plan routes from orbital images and send commands that the rover carries out the next day. Perseverance’s AutoNav builds a 3D map of the terrain, finds hazards and plans around them while its wheels are turning. NASA gives its possible top speed as up to about 120 meters per hour, compared with roughly 20 meters per hour for Curiosity. Its wheels also have 48 treads each, twice as many as Curiosity’s 24. During landing, rovers relay data to Earth through orbiters such as Mars Odyssey and the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.

That self-driving pays off. By October 11, 2026, Perseverance’s odometer read 45.22 km (28.10 miles), passing Opportunity’s record of 45.16 km for the farthest distance driven on another world. Opportunity needed almost 15 years to drive that far; Perseverance did it in about five and a half. Along the way it has collected more than two dozen rock samples, including one called Cheyava Falls that shows potential signs of past life, and its MOXIE instrument made oxygen from the carbon dioxide in the Martian air.

Ingenuity, a small helicopter, showed that robots can fly on Mars too. NASA planned up to five test flights over 30 days. Instead, Ingenuity flew 72 times, for more than two hours in total, and covered more than 14 times the distance planned. On its last flight, on January 18, 2024, it was flying over smooth, featureless sand ripples that confused its navigation system, and its rotor blades were damaged on landing. NASA ended the mission on January 25, 2024. Ingenuity is a good example of engineering margin: a demonstrator built to prove one idea kept working far past its design life.

09. In protecting human lives

Some of the most important robots exist to keep people out of danger:

  • Bomb disposal: police and military teams use remotely operated robots to inspect and disarm explosive devices from a safe distance.
  • Search and rescue: after the World Trade Center collapse in 2001, small robots were sent into rubble too tight or unstable for rescuers. They found no survivors, but the deployment was the first major test of rescue robots and shaped how they are designed and used today.
  • Nuclear and hazardous sites: after the 2011 Fukushima Daiichi accident, iRobot PackBots entered the reactor buildings on April 17–18, 2011 to measure radiation and record video, and the Japanese robot Quince followed in July 2011. Robots were still at work there in 2024.
  • Dangerous industrial work: robots take over welding, painting and heavy lifting, which reduces workers’ exposure to fumes, injuries and repetitive strain.

10. Case study: rescue robots at the World Trade Center

After the 2001 attacks, the Center for Robot-Assisted Search and Rescue (CRASAR), then at the University of South Florida, brought small robots to Ground Zero. According to Robin Murphy’s account, only three models were actually used on the rubble pile between September 11 and 21: the Inuktun micro-Tracs, the Inuktun micro-VGTV and the Foster-Miller Solem. Two other robots, the PackBot and the SPAWAR Urbot, were used briefly in nearby buildings. The rescue robots were called out eight times, and were used on four shifts to search eight voids. The micro-Tracs went on seven of those eight call-outs.

These were shoebox-sized machines. The Inuktun robots measured about 17 × 32 × 6 cm, weighed about 4.5 kg, moved at no more than 7.6 cm/s and were connected to the operator by a tether. The wireless Solem was larger, about 51 × 37 × 20 cm and 15 kg. Most of the voids they entered were less than a metre across. The robots went 5 to 20 m into the rubble, compared with about 2 m for a camera on a pole.

The robots found no survivors, but they did find several sets of human remains. Some of those remains were missed by the people watching the live video and were spotted only later, when the recordings were reviewed. That is a lesson in itself: a robot’s sensors are only as useful as the people and software interpreting them.

Murphy also recorded what went wrong, and the numbers show why field robotics is hard:

  • Tethers: the tether had to be pulled free about 7.75 times per drop into a void.
  • Getting stuck: operators gave about 9.25 “gravity assists” per drop, helping the robot along by hand, and the robots were stuck or high-centred 8.9% of the time.
  • Poor view: the camera view was blocked for about 18% of each run.
  • Lost robot: the Solem was lost when its wireless link dropped out and its safety rope broke.
  • Time in a void: the average run lasted 6 minutes 44 seconds and the longest 24 minutes 40 seconds.

Murphy’s published account of the deployment remains a useful read for anyone designing rescue robots.

11. Case study: Fukushima Daiichi

After the 2011 earthquake and tsunami, radiation inside the Fukushima Daiichi reactor buildings was too high for workers. On April 17–18, 2011, iRobot PackBots made the first robot entry into the reactor buildings, on the first floors of Units 1 to 3. Each PackBot weighs about 30 kg and carried a gamma camera; the PackBot fleet built up more than 40 sieverts of radiation exposure, and some parts had to be replaced. Quince, a tracked robot of about 50 kg built by the Chiba Institute of Technology, Tohoku University and the International Rescue System Institute, followed. On July 8, 2011 it reached the third floor of Unit 2, the first robot to reach an upper floor, trailing several hundred metres of cable behind it.

Other survey robots, decontamination robots and remote-controlled construction machines were also used. iRobot’s 222 kg Warrior began vacuuming and clearing obstacles on June 30, 2011, and remote-controlled construction machines moved rubble about 1 km. The robots were designed to tolerate radiation of several hundred millisieverts per hour, far beyond what a person can safely be exposed to. The reactors reached cold shutdown on December 16, 2011, and on April 18, 2012 the Survey Runner robot made the first entry into a basement, in Unit 2.

The work did not end there. The melted nuclear fuel, mixed with metal and concrete, is called fuel debris, and finding it has taken robots more than a decade:

  • February 2017, surveying: the Scorpion robot, built by Toshiba and the International Research Institute for Nuclear Decommissioning (IRID), entered the Unit 2 containment vessel. It found that part of the metal grating inside the reactor’s support pedestal had fallen, and deposits blocked its path, so it could not reach the area under the reactor vessel.
  • July 2017, finding: a small submersible robot from Toshiba and IRID was lowered into the flooded Unit 3 containment vessel on July 19, 2017. On July 21, TEPCO reported that it had seen what was possibly melted fuel and fallen debris inside the pedestal.
  • 2019, touching: a Toshiba device about 30 cm long was built to touch the deposits in Unit 2 for the first time.
  • November 2024, retrieving: an extendable “telesco” robot gripped a piece of fuel debris about 5 mm across from Unit 2 and brought it out, the first sample ever retrieved. The target was a sample of about 3 grams or less.

IRID estimates that about 880 tonnes of fuel debris remain in the three damaged reactors. Thirteen years of robot work produced a few grams. Fukushima shows both what robots make possible and how far they still have to go.

12. What is new: humanoids and AI foundation models

Humanoid robots get a lot of attention, but they are still a small, early application. IFR estimates that about 6,900 humanoid robots were sold worldwide in 2025, mostly by companies based in Asia, and it has identified 171 humanoid manufacturers. Compare that with 603,307 industrial robots installed in the same year: humanoids are about 1% of that number. IFR says “most humanoids are still prototypes or pilot-stage” and that conventional industrial robots are currently economically superior for production work. Wider use depends on reliability, safety, how long the robots can run, and cost.

Pilots are moving forward anyway. Besides the BMW and Figure work above, Agility Robotics signed what it called the industry’s first multi-year humanoid Robots-as-a-Service agreement with the logistics company GXO on June 27, 2024, after a pilot in late 2023. Its humanoid, Digit, moves totes from collaborative robots and mobile robots onto conveyors, and by November 20, 2025 it had moved more than 100,000 totes at GXO’s facility in Flowery Branch.

The software side is changing too. A foundation model is a large AI model trained on huge amounts of data that can be adapted to many tasks. In 2025 robotics companies began releasing vision-language-action (VLA) models, which take in camera images and a spoken or written instruction and output robot actions:

  • Gemini Robotics (Google DeepMind, March 12, 2025): a VLA model built on Gemini 2.0. DeepMind says it more than doubles performance on a broad generalization benchmark compared with other leading VLA models, and it is working with the humanoid maker Apptronik.
  • Isaac GR00T N1 (NVIDIA, March 2025): released as an “open foundation model for generalist humanoid robots.”
  • DeepFleet (Amazon, July 2025): a foundation model that coordinates the movement of a whole fleet of warehouse robots rather than a single robot.

Keep both halves of the picture in mind. The robots doing most of the world’s automated work in 2025 were conventional arms and mobile robots running carefully engineered programs, and that is what the rest of this course teaches you to build.
