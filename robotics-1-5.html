      1.5 Common Applications of Robotics

      Gerald Lê
      Chapter 1, lesson 5 of 7 ← Previous: 1.4 Preliminary Requirements Next: 1.6 Robot Anatomy and Coordinate Frames →
      Start here

      This lesson shows where robots work today, from factories and warehouses to hospitals, farms and space. It matters because it gives you a feel for what robots do before you learn how they are built. You do not need to memorize any of the numbers. On a first read, pick one or two application sections that interest you. You can safely skim the country tables and the disaster details.

      • You will learn where robots work today, from factories and warehouses to hospitals and disaster zones.
      • You will learn why total robot count differs from robot density per worker.
      • You will learn how robots operate on Mars, deep underwater and in extreme rescue missions.
      • You will learn why humanoid robots and AI foundation models are still in early stages.

      01. Robots at work: the big picture

      Before you learn how to build or program a robot, it helps to see where robots already work. Today you can find them welding car bodies in factories, lifting heavy loads in warehouses, and assisting surgeons in hospitals. Seeing these jobs shows you which skills matter most for the kind of robot you want to work on. Most robots still work inside factories, and one industry group tracks those numbers each year.

      That group is the International Federation of Robotics (IFR). Each year, it counts industrial robots installed around the world in its World Robotics report. Its 2026 edition covers the year 2025:[1]

      • New robots in 2025: Factories installed 603,307 industrial robots. That was 11% more than in 2024 and 9% above the old record of 552,946 set in 2022.[1][2]
      • Robots in operation: The operational stock is all industrial robots still working, like cars on the road versus cars sold this year. It grew 9% to 5,079,078. For the first time, more than five million robots work in factories.[1][2]
      • By region: Asia installed 457,315 robots (about 76%). Europe installed 73,054 (about 12%, down 14%), and the Americas installed a record 57,044 (about 9%).[2]

      In plain words: more than half a million new industrial robots are installed each year, and the total number working keeps growing.

      For the curious: report release date, ten-year growth, and forecasts

      The 2026 World Robotics edition was released on September 24, 2026.[1]

      The 2024 total of about 542,000 installations was more than double the figure ten years earlier.[3] IFR expects about 655,000 installations in 2026 and about 806,000 in 2029, with the operational stock passing six million in 2028.[2]

      02. Where the robots are: countries and robot density

      Optional on first reading. This section compares national markets and robot density figures. You can safely skip these tables now and return to them in lesson 5.3.

      Not every country uses robots in the same way. Some places buy huge numbers of factory arms simply because they have huge factories. Smaller countries often put more robots beside each human worker. Looking at both numbers helps you avoid a common trap when people talk about which country is “most automated.”

      China is by far the largest market. In 2025 it installed 354,226 robots, up 20% from 2024. That was about 59% of the world total. Since 2024, more than half of those robots have come from Chinese manufacturers.[2]

That is because its factories also employ a very large number of people.[7] When someone calls a country “the most automated,” ask whether they mean total robots or robots per worker.

      In plain words: think of cars and cities. A big city can have more cars in total than a small town, but fewer cars per person.

      03. What factory robots do: tasks and industries

      Most factory robots do jobs that are too heavy, too fast, or too repetitive for people to do safely all day. A robot arm might weld a car body, pack boxes onto a pallet, or handle a silicon wafer inside a clean room. Traditional industrial arms handle heavy and fast work, while collaborative robots work right beside human teammates. Industry reports group these factory jobs into four main kinds of work.

      Production is still where most robots work. IFR sorts industrial robot jobs by application:

      • Handling: picking, placing, packing, and palletizing (stacking items on a pallet) parts, plus loading other machines. This is the largest group. In 2023, the latest year with a public split, factories installed about 280,000 handling robots. That was roughly half of all industrial robots installed that year.[8]
      • Welding: spot welding of car bodies and arc welding of seams. Factories installed about 92,000 welding robots in 2023.[8]
      • Assembly, dispensing, and processing: fitting parts together, painting, and dispensing (applying glue or sealant). This group also covers machining, grinding, and polishing.
      • Cleanroom work: handling silicon wafers and display panels, where people would bring in too much dust.

      Three customer industries bought the most robots in 2025.[2] Electrical and electronics led with 142,235 robots (about 24%). Automotive came next with 138,429 (about 23%, a new peak). Metal and machinery followed with 108,571 (18%, up 22%).[2] Factory robots are also spreading beyond car plants into areas such as metalworking and food production.

      Artificial intelligence (AI) already helps in factories, though only in specific jobs. IFR calls three factory AI uses “industry-grade.” The first is machine vision (a camera and software) for finding and guiding parts. The second is predictive maintenance, which spots a failing part before it breaks. The third is AI-based inspection for checking parts for flaws. General-purpose “physical AI” is not there yet.[2]

      You will explore machine vision in lesson 7.1 and predictive maintenance in Chapter 9.

      For the curious: factory robot shares in Germany and the United States

      In Germany, the automotive industry took 40% of new robots in 2020 but only 23% in 2025, with metalworking close behind at 21%.[6]

      In the United States, car makers are still the biggest customer at about 35% of installations, while food and beverage installations jumped 30% to about 2,900.[4]

      04. Case studies: warehouses and car plants

      Numbers tell you how many robots exist, while real workplaces show what those robots do every day. In a warehouse, mobile robots move heavy loads across the floor so workers do not have to carry them. On a car assembly line, a two-legged humanoid robot has even been tested placing metal parts for welding. These two case studies also show an important lesson: not every robot pilot makes it into daily use.

      Amazon. Amazon shows how robots move things in a warehouse. The company started its robotics program in 2012 with one type of robot. On July 1, 2025, it deployed its one millionth robot at a fulfillment center in Japan.

      The fleet includes Hercules, which can lift up to 1,250 lb (about 570 kg). It also includes Proteus, Amazon’s first fully autonomous mobile robot. Amazon also launched DeepFleet, a generative AI model that coordinates how the robots move around a building. Amazon says DeepFleet improves the fleet’s travel time by 10%.[9]

      Not every robot makes it. In October 2025, Amazon showed Blue Jay, a cell with several arms that picks and stows items. It could handle about 75% of the item types at a South Carolina site. In February 2026, Amazon said it “is no longer utilizing Blue Jay in operations.”[10] Pilots are experiments, and some of them end. A demo is not a product.

      BMW and Figure. In this test, a humanoid robot did real factory work for ten months. A Figure 02 humanoid robot worked in the body shop at BMW’s plant in Spartanburg, South Carolina, in 2025. It placed sheet-metal parts for welding with millimeter precision.

      Detailed operating numbers show what it achieved over ten months of ten-hour weekday shifts. It logged about 1,250 operating hours and moved more than 90,000 parts. It walked about 1.2 million steps and helped build more than 30,000 BMW X3 cars.[11] Hard numbers like these are rare for humanoid pilots.

      05. Robots in medicine

      In a hospital, robots help surgeons control instruments during an operation, or work as wearables that support a person’s movement. They do not replace the doctor, and they do not decide what to do on their own. Instead, a trained person stays in charge while the machine makes motions steady and precise.

      The best-known medical robot is Intuitive’s da Vinci surgical system. It is teleoperated: a surgeon at a console controls the instruments and the robot follows. In International Organization for Standardization (ISO) terms from lesson 1.1, this makes it a robotic device rather than an autonomous robot. The da Vinci system does not operate on its own. Most payers (insurance organizations that pay for healthcare), including Medicare, treat it as a tool. They do not pay more because a robot helped.[12]

      • Procedures: Surgeons performed about 3,153,000 da Vinci procedures in 2025, up 18% from 2024.[13] Growth reached about 14% in the third quarter of 2026.[14]
      • Systems in hospitals: Hospitals had 12,094 da Vinci systems installed by September 30, 2026.[14]
      • Typical operations: Common uses include gallbladder removal, hernia repair, prostate removal, and hysterectomy. More than half of da Vinci procedures treat benign (non-cancerous) conditions.[12]

      IFR also reports that sales of exoskeletons rose substantially in 2025.[15] Exoskeletons are wearable robots that support a person’s movement, fitting the wearable robot category from lesson 1.1.

      06. Service robots around people

      Outside factories, robots now roll down sidewalks with deliveries, clean floors, greet guests in hotels, and milk cows on farms. Because these machines share space with everyday people, they cannot stay separated from people. They must behave in steady, predictable ways whenever someone walks nearby.

      A service robot does useful work for people outside factory production. IFR reports that sales of professional service robots, not counting medical ones, grew 24% in 2025.[15] Approximate sales, read from IFR’s chart, are shown below for reference:

      • Sidewalk delivery: By April 2025, Starship Technologies had completed more than 8 million autonomous deliveries. Its fleet held more than 2,000 robots across 6 countries.[16]
      • Milking robots: Lely introduced its Astronaut milking robot commercially in 1995 and installed its 50,000th in June 2024. Lely says these robots milk more than 2.5 million cows a day in more than 50 countries.[17]

      Robots that work around people need predictable behavior. Engineers often organize a hotel hospitality robot as a state machine. That is a model where a robot switches between distinct operational states based on inputs. Example states are Idle (waiting for a wake word), Listening, and Responding. A rule on top makes the robot slow or stop whenever a person comes too close.

      In plain words: a state machine works like a traffic light. The machine stays in one mode until a trigger switches it to the next step. You will learn to build state machines in lesson 6.7.

      07. Exploring space and the deep sea

      Some environments are too far away or too deep underwater for people to work there directly. Robots let scientists move heavy gear in orbit and map the ocean floor far beyond the reach of divers. Whether swinging an arm outside a space station or cruising near the seabed, a robot can go where humans cannot easily stay.

      In orbit, the Canadian Space Agency’s Canadarm2 has worked on the International Space Station since its launch on April 19, 2001. It is 17 meters long and has seven degrees of freedom, like a human arm. It has three joints at the shoulder, one at the elbow, and three at the wrist.[18]

      Under the sea, remotely operated and autonomous underwater vehicles map the deep sea floor far beyond the reach of divers. For example, the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution’s autonomous vehicle Sentry can dive to 5,000 meters without a tether. It builds its maps with multibeam sonar, which measures distance underwater with sound.[19]

      08. A closer look: robots on Mars

      Driving a robot on another planet is not like steering one with a joystick on Earth. Radio signals take many minutes to travel across space between Earth and Mars. If a rover meets a hazard, waiting for a driver on Earth to react would be too late. That is why Mars rovers must drive part of the way on their own.

      The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) sends Mars rovers across another planet while scientists on Earth plan their routes and experiments:

      • Sojourner: landed with Mars Pathfinder on July 4, 1997.[20]
      • Spirit and Opportunity: landed in January 2004. Spirit’s mission ended in 2010, and Opportunity last contacted Earth on June 10, 2018.[21]
      • Curiosity: landed in Gale Crater on August 5, 2012 (Pacific time).[22]
      • Perseverance: landed on February 18, 2021, carrying the Ingenuity helicopter.[23][24]

      A radio signal can take more than 20 minutes to travel one way between Earth and Mars. NASA puts the longest one-way delay at about 22 minutes.[25] That is why rovers cannot be driven with a joystick. Teams plan routes from orbital images and send commands that the rover carries out the next day.[26]

      In plain words: think of a video call with a 20-minute delay. Because a signal takes so long to arrive, you cannot steer in real time. The rover must drive itself.

      Perseverance uses a self-driving system called AutoNav, which is software that lets the rover drive itself. AutoNav builds a three-dimensional (3D) map of the terrain, finds hazards, and plans around them while its wheels are turning. NASA gives its possible top speed as up to about 120 meters per hour, compared with roughly 20 meters per hour for Curiosity.[26] During landing, rovers relay data to Earth through orbiters such as Mars Odyssey and the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.[27]

      Self-driving pays off. By October 11, 2026, Perseverance had driven 45.22 km, passing Opportunity’s record of 45.16 km for the farthest drive on another world. Opportunity needed almost 15 years, while Perseverance took about five and a half. It has also collected more than two dozen rock samples.[23]

      Ingenuity, a small helicopter, was planned for up to five test flights over 30 days. It flew 72 times, for more than two hours in total. On its last flight, on January 18, 2024, sand ripples with no features confused its navigation system, and its rotor blades were damaged on landing.[28] It is a good example of engineering margin: a demonstrator that kept working far past its design life.

      09. Robots that protect human lives

      Some places are simply too dangerous for people to enter. Whether a building has collapsed, an explosive device may go off, or radiation fills a room, sending a robot first keeps human teams out of harm’s way. Even inside factories, robots take over heavy or messy jobs that strain human bodies.

      Here are four ways robots protect human lives:

      • Bomb disposal: Police and military teams use remotely operated robots to inspect and disarm explosive devices from a safe distance.[29]
      • Search and rescue: After the World Trade Center collapse in 2001, small robots went into rubble too tight or unstable for rescuers (section 10).[30]
      • Nuclear and hazardous sites: After the 2011 Fukushima Daiichi accident, iRobot PackBots entered the reactor buildings on April 17–18, 2011[31][32] to measure radiation and record video. The Japanese robot Quince followed them into the buildings in July 2011 (section 11).[33]
      • Dangerous industrial work: Robots take over welding, painting, and heavy lifting. That reduces workers’ exposure to fumes, injuries, and repetitive strain.

      10. Case study: rescue robots at the World Trade Center

      Disaster sites are far harder for robots than clean labs or factory floors. Collapsed buildings leave tight voids, unstable rubble, blocked camera views, and poor radio links. Looking at a real rescue mission shows you why field robots fail so often and what engineers still have to solve.

      After the 2001 attacks, the Center for Robot-Assisted Search and Rescue (CRASAR), then at the University of South Florida, brought small robots to Ground Zero. Teams used three shoebox-sized robot models on the rubble pile between September 11 and 21. The robots were called out eight times and searched eight voids over four shifts.[30]

      Most voids were less than a meter across. The small robots went 5 to 20 meters into the rubble, compared with about 2 meters for a camera on a pole.[30] They found no survivors, though they did find several sets of human remains. Rescuers missed some of these during the live feed and spotted them only later on the recorded video.[30]

      The deployment was the first major test of rescue robots, and four problems showed why field robotics is hard:

      • Tethers: Power and communication cables snagged in the rubble and had to be pulled free several times per drop.[30]
      • Getting stuck: The robots got wedged or high-centered (stuck on an obstacle with their tracks lifted off the ground).[30]
      • Poor view: The camera view was blocked for part of each run.[30]
      • Lost robot: One wireless robot was lost when its radio link dropped out and its safety rope broke.[30]
      For the curious: robot models, dimensions, and field measurements from Murphy’s report

      According to Robin Murphy’s account, only three models were used on the rubble pile between September 11 and 21: the Inuktun micro-Tracs, the Inuktun micro-VGTV, and the Foster-Miller Solem. The tethered Inuktun robots weighed about 4.5 kg and measured about 17 × 32 × 6 cm.[30]

      Murphy’s numbers show how often field failures happened: the tether had to be pulled free about 7.75 times per drop into a void, robots were stuck or high-centered 8.9% of the time, the camera view was blocked for about 18% of each run, and the wireless Solem was lost when its radio link dropped out and its safety rope broke.[30]

      11. Case study: robots at Fukushima Daiichi

      Radiation inside a damaged nuclear plant can be far above what a person can safely take. Remote-controlled robots let human teams stay outside while machines go in to take readings and send back video. Even with robots, inspecting and sampling a damaged reactor is slow work that can take more than a decade.

      After the 2011 earthquake and tsunami, radiation inside the Fukushima Daiichi reactor buildings was too high for workers. On April 17–18, 2011, iRobot PackBots[31][32] entered the first floors of the reactor buildings of Units 1 to 3. The tracked robot Quince followed and reached the third floor of Unit 2 in July 2011.[33]

      Teams also used other survey robots, decontamination robots, and remote-controlled construction machines. The robots were designed to keep working in radiation far beyond what a person can safely be exposed to.[33]

      The hardest job is reaching the melted fuel, mixed with metal and concrete, called fuel debris. It has taken robots more than a decade to retrieve a single small sample:

      • February 2017, surveying: The Scorpion robot from Toshiba and the International Research Institute for Nuclear Decommissioning (IRID) entered the Unit 2 containment vessel. Deposits blocked its path to the area under the reactor.[34]
      • July 2017, finding: A small Toshiba and IRID submersible entered the flooded Unit 3 containment vessel. Tokyo Electric Power Company (TEPCO) reported that it had seen what was possibly melted fuel.[35]
      • 2019, touching: A Toshiba device about 30 cm long touched the Unit 2 deposits for the first time.[36]
      • November 2024, retrieving: An extendable “telesco” robot brought out a piece of fuel debris about 5 mm across from Unit 2. This was the first sample retrieved.[37]

      IRID estimates that about 880 tonnes of fuel debris remain in the three reactors.[37] Thirteen years of robot work have retrieved a few grams.

      For the curious: Quince’s builders, weight, and exact climb date

      Quince weighed about 50 kg and was built by the Chiba Institute of Technology, Tohoku University, and the International Rescue System Institute. It reached the third floor of Unit 2 on July 8, 2011.[33]

      12. What is new: humanoids and AI foundation models

      Walking, two-armed humanoid robots get most of the headlines today. Yet if you step inside a real factory, almost all the work is still done by conventional robot arms and mobile robots. Looking at the real numbers helps you separate exciting demos from the tools companies rely on every day.

      Humanoid robots are still a small, early application compared with factory arms. IFR estimates that about 6,900 humanoids were sold in 2025 from 171 known manufacturers.[15] That is about 1% of the 603,307 industrial robots installed that year. IFR says “most humanoids are still prototypes or pilot-stage.” It adds that conventional industrial robots are currently economically superior for production work.[2]

      Pilots still continue. Besides BMW and Figure, Agility Robotics signed a multi-year agreement with the logistics company GXO in June 2024. Under that deal, it rents out its humanoid, Digit, which moves totes onto conveyors.[38] By November 2025, Digit had moved more than 100,000 totes at one GXO site.[39]

      A foundation model is a large AI model trained on huge amounts of data that you can adapt to many tasks. In 2025, the first vision-language-action (VLA) models for robots appeared. They take in camera images and an instruction, then output robot actions. One example is Google DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics (March 2025).[40] Another is NVIDIA’s Isaac GR00T N1, an “open foundation model for generalist humanoid robots.”[41] Amazon’s DeepFleet applies the same idea to a whole fleet of robots.[9]

      This is an active research area that you do not need to master yet. You will return to AI uses and limits in lesson 5.5.

      Most automated work in 2025 was still done by conventional arms and mobile robots running carefully engineered programs. That is what this course teaches you to build.
