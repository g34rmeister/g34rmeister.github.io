      2.1 Types of Programming

      Gerald Lê
      Chapter 2, lesson 1 of 8 ← Previous: Chapter 2 overview Next: 2.2 Common Programming Environments →
      Start here

      This lesson compares the ways engineers teach a robot what to do: jogging on the physical arm, leading a cobot by hand, simulating offline on a PC, or snapping visual blocks together. On a first read, focus on why online teaching still dominates shop floors and how calibration closes the gap between simulation and reality.

      • You will learn how teach pendants, lead-through, offline programming, and block tools differ in workflow and cost.
      • You will learn the pendant safety interlocks: the three-position enabling device and the 250 mm/s reduced teach speed.
      • You will learn how frame and kinematic calibration fix the simulation-to-reality gap.

      01. Ways to program a robot

      Teaching a robot is not like writing an app on your laptop. A bug in ordinary software might show an error box on your screen. A bug in a robot program can drive a heavy arm into steel tooling at speed. That crash can break end-effectors (the tools on the arm) and fixtures and put nearby workers at risk. Before you write a command, you need to choose how you will teach the arm its path.

      To talk clearly about these machines, engineers use vocabulary from the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). ISO publishes ISO 8373, the international standard that fixes the vocabulary of robotics. In everyday words, an industrial robot is a factory arm with three or more movable joints that can be reprogrammed for new jobs; the standard puts it this way: an “automatically controlled, reprogrammable multipurpose manipulator, programmable in three or more axes” used in industrial automation.[1][2]

      Reviews of the field sort robot programming into two main approaches.[3] In online programming, you teach moves right on the physical robot.[3] In offline programming, you build the program on a computer model instead.[3] A third approach, block-based visual programming, lets shop-floor operators without coding experience build jobs for industrial robots and collaborative robots (cobots).[4]

      Which method fits best depends on four things: production volume, path complexity, safety restrictions, and the cost of stopping the factory floor.

      For the curious: ISO 8373 edition cited here

      The edition quoted above is ISO 8373:2021.[1][2]

      02. Teaching on the robot with a pendant

      Suppose you need a robot arm to pick up a part and place it in a machine. With online programming, you walk up to the real arm, steer it to each spot with a handheld controller, and save each stop. You have to pause factory production while you do this, because you are using the live robot. Still, it remains the most common way to program simple shop-floor jobs.

      Online programming means taking the robot out of automatic production and switching its controller to manual mode.[3][5] Your main tool is the teach pendant, a portable handheld control box wired to the robot controller.[5]

      In a standard session, you drive each axis of the arm to a chosen spot and record it as a target point. In a 2017 article, Jonathan Wilkins of EU Automation wrote that this teach method is the most common, with over 90 per cent of industrial robots programmed this way.[6]

      Major robot makers build their own teach pendant hardware to match their controllers:

      • ABB FlexPendant: Used with ABB IRC5 controllers, it has a touch screen, dedicated hardware buttons (four you can assign yourself), and a joystick to jog the arm.[7] Buttons switch the joystick between motion modes such as axes 1–3, axes 4–6, linear moves, and reorienting the tool.[7]
      • FANUC iPendant: Supplied with R-30iB Plus controllers, it offers higher screen resolution and faster processing. Its optional 4D (four-dimensional) Graphics feature animates the robot live on screen. It also shows process data, programmed paths, and Dual Check Safety (DCS) zones, FANUC’s position and speed safety monitoring software.[8]
      • KUKA smartPAD: This pendant pairs an 8.4-inch capacitive touch screen with a 6D (six-degree-of-freedom) mouse puck you can tilt, push, and twist. That puck lets you move and reorient the robot in three or six degrees of freedom. It is hot-pluggable, so you can connect and disconnect it at any time.[9]
      • Universal Robots Teach Pendant: Paired with Universal Robots (UR) cobot controllers running PolyScope, this touchscreen moves the arm with joint position values and on-screen arrows. Those arrows move the tool center point (TCP), the working tip of the tool. You can also switch on Freedrive and pull the arm into position by hand.[10]

      03. Staying safe while teaching up close

      When you teach a robot online, you often stand right beside it, within reach of the arm. A sudden move at full production speed could strike you before you have time to react. That is why safety standards require built-in hardware and software limits whenever a robot runs in manual teach mode.

      Because online programming places a person within reach of an industrial manipulator, safety standards require protections in manual mode. ISO 10218-1, the international safety standard for the design of industrial robots, sets those requirements.[11] A matching national standard in the United States follows the same approach.[5]

      First, the teach pendant carries an enabling device, typically a three-position switch (often called a deadman switch). The worker must hold it in the center-ON position; otherwise the robot system’s motion is inhibited.[5] ABB describes its enabling device as a manually operated, constant-pressure push-button that must be pressed only halfway to switch the motors on.[7] Releasing the switch, or squeezing it fully in a moment of panic, therefore stops the robot.

      Second, teaching is done at reduced speed, measured in millimeters per second (mm/s). Guidance from the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) states that robot speeds during programming sessions are below 250 mm/s (10 inches per second).[5] ABB’s manual states that in manual reduced speed mode the movement is limited to 250 mm/s.[7] This ceiling gives an operator close to the arm time to release the enabling device or press the emergency stop before a collision.

      For the curious: safety standard editions (ISO 10218-1 and ANSI/RIA R15.06)

      The international standard cited here is ISO 10218-1:2025, which sets safety requirements for the design of industrial robots.[11] In the United States, ANSI/RIA R15.06-2012 is the national adoption of the 2011 edition of ISO 10218.[5]

      04. Choosing how the arm moves when you jog

      When you push a teach pendant joystick to the right, which way should the robot go? Should it spin one motor joint, glide straight across the factory floor, or push straight along a drill bit on its wrist? Picking the right coordinate system makes lining up a tilted bracket or a tight hole much easier.

      When you jog an arm with a pendant, you choose a coordinate system and a motion mode. The ABB FlexPendant, for example, offers world, base, tool, and work object coordinate systems, plus axis-by-axis motion modes.[7] Your choice decides how the arm moves when you push the joystick or a button:

      • Joint Jogging: Moves one motor axis at a time (axes 1 through 6). It needs no inverse kinematics math, so it is the simplest mode. You use it to back axes away from joint limits or move out of singular (locked-up) postures.
      • World / Base Coordinates: The controller coordinates several joints so the TCP moves in straight lines. Those lines follow the fixed X, Y, and Z axes of the robot base or the cell. Moving along +Z lifts the tool straight up regardless of the current joint angles.
      • Tool Coordinates (TCP Frame): Motion follows the tool mounted on the robot wrist. Pushing +Z moves the tool along its working direction, such as pushing a drill bit straight into a pilot hole.
      • User / Work Object Coordinates: Motion lines up with a fixture, an inclined conveyor, or a pallet. Suppose a bracket sits tilted at 30 degrees. Setting a work object frame on its surface lets you jog parallel to the part with one axis input. Teaching points in that frame also lets you shift the program to a new spot without re-teaching every point.[12]

      05. Guiding a collaborative robot by hand

      Steering an arm with pendant buttons or a joystick takes practice. On a cobot, you can often skip the joystick and simply grab the arm with your hands. You pull it through each pick or drop spot, save the points, and let the robot replay them. That makes simple setups fast, though a human hand is not as steady as a computer-generated path.

      In lead-through programming (also called hand guiding), you grasp the arm and physically move it through the task.[6] ABB, for example, advertises lead-through programming on its GoFa cobot “by ‘grabbing’ anywhere on the robot.”[13]

      On Universal Robots e-Series arms, you press the Freedrive button on the pendant or on the robot itself and pull the arm into place.[10][14] Freedrive releases the joint brakes so the arm moves with little resistance. Resistance rises as the arm nears a predefined limit or safety plane.[14]

      Cobots sense the forces you apply in two main ways:

      • Joint Torque Sensors: The KUKA LBR iiwa puts torque sensors in all seven axes.[15] The ABB GoFa puts torque and position sensors in each of its six joints.[13] These sensors detect contact right away so the robot can respond to forces applied by a person.[15]
      • Wrist Force/Torque Sensors: The Universal Robots e-Series includes a built-in sensor right behind the tool.[16] A six-axis sensor of this kind measures three forces (Fx, Fy, Fz) and three torques (Mx, My, Mz) at the tool.

      Lead-through programming lowers setup time for simple pick-and-place, machine tending, and palletizing. However, a path taught by hand picks up your hesitations and small errors. Those flaws are hard to fix without reprogramming the whole task.[6] A person cannot guide an arm in a ruler-straight line or hold a steady speed for precision sealing. For those high-precision contours, engineers turn to offline tools.

      06. Programming in 3D on a computer (OLP)

      Every hour a factory welding arm sits idle for programming, it stops making products. Offline programming lets you build and test the whole robot job on a computer while the real robot keeps running. You only pause production at the end to load the new program and check a few points. That saves huge amounts of downtime on complex cells.

      Offline programming (OLP) creates robot programs on a personal computer (PC) instead of on the physical arm.[3][6] The real robot only stops while you download and test the new code.[6] You work inside a three-dimensional (3D) simulation built from computer-aided design (CAD) models of the robot, tooling, parts, and fixtures. FANUC ROBOGUIDE, for example, imports CAD models to lay out cells accurately and uses virtual robots to design and visualize them.[17]

      An offline programming system usually combines four main functions:

      • CAD-to-path generation: The software pulls curves and surfaces from part CAD models and turns them into robot paths. ROBOGUIDE’s WeldPRO package, for example, uses CAD-to-path programming to generate coordinated welding moves, including torch angles and process parameters.[17]
      • Kinematic feasibility check: The software checks whether the arm can reach each target with valid joint angles. In RoboDK, a red cross flags an infeasible program so you can adjust tool orientation, start point, or part placement.[18]
      • Collision detection: The simulation checks the moving robot against everything in the cell. Yaskawa’s MotoSim EG-VRC turns robots red to signal a potential collision so you can fix it before the code reaches the factory floor.[19]
      • Post-processor: A post-processor translates the simulated path into the robot brand’s own language. RoboDK includes over 100 post-processors for more than 1400 robots from 80 manufacturers.[20] The same path can become RAPID for ABB, URScript for Universal Robots, or another brand’s native language.

      07. Closing the simulation-to-reality gap

      A welding path that looks perfect on your monitor can still miss a real seam by a few millimeters on the factory floor. In a 3D model, parts are flawless and tables sit in exact positions. In a real cell, parts warp, fixtures get bolted slightly off-mark, and the arm flexes slightly under load. Calibration measures those real-world differences so your offline program hits its targets.

      The main hurdle in offline programming is the simulation-to-reality gap. In CAD, parts are exact, link lengths have zero error, and fixtures sit at their textbook positions. In the real cell, parts warp, fixtures sit slightly off their CAD spots, and the arm does not move identically to its ideal model.

      Industrial arms are highly repeatable, returning to a taught point reliably, yet they are less accurate on brand-new coordinates. Their absolute positioning error depends on the brand and model.[21] ISO 9283, the international standard for robot performance testing, sets out the criteria and test methods used to measure properties like these.[22] If you load an uncalibrated OLP program straight onto a real arm, a weld gun may miss the seam or gouge the metal.

      Bridging this gap requires calibration before production:

      • Tool Center Point (TCP) Calibration: You touch the tool tip to the same fixed point from several tool angles. The software then calculates the TCP offset from the flange. RoboDK advises 8 points or more to define a TCP accurately, or 3 points if accuracy is not critical.[12]
      • Work Object / User Frame Calibration: You touch three reference points on the physical fixture with your calibrated TCP, such as the origin and points along its axes. The software then calculates the frame. Define the TCP first, because TCP errors carry into the frame.[12]
      • Kinematic Calibration: For demanding jobs like aerospace drilling, a measurement tool such as a laser tracker measures the arm in many poses. Software then finds corrected kinematic parameters. RoboDK states that calibration can improve accuracy by a factor of 2 to 10, reaching up to 0.050 mm for small robots and 0.150 mm for medium-sized robots, depending on the robot and setup.[21] It can remarkably improve the accuracy of robots programmed offline.[21]

      08. Programming with drag-and-drop blocks

      You do not always need to type lines of code to set up a cobot cell. Visual block tools let operators snap commands together like puzzle pieces right on a touchscreen. If you can plan a pick-and-place task step by step, you can build a working program without worrying about typos or brackets. Behind the scenes, the software writes native robot code for you.

      While heavy manufacturing relies on teach pendants and offline simulation, collaborative applications increasingly use block-based programming.[4] Blockly is an open-source library for building visual editors with drag-and-drop blocks, and it powers many popular block-based platforms.[23]

      ABB released Wizard Easy Programming at the end of March 2020 for its single-arm YuMi cobot. Built on Blockly, Wizard lets you drag and drop blocks on the FlexPendant and converts them in real time into ABB’s RAPID language.[24] In November 2023, ABB extended Wizard to six-axis industrial robots with the OmniCore controller. Its blocks cover robot moves, signal instructions, and force control, and a Skill Creator tool lets integrators build custom blocks.[4]

      Universal Robots uses a similar visual layout in PolyScope. Your program appears as a tree of nodes, and you configure each node in the Command tab. To teach a waypoint, you move the arm (for example with Freedrive) and save the waypoint in the tree.[10]

      The block programming demo needs JavaScript. With it on, you can arrange pick-and-place blocks and see the RAPID and URScript they turn into.

      09. Comparing cost, precision, and downtime

      No single programming method wins in every factory. A car plant loses fortunes when a line stops, while a small shop just wants one cobot to tend a mill. Comparing downtime, software cost, accuracy, and operator training helps you pick the method that fits your job.

      Choosing a method means balancing software cost and skills against robot downtime.[3][6] The comparison below sums up the general trade-offs described in the sources. It is a qualitative guide, not measured data.

      Criteria Teach Pendant Jogging Cobot Lead-Through Offline Programming (OLP)
      Robot Downtime High; the robot is out of production while it is taught.[3] Medium; teaching is fast, but the cell is unavailable while teaching. Low; the robot only stops while the new program is downloaded and tested.[6]
      Software Cost No extra software; the pendant comes with the controller. Built into the cobot; ABB’s Wizard block programming, for example, is free and preinstalled.[4] Requires a separate simulation software license and a PC workstation.
      Geometric Precision Limited by the operator’s eyesight and jog alignment. Low on continuous paths; hesitations and inaccuracies creep in.[6] High once the cell is calibrated; points come from CAD models.[21]
      Operator Skill Required Medium; requires knowledge of robot frames and manual modes. Low; intuitive physical guidance with minimal training. High; programming complexity is a major barrier for small firms.[3]
      Ideal Application Simple pick-and-place, small batch adjustments, machine tending. Palletizing, light assembly, packaging, benchtop cobots. Automotive spot welding, multi-axis laser cutting, complex deburring.

      Downtime in car plants is very expensive. A 2024 Siemens survey report put the cost of an idle line at a large automotive plant at $2.3 million per hour, roughly double the 2019 figure.[25] Stopping a body line to teach hundreds of weld spots by pendant would lose massive output, making offline programming attractive there. By contrast, consider a small machine shop with one cobot tending a few computer numerical control (CNC) mills. There, an OLP license is hard to justify, so lead-through programming is usually the economical choice.

      10. Worked examples: spot welding vs. custom deburring

      Let us put those trade-offs side by side with two contrasting jobs. One job spot-welds thousands of identical car bodies with two large arms. The other job smooths rough edges on small batches of cast aluminum parts whose shapes vary slightly. Comparing them shows why factories still keep both offline and online tools.

      The numbers in this case study are an illustrative worked example, not measured data. Picture an automotive spot-welding cell with two six-axis arms, where a new car body needs 450 spot welds. Teaching online with a pendant means jogging to 450 spots, checking gun clearances, and adding approach moves. That might take two technicians 40 to 60 hours inside the cell while the line builds no cars.

      Instead, engineers use an offline suite such as ABB RobotStudio or FANUC ROBOGUIDE. They import car body CAD data, generate weld paths, simulate both robots to avoid collisions, and estimate cycle time. ROBOGUIDE’s virtual controller calculates and validates cycle times, and its SpotPRO package balances welds across several robots while avoiding collisions.[17] During changeover, they download the program and spend a few hours touching up calibration frames before production restarts.

      Now consider deburring (smoothing rough edges on) cast aluminum housings of varying shapes in small batches of twenty. Building an offline CAD cell for every minor part variation would take longer than the production run itself. Here, you can teach the contour in minutes using lead-through or block-based routines, making online programming the better choice.
