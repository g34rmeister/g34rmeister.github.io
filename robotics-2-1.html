2.1 Types of Programming

Gerald Lê

01. The spectrum of programming methods

ISO 8373:2021 defines an industrial robot as an “automatically controlled, reprogrammable multipurpose manipulator, programmable in three or more axes” used in industrial automation.[1][2] Writing instructions for such an arm is different from writing software for a desktop computer. A conventional software bug might throw an exception or show an error dialog. A bug in a robot program can drive a heavy arm into steel tooling at speed, breaking end-effectors and fixtures and putting nearby workers at risk.

For this reason, robot programming methods have developed along distinct paths that balance safety, speed, setup time, and geometric precision. Reviews of the field group them into online programming, where instructions are taught on the physical robot, and offline programming, where the program is built on a computer model.[3] A third approach, block-based visual programming, lets shop-floor operators without programming experience build applications for collaborative and industrial robots.[4]

Which method to select depends on production volume, path complexity, safety restrictions, and the cost of stopping the factory floor.

02. Online programming: teach pendants in practice

Online programming means taking the robot out of automatic production, switching the controller into manual mode, and authoring the motion program directly on the physical machine.[3][5] The main tool for this workflow is the teach pendant, a portable handheld control device connected to the robot controller.[5]

In a standard teach pendant workflow, the technician drives each axis of the robot to a desired position and records that position in the program as a target point. In a 2017 article, Jonathan Wilkins of EU Automation wrote that the teach method is the most common, with over 90 per cent of industrial robots programmed this way.[6]

Major robot manufacturers have developed their own teach pendant hardware tailored to their controllers:

  • ABB FlexPendant: Used with ABB IRC5 controllers, the FlexPendant has a touch screen, dedicated hardware buttons (four of which the user can assign), and a joystick that is used to jog the manipulator.[7] Buttons toggle the joystick between motion modes such as axes 1–3, axes 4–6, linear, and reorient.[7]
  • FANUC iPendant: The iPendant supplied with R-30iB Plus controllers has enhanced screen resolution and processing capability. The optional 4D Graphics feature animates the robot on the pendant in real time and shows process data, safety (DCS, Dual Check Safety, FANUC's position and speed safety monitoring software) zones, and programmed paths.[8]
  • KUKA smartPAD: The smartPAD combines an 8.4-inch capacitive touch display with a 6D mouse (a control puck that can be tilted, pushed, and rotated) that lets the operator jog and reorient the robot in three or six degrees of freedom. It is hot-pluggable, so it can be connected and disconnected at any time.[9]
  • Universal Robots Teach Pendant: Paired with UR cobot controllers running PolyScope, this touchscreen pendant moves the robot with on-screen TCP arrows and joint position values, or by Freedrive, where the operator pulls the arm into position by hand.[10]

03. Pendant safety interlocks and reduced speed

Because online programming places a person within reach of an industrial manipulator, safety standards require hardware and software protections in manual mode. ISO 10218-1:2025 sets safety requirements for the design of industrial robots,[11] and the US standard ANSI/RIA R15.06-2012 is the national adoption of the 2011 edition of ISO 10218.[5]

First, the teach pendant carries an enabling device, typically a three-position switch (often called a deadman switch). The worker must hold it in the center-ON position; otherwise the robot system's motion is inhibited.[5] ABB describes its enabling device as a manually operated, constant-pressure push-button that must be pressed only halfway to switch the motors on.[7] Releasing the switch, or squeezing it fully in a moment of panic, therefore stops the robot.

Second, teaching is done at reduced speed. OSHA guidance states that robot speeds during programming sessions are below 250 mm/s (10 inches per second),[5] and ABB's manual states that in manual reduced speed mode the movement is limited to 250 mm/s.[7] This ceiling gives an operator close to the arm time to release the enabling device or press the emergency stop before a collision.

04. Coordinate frames for manual jogging

When jogging an industrial arm with a teach pendant, the operator selects a coordinate system and a motion mode. The ABB FlexPendant, for example, offers world, base, tool, and work object coordinate systems, plus axis-by-axis motion modes.[7] The choice determines how the arm moves when the joystick or a direction button is used:

  • Joint Jogging: Moves one motor axis at a time (axes 1 through 6). Joint jogging needs no inverse kinematics calculation, so it is the simplest mode. It is used to back axes away from joint limits or move the arm away from singular configurations.
  • World / Base Coordinates: The controller coordinates several joints so the tool center point moves in straight lines along the fixed X, Y, and Z axes of the robot base or the cell. Moving along +Z lifts the tool straight up regardless of the current joint angles.
  • Tool Coordinates (TCP Frame): Motion is defined relative to the tool mounted on the robot wrist. Pushing +Z moves the tool along its working direction (such as advancing a drill bit into a pilot hole).
  • User / Work Object Coordinates: Motion is aligned with a fixture, an inclined conveyor, or a pallet. If a bracket sits tilted at 30 degrees, defining a work object frame on the bracket surface lets the technician jog parallel to the part with single-axis inputs. Teaching targets relative to such a frame also lets a program be moved to a new location without re-teaching every target.[12]

05. Lead-through programming and collaborative robots

Traditional industrial arms are jogged with pendant buttons or joysticks. In lead-through programming (also called hand guiding), the programmer instead grasps the arm and physically moves it through the task.[6] ABB, for example, advertises lead-through programming of its GoFa cobot “by ‘grabbing’ anywhere on the robot.”[13]

On Universal Robots e-Series arms, the operator presses the Freedrive button on the teach pendant (or a Freedrive button on the robot) and pulls the arm into the desired position.[14][10] In Freedrive the brakes are released and the joints move with little resistance; resistance increases as the arm approaches a predefined limit or safety plane.[14]

Collaborative robots sense the forces a person applies in two main ways:

  • Joint Torque Sensors: The KUKA LBR iiwa has joint torque sensors in all seven axes,[15] and the ABB GoFa has torque and position sensors in each of its six joints.[13] These sensors let the robot detect contact immediately and respond to forces applied by a person.[15]
  • Wrist Force/Torque Sensors: The Universal Robots e-Series has a built-in, tool-centric force/torque sensor.[16] A six-axis sensor of this kind measures three forces (Fx, Fy, Fz) and three torques (Mx, My, Mz) at the tool.

Lead-through programming lowers setup time for simple pick-and-place, machine tending, and palletizing. However, teaching paths purely by hand is prone to human inconsistency: hesitations and inaccuracies creep in and are hard to fix without reprogramming the whole task.[6] A person cannot hand-guide an arm along a perfect straight line or hold a constant speed for precision sealing, so for high-precision continuous contours engineers turn to offline tools.

06. Offline programming (OLP) principles and architecture

Offline programming (OLP) generates robot programs on a computer instead of on the physical robot, which only stops while the new program is downloaded and tested.[3][6] The programmer works inside a 3D simulation built from CAD models of the robot, tooling, parts, and fixtures; FANUC ROBOGUIDE, for example, imports CAD models to build accurate layouts and uses virtual robots to design and visualize cells.[17]

An offline programming system typically combines four functions:

  • CAD-to-path generation: The software extracts curves and surfaces from part CAD models and turns them into robot paths. ROBOGUIDE's WeldPRO package, for example, offers CAD-to-path programming that automatically generates coordinated motion programs for welding, including torch angles and process parameters.[17]
  • Kinematic feasibility check: The software checks that each target is reachable with valid joint values. In RoboDK, a red cross marks a program that is not feasible, and the user may need to adjust the tool orientation, start point, or part location.[18]
  • Collision detection: The simulation checks the moving robot against the cell. Yaskawa's MotoSim EG-VRC, for example, turns robots red to signal a potential interference so programmers can fix it before the program reaches the factory floor.[19]
  • Post-processor: A post-processor translates the simulated path into the robot maker's own language. RoboDK states that it includes over 100 post processors to generate programs for more than 1400 robots from 80 robot manufacturers.[20] The same path can therefore be output as RAPID for ABB, URScript for Universal Robots, or the native languages of other brands.

07. The simulation-to-reality gap and cell calibration

The main technical barrier in offline programming is the simulation-to-reality gap. In a CAD environment, parts are perfect, link lengths have no tolerance, and fixtures sit exactly at their nominal positions. In the real cell, parts warp, fixtures are bolted down slightly away from their CAD positions, and the robot itself does not move exactly as its nominal model says.

Industrial robot arms are highly repeatable but not accurate: they return to a taught point very reliably, but their absolute positioning error is larger and depends on the brand and model.[21] ISO 9283 sets out the performance criteria and test methods used to measure properties such as these.[22] If an uncalibrated OLP program is downloaded directly to a real arm, a weld gun programmed to follow a seam may miss the joint or gouge the metal.

Bridging this gap requires calibration before production:

  • Tool Center Point (TCP) Calibration: The tool tip touches the same fixed point with several different tool orientations, and the software calculates the TCP offset from the flange. RoboDK advises 8 points or more to define a TCP accurately, or 3 points if accuracy is not important.[12]
  • Work Object / User Frame Calibration: Three reference points on the physical fixture (for example, the origin and points along its axes) are touched with the calibrated TCP, and the software calculates the frame. The TCP should be defined first, because TCP errors carry into the frame.[12]
  • Kinematic Calibration: For demanding work such as aerospace drilling, a measurement system such as a laser tracker measures the robot in many poses and software identifies corrected kinematic parameters. RoboDK states that calibration can improve accuracy by a factor of 2 to 10, reaching up to 0.050 mm for small robots and 0.150 mm for medium-sized robots, depending on the robot and setup, and that it can remarkably improve the accuracy of robots programmed offline.[21]

08. Block-based visual programming

While heavy manufacturing relies on teach pendants and offline simulation, collaborative applications increasingly use block-based programming.[4] Blockly is an open-source library for building visual programming editors with drag-and-drop blocks, and it powers many popular block-based coding platforms.[23]

ABB released Wizard Easy Programming at the end of March 2020 for its single-arm YuMi collaborative robot. Wizard is built on Blockly: users drag and drop interlocking blocks on the FlexPendant, and the program is converted in real time into ABB's RAPID language.[24] In November 2023 ABB extended Wizard to six-axis industrial robots powered by the OmniCore controller. Its blocks cover robot movements, signal instructions, and force control, and a Skill Creator tool lets integrators build application-specific blocks.[4]

Universal Robots uses a similar visual approach in PolyScope. A program is displayed as a tree of nodes, and each selected node is configured from the Command tab. To teach a waypoint, the operator moves the arm (for example with Freedrive) and sets the waypoint in the program.[10]

The block programming demo needs JavaScript. With it on, you can arrange pick-and-place blocks and see the RAPID and URScript they turn into.

09. Economic and operational trade-offs

Selecting a programming method means balancing software cost and skills against robot downtime.[3][6] The comparison below summarizes the general trade-offs described in the sources; it is a qualitative guide, not measured data.

Criteria Teach Pendant Jogging Cobot Lead-Through Offline Programming (OLP)
Robot Downtime High; the robot is out of production while it is taught.[3] Medium; teaching is fast, but the cell is unavailable while teaching. Low; the robot only stops while the new program is downloaded and tested.[6]
Software Cost No extra software; the pendant comes with the controller. Built into the cobot; ABB's Wizard block programming, for example, is free and preinstalled.[4] Requires a separate simulation software licence and a PC workstation.
Geometric Precision Limited by the operator's eyesight and jog alignment. Low on continuous paths; hesitations and inaccuracies creep in.[6] High once the cell is calibrated; points come from CAD models.[21]
Operator Skill Required Medium; requires knowledge of robot frames and manual modes. Low; intuitive physical guidance with minimal training. High; programming complexity is a major barrier for small firms.[3]
Ideal Application Simple pick-and-place, small batch adjustments, machine tending. Palletizing, light assembly, packaging, benchtop cobots. Automotive spot welding, multi-axis laser cutting, complex deburring.

Downtime in car plants is very expensive: a 2024 Siemens survey report put the cost of an idle production line at a large automotive plant at $2.3 million per hour, roughly double the 2019 figure.[25] Stopping a body line to teach hundreds of weld spots by pendant would therefore cost a great deal of lost output, which is why offline programming is attractive there. Conversely, for a small machine shop with one cobot tending a few CNC mills, an OLP licence is hard to justify, and lead-through programming is usually the economical choice.

10. Case study: spot welding vs. custom deburring

The numbers in this case study are an illustrative worked example, not measured data. Consider an automotive spot-welding cell with two six-axis arms. A new car body requires 450 spot welds. If programmed online with a teach pendant, jogging to 450 points, checking gun clearances, and programming approach moves might take two technicians 40 to 60 hours inside the cell, during which the line cannot build cars.

Using an offline suite such as ABB RobotStudio or FANUC ROBOGUIDE, engineers import the car body CAD data, generate weld paths, simulate the two robots together to avoid collisions, and estimate cycle time. ROBOGUIDE's virtual controller, for example, calculates and validates cycle times, and its SpotPRO package balances spot-welding tasks across several robots while avoiding collisions.[17] When the line shuts down for changeover, the program is downloaded and the technician spends a few hours touching up calibration frames before production restarts.

In contrast, consider a deburring task on cast aluminum housings of varying shapes in small batches of twenty. Building an offline CAD cell for every minor part variation would take longer than the production run itself. Here, an operator can teach the contour in minutes using lead-through or block-based routines, making online programming the better choice.

11. Comparing online and offline workflows

Try it
  1. Imagine an industrial cell where an ABB arm palletizes boxes coming off a conveyor. Would you program this task using joint coordinates, base Cartesian coordinates, or work object coordinates? Justify your decision.
  2. An offline simulation predicts a total cycle time of 14.2 seconds, but the real robot completes the cycle in 16.1 seconds. Identify two physical factors that could account for this discrepancy.
  3. Explore the free Blockly visual environment at docs.blockly.com and construct a loop with an if/else conditional branch. Inspect how visual blocks translate into text-based code.
