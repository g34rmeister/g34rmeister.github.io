5.2 Pulse Width Modulation (PWM)

Gerald Lê

01. Simulating analog control in a digital world

Digital microcontrollers are fundamentally binary devices. Internally, their logic gates and output pins operate strictly at two voltage rails: logic LOW (0 V, Ground) or logic HIGH (5.0 V or 3.3 V). However, the physical world inhabited by robots is analog and continuous. A mobile robot rarely navigates by running its drive motors at either 0% standstill or 100% full-throttle maximum speed. To turn smoothly, track a line, or hold a steady velocity down an incline, the robot controller must command intermediate power levels.[1]

Generating true intermediate analog voltages would require an onboard Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) paired with a high-current linear power amplifier. However, linear regulators control voltage by dropping excess voltage across internal transistors, converting surplus power directly into waste heat. In battery-powered mobile robotics, this inefficiency is unacceptable.[1]

The universal engineering solution is Pulse Width Modulation (PWM).[1] Instead of outputting a steady partial voltage, a digital pin switches between 0 V and full supply voltage thousands of times per second. By modulating the relative duration of time the pulse remains HIGH versus LOW, the circuit delivers an adjustable average electrical power to actuators with near 90% power efficiency.[1]

02. Mathematics of duty cycle and average voltage

A periodic square wave is characterized by two fundamental variables: its period \(T\) (the duration of one complete cycle in seconds, or frequency \(f = 1/T\) in Hertz) and the active pulse duration \(t_{on}\).[1]

The duty cycle \(D\) represents the proportion of each repetitive period during which the digital signal remains in the active HIGH state:[1]

\[D = \frac{t_{on}}{T} = \frac{t_{on}}{t_{on} + t_{off}}\]

When expressed as a percentage, a 0% duty cycle is permanently OFF (0 V), a 50% duty cycle is ON for half the period and OFF for half the period, and a 100% duty cycle is permanently ON (continuous supply voltage). Because mechanical actuators like DC motors possess mechanical inertia and electrical inductance, they act as natural low-pass filters, integrating the fast electrical pulses into a smooth mechanical response.[1]

The effective average voltage \(V_{avg}\) delivered across the load is directly proportional to the duty cycle and peak supply voltage \(V_{cc}\):[1]

\[V_{avg} = D \times V_{cc}\]

Consider a practical robotics example: a motor driver runs at a 25% duty cycle from a 12 V battery supply. What is the average voltage delivered to the motor? The calculation is straightforward:[1]

\[V_{avg} = 0.25 \times 12\text{ V} = 3.0\text{ V}\]

In the Arduino programming environment, the built-in function analogWrite(pin, value) controls hardware PWM.[2] Despite its name, analogWrite() does not produce an analog voltage; it configures the internal microcontroller timer to generate a PWM square wave. The parameter value is an 8-bit integer ranging from 0 (0% duty cycle, permanently 0 V) to 255 (100% duty cycle, permanently 5.0 V). For instance, commanding an average 3.0 V from a 5.0 V Arduino rail corresponds to:[2]

\[\text{value} = \text{round}\left(\frac{3.0\text{ V}}{5.0\text{ V}} \times 255\right) = \text{round}(0.60 \times 255) = 153\]

04. Microcontroller hardware timers: ATmega328P and RA4M1

Generating PWM by manually toggling pins in software (using loops and microsecond delays) wastes CPU processing cycles and results in timing jitter whenever an interrupt triggers. Professional microcontrollers generate PWM entirely through autonomous hardware timer/counter peripherals.[1] Once configured, the hardware timer toggles the output pin automatically at the clock level without any CPU software overhead.[1]

On the Arduino Uno R3 (ATmega328P), PWM output is driven by three internal hardware timers across six dedicated pins:[1]

  • Timer0 (8-bit): Drives PWM on pins 5 and 6. It operates in Fast PWM mode with a clock prescaler of 64, yielding a base carrier frequency of approximately 976.56 Hz (commonly rounded to 980 Hz). Note: Timer0 also drives the internal system tick counter for millis() and delay(), so altering Timer0 registers directly alters sketch timekeeping![1]
  • Timer1 (16-bit): Drives PWM on pins 9 and 10. It operates in Phase-Correct PWM mode with a clock prescaler of 64, generating a carrier frequency of approximately 490.20 Hz (commonly rounded to 490 Hz). Phase-correct mode counts up to 255 and then counts down to 0, producing symmetrical pulses that reduce electrical motor noise.[1]
  • Timer2 (8-bit): Drives PWM on pins 3 and 11. It operates in Phase-Correct PWM mode with a prescaler of 64, resulting in a carrier frequency of approximately 490.20 Hz (490 Hz).[1]

On the modern 32-bit Arduino Uno R4 (Renesas RA4M1), timing is handled by General PWM Timers (GPT) clocked up to 48 MHz.[3] The R4 supports native 16-bit and 32-bit resolution across all PWM pins, allowing frequencies to be configured into the tens of kilohertz without sacrificing resolution.[3]

05. DC motor PWM versus RC servo pulse timing

A frequent source of confusion among roboticists is the difference between PWM used for DC motor speed control and PWM used for RC servomotor position control. While both signals are pulse-width modulated square waves, they communicate fundamentally different physical quantities:[4][5]

Feature DC Motor PWM RC Servomotor PWM
Controlled Variable Electrical power, armature voltage, and rotational speed Angular shaft position (0° to 180°)
Signal Metric Duty cycle percentage (0% to 100%) Absolute high-pulse duration (1.0 ms to 2.0 ms)
Carrier Frequency 490 Hz, 980 Hz, or ultrasonic (>20 kHz) Fixed at 50 Hz (period \(T = 20\text{ ms}\))
Power Handling Carries motor drive current through external H-bridge Logic control signal only; power delivered on separate lines
Internal Driver Requires external H-bridge (L298N, TB6612FNG) Control circuit, H-bridge, and gear train built inside servo

A standard hobby servomotor (such as the SG90, MG996R, or Dynamixel series) contains an internal potentiometer connected to the output shaft, a small DC motor, an H-bridge driver, and a feedback control chip.[5] The servo decodes the absolute width of a positive pulse sent once every 20 milliseconds (50 Hz):[4][5]

  • 1.0 millisecond pulse: Commands the servo to its minimum angle (0 degrees).
  • 1.5 millisecond pulse: Commands the servo to its neutral center position (90 degrees).
  • 2.0 millisecond pulse: Commands the servo to its maximum angle (180 degrees).

What pulse width sent at 50 Hz commands a standard hobby servomotor to its neutral center position? The answer is 1.5 milliseconds (1500 microseconds).[4][5] In practice, manufacturing tolerances mean real servos vary slightly; the Arduino Servo library defaults to a pulse range between 544 microseconds and 2400 microseconds to accommodate extended travel.[4]

06. The Timer1 conflict: Servo library and pin trade-offs

When programming robotic arms or mobile robots that combine DC motors and hobby servos, engineers encounter a critical architectural conflict on the Arduino Uno R3.[4]

To generate jitter-free microsecond pulses for servos, the Arduino Servo library commandeers the 16-bit Timer1 peripheral.[4] Why does calling myServo.attach() on an Arduino Uno R3 disable analogWrite() PWM on pins 9 and 10? The answer is that the Servo library reconfigures Timer1 into input capture/interrupt mode, which disables the hardware PWM output channels connected to pins 9 and 10.[4]

If an engineer attempts to drive a DC motor driver using PWM on pin 9 or pin 10 while simultaneously controlling a servo on any pin, analogWrite(9, speed) will fail to generate a proper PWM wave. The motor will either stop completely or jump to full speed. Therefore, when designing an Arduino robot incorporating servos, motor PWM lines must always be allocated to pins 3, 5, 6, or 11.[4]

07. Acoustic whine and ultrasonic modulation

When driving DC motors at standard Arduino PWM frequencies (490 Hz or 980 Hz), robots frequently emit an irritating, high-pitched acoustic whistle.[6]

Human hearing spans roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz), with maximum acoustic sensitivity between 1,000 Hz and 4,000 Hz.[6] The carrier frequencies of 490 Hz and 980 Hz sit squarely within this audible window. As the square wave pulses current through the motor coils, the rapidly expanding and collapsing magnetic fields induce mechanical stress within the iron core laminations and copper windings (a phenomenon known as magnetostriction). The entire motor acts as an acoustic loudspeaker, radiating sound into the robot chassis.[6]

To silence robotic drives, industrial motor controllers and advanced hobby ESCs (electronic speed controllers) elevate the PWM carrier frequency to ultrasonic rates above 20 kHz (typically 20 kHz to 32 kHz). At 25 kHz, mechanical coil vibrations occur beyond the upper limit of human hearing, making the motor completely silent.[6]

However, ultrasonic PWM introduces an electrical trade-off: switching power losses. Every time a power MOSFET or BJT transitions between fully ON and fully OFF, it passes through a linear resistance zone where both voltage and current are non-zero, dissipating energy as heat:[7][8]

\[P_{switching} = \frac{1}{2} V_{supply} I_{load} (t_{rise} + t_{fall}) f_{PWM}\]

Switching at 25 kHz dissipates approximately 50 times more switching heat in the motor driver than switching at 500 Hz. Engineers must ensure driver MOSFETs feature low gate capacitance, fast gate drivers, and adequate heat dissipation when operating at ultrasonic frequencies.[7][8]

08. Mechanical dead zones, stiction, and flyback protection

When applying PWM to physical DC motors, two practical electrical and mechanical constraints must be managed:

  • Mechanical Dead Zone (Stiction): Real gearboxes suffer from static friction, or "stiction." At low duty cycles (for example, analogWrite values below 40 out of 255, or ~15% duty cycle), the electromagnetic torque produced by the rotor is insufficient to overcome the mechanical friction of the gear teeth and bearings. Current flows through the windings, dissipating resistive heat, but the shaft remains motionless. Robotic controllers must incorporate a software dead zone compensation offset, mapping output commands so that the lowest non-zero command jumps immediately to the minimum breakaway threshold.
  • Inductive Flyback Voltage Spikes: Because motor windings are inductors, abruptly turning off current generates a destructive reverse voltage spike (\(V = -L \frac{di}{dt}\)). H-bridge motor drivers must incorporate high-speed flyback diodes (Schottky or fast-recovery diodes) across each switching transistor to clamp these inductive spikes and recirculate current safely back to the power rails.[7][8]

09. Application case study: precision robotic gripper

A robotic parallel gripper illustrates the power of combining PWM with sensor feedback. If a gripper is driven purely by a binary on/off switch, the fingers slam shut at maximum speed and force, either crushing delicate objects (such as eggs or fruit) or causing the motor to stall and overheat.[7]

By modulating the motor with PWM, the robot executes a sophisticated staged grasping routine:

  1. Approach Phase: The gripper closes at a high duty cycle (e.g., 70% PWM) to minimize cycle time while moving through free air.
  2. Deceleration Phase: Optical distance sensors or tactile switches detect proximity to the target object, prompting the controller to drop PWM to a gentle 20% duty cycle.
  3. Contact and Grip Holding: Current sensors or stall-detecting shaft encoders indicate that finger motion has stopped against the object. The controller applies a continuous holding duty cycle (e.g., 15% PWM), supplying sufficient torque to prevent the object from slipping without overheating the motor windings or crushing the payload.
Try it
  1. In the interactive PWM simulator above, set the supply voltage to 12 V and adjust the duty cycle slider until the average output voltage displays 6.0 V. Calculate the corresponding 8-bit integer value required by Arduino analogWrite().
  2. Connect a standard hobby servomotor to an Arduino board and write a sketch using myServo.writeMicroseconds(). Sweep pulse widths between 1000 and 2000 microseconds in 100-microsecond increments, observing the resulting shaft angles.
  3. Connect a DC gearmotor to an H-bridge driver and slowly ramp the PWM duty cycle from 0 to 255 in steps of 5. Identify the exact threshold value at which the shaft begins turning; this represents your motor's static friction dead zone.
