5.2 Pulse Width Modulation (PWM)

Gerald Lê

01. Simulating analog control in a digital world

Digital microcontroller pins are binary: an output is either fully on (the board's supply voltage, such as 5 V on an Uno or 3.3 V on boards like the MKR family) or fully off (0 V).[1] However, the physical world inhabited by robots is analog and continuous. A mobile robot rarely drives its motors at only 0% or 100% speed. To turn smoothly, track a line or hold a steady speed down a slope, the controller must command intermediate power levels.

Producing a true intermediate voltage at motor currents would need extra analog circuitry, such as a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) followed by a power amplifier, and a linear amplifier turns the voltage it drops into heat. In battery-powered robots, that wasted heat means shorter run time, so designers avoid it.

The standard solution is Pulse Width Modulation (PWM), "a technique for getting analog results with digital means".[1] Instead of outputting a steady partial voltage, a pin switches rapidly between 0 V and the full supply voltage. Changing the fraction of time the signal stays on simulates voltages between 0 V and the supply.[1] Because the switches are either fully on or fully off most of the time, little power is lost in them, apart from the switching losses discussed in Section 07.[2]

02. Mathematics of duty cycle and average voltage

A periodic square wave has two basic variables: its period \(T\) (the duration of one complete cycle in seconds, or frequency \(f = 1/T\) in hertz) and the on-time \(t_{on}\) within each period.[1]

The duty cycle \(D\) is the fraction of each period during which the signal is HIGH:[1]

\[D = \frac{t_{on}}{T} = \frac{t_{on}}{t_{on} + t_{off}}\]

As a percentage, a 0% duty cycle is always off, a 50% duty cycle is on for half of each period, and a 100% duty cycle is always on.[1] A DC motor responds to the average of these fast pulses because its rotor's inertia and its winding's inductance smooth them out; the pulses are much faster than the motor can react to.

The effective average voltage \(V_{avg}\) across the load is the duty cycle times the supply voltage \(V_{cc}\):[1]

\[V_{avg} = D \times V_{cc}\]

Worked example: a motor driver runs at a 25% duty cycle from a 12 V battery. What is the average voltage delivered to the motor? The calculation is straightforward:

\[V_{avg} = 0.25 \times 12\text{ V} = 3.0\text{ V}\]

In the Arduino environment, analogWrite(pin, value) controls hardware PWM.[3] Despite its name, analogWrite() does not output an analog voltage on most pins: it makes the pin generate a steady rectangular wave of the chosen duty cycle until the next call.[3] The value runs on a 0 to 255 scale: 255 means always on and 127 means on about half the time.[1] Worked example: commanding an average of 3.0 V from a 5.0 V Arduino pin corresponds to:

\[\text{value} = \text{round}\left(\frac{3.0\text{ V}}{5.0\text{ V}} \times 255\right) = \text{round}(0.60 \times 255) = 153\]

04. Microcontroller hardware timers: ATmega328P and RA4M1

Generating PWM by toggling a pin in software (with loops and short delays) ties up the processor and is disturbed whenever an interrupt runs. Microcontrollers instead generate PWM with hardware timer/counter peripherals: once configured, the timer's output-compare hardware switches the pin by itself on every timer cycle.[4]

On the Arduino Uno R3 (ATmega328P), PWM comes from three hardware timers across six pins. The Arduino core sets the prescaler of all three timers to 64, so each timer counts at \(16\text{ MHz} / 64 = 250\text{ kHz}\).[4][5]

  • Timer0 (8-bit): Drives PWM on pins 5 and 6 in Fast PWM mode, which counts from 0 to 255 and restarts, giving \(16\text{ MHz} / (64 \times 256) \approx 976.56\text{ Hz}\) (listed by Arduino as 980 Hz).[4][5][3] Timer0 also drives millis() and delay(), so changing its settings breaks sketch timekeeping.[6][4] Arduino also notes that pins 5 and 6 give slightly higher-than-expected duty cycles at low settings because of this shared timer.[3]
  • Timer1 (16-bit): Drives PWM on pins 9 and 10. The Arduino core puts it in 8-bit Phase-Correct PWM mode, which counts up to 255 and back down to 0 (510 clock steps per cycle), giving \(16\text{ MHz} / (64 \times 510) \approx 490.20\text{ Hz}\) (listed as 490 Hz). Counting up and then down makes the pulses more symmetrical than Fast PWM.[4][5][3]
  • Timer2 (8-bit): Drives PWM on pins 3 and 11, also in Phase-Correct PWM mode with a prescaler of 64, so it too runs at about 490.20 Hz (490 Hz).[4][5][3]

On the 32-bit Arduino Uno R4 (Renesas RA4M1), analogWrite() uses the same six pins (3, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 11) and runs at 490 Hz by default.[3] Underneath, the RA4M1 contains a General PWM Timer (GPT) block with two 32-bit channels and six 16-bit channels that can count up, down, or up and down, and its CPU runs at up to 48 MHz.[7] Higher PWM frequencies on the R4 require configuring these timers beyond the default analogWrite() settings.

05. DC motor PWM versus RC servo pulse timing

A frequent source of confusion is the difference between PWM for DC motor speed control and the pulse signal used for RC servo position control. Both are trains of rectangular pulses, but they carry different information:[8][3]

Feature DC Motor PWM RC Servomotor PWM
Controlled Variable Average motor voltage, and so motor speed[1] Angular shaft position (common servos move about 90° over the standard pulse range; some are rated for 180°)[8]
Signal Metric Duty cycle (0% to 100%)[1] Absolute pulse width (1.0 ms to 2.0 ms)[8]
Carrier Frequency 490 Hz or 980 Hz on an Uno by default, or ultrasonic (above 20 kHz) with a reconfigured timer[3][9] One pulse every 20 ms (50 Hz)[8][10]
Power Handling The PWM signal switches the motor current through an external H-bridge[11] Signal wire carries control pulses only; power arrives on separate power and ground wires[12]
Internal Driver Requires an external H-bridge (for example L298 or TB6612FNG)[11][13] Motor, gears, position potentiometer and control board with drive transistors built inside the servo[8]

A standard hobby servo contains a small DC motor, a gear train, a potentiometer attached to the output shaft to sense its position, and a small circuit board whose transistors likely form an H-bridge so the motor can turn either way.[8] The servo reads the width of a pulse that repeats every 20 milliseconds (50 Hz):[8][10]

  • 1.0 millisecond pulse: Moves the servo to one end of its standard range.[8]
  • 1.5 millisecond pulse: Moves the servo to the center (neutral) position.[8][10]
  • 2.0 millisecond pulse: Moves the servo to the other end of its standard range.[8]

What pulse width sent at 50 Hz commands a standard hobby servo to its neutral center position? The answer is 1.5 milliseconds (1500 microseconds).[8][10] Real servos differ: some accept wider pulses, while others hit mechanical limits and can be damaged if driven past them.[8] The Arduino Servo library's write(angle) maps 0° to 180° onto a default pulse range of 544 to 2400 microseconds, starts servos at 1500 microseconds, and refreshes them every 20 milliseconds; writeMicroseconds() sets the pulse width directly.[12]

06. The Timer1 conflict: Servo library and pin trade-offs

Robots that combine DC motors and hobby servos run into a timer conflict on the Arduino Uno R3. The Servo library documentation states that on boards other than the Mega, using the library disables analogWrite() PWM on pins 9 and 10, whether or not a servo is attached to those pins.[12]

The reason is the timer. On the ATmega328P the library uses the 16-bit Timer1 to time its servo pulses.[12] Why does calling myServo.attach() on an Arduino Uno R3 disable analogWrite() PWM on pins 9 and 10? Because the Servo library takes over Timer1: it switches the timer to normal counting mode with a prescaler of 8 and uses its output-compare interrupt to end each servo pulse, so Timer1 can no longer produce the PWM outputs on pins 9 and 10.[12][4]

If you try to drive a motor driver with analogWrite(9, speed) while the Servo library is in use, pin 9 will not produce the expected PWM signal. So, when an Uno robot uses servos, put the motor PWM lines on pins 3, 5, 6 or 11, which are driven by Timer0 and Timer2.[12][4]

07. Acoustic whine and ultrasonic modulation

When DC motors are driven at the standard Arduino PWM frequencies (490 Hz or 980 Hz), robots often emit an annoying whine at the PWM frequency.[3][9]

Normal human hearing covers about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz), and the ear is most sensitive between about 2,000 Hz and 5,000 Hz.[14] Carrier frequencies of 490 Hz and 980 Hz sit well inside the audible range. Each current pulse slightly shakes the motor's windings and structure at the PWM frequency, so the motor sounds a tone at that pitch.

Pololu's engineers note that the best way to reduce PWM-related motor whine is an ultrasonic PWM frequency, such as 20 kHz, above the range of human hearing.[9][14][15] Modern drivers support this: the TB6612FNG, for example, is specified for PWM switching frequencies up to 100 kHz.[13]

Ultrasonic PWM has an electrical cost: switching losses. Each time a transistor turns on or off, it passes briefly through a state where both its voltage and its current are non-zero, and it dissipates energy as heat. A common estimate of this loss is:[2]

\[P_{switching} = \frac{1}{2} V_{supply} I_{load} (t_{rise} + t_{fall}) f_{PWM}\]

Because the loss is proportional to \(f_{PWM}\), switching at 25 kHz produces about \(25{,}000 / 500 = 50\) times more switching loss than switching at 500 Hz with the same transistors and load.[2] For a small driver such as the DRV8833, TI notes that conduction (on-resistance) loss usually dominates and switching losses are typically 10% to 30% of it, so check the driver's thermal limits when you raise the frequency.[16]

08. Mechanical dead zones, stiction, and flyback protection

When applying PWM to physical DC motors, two practical constraints must be managed:

  • Mechanical Dead Zone (Stiction): Real gearboxes have static friction, or "stiction." At low duty cycles the motor's torque is too small to overcome the friction of the gears and bearings: current flows and heats the windings, but the shaft does not turn. The threshold differs from motor to motor, so measure it for your own motor (see the Try it box). Robot controllers add a dead zone compensation offset, mapping commands so that the smallest non-zero command jumps straight to the measured breakaway value.
  • Inductive Flyback Voltage Spikes: Motor windings are inductors, so abruptly switching off their current produces a voltage spike. H-bridge circuits must give this current a safe path. The L298 datasheet requires an external bridge of fast-recovery diodes (trr of 200 ns or less) when driving inductive loads.[11] MOSFET drivers such as the DRV8833 instead let the recirculating current flow back to the power supply through the output transistors' body diodes (fast decay) or recirculate it through the shorted motor winding (slow decay).[16]

09. Application case study: precision robotic gripper

This section is an illustrative design, not a description of a particular product. A robotic parallel gripper shows the value of combining PWM with sensor feedback. If a gripper is driven only by an on/off switch, the fingers close at full speed and force, which can crush delicate objects (such as eggs or fruit) or stall and overheat the motor.

With PWM, the robot can run a staged grasping routine:

  1. Approach Phase: The gripper closes at a high duty cycle (e.g., 70% PWM) to minimize cycle time while moving through free air.
  2. Deceleration Phase: Optical distance sensors or tactile switches detect proximity to the target object, prompting the controller to drop PWM to a gentle 20% duty cycle.
  3. Contact and Grip Holding: Current sensors or stall-detecting shaft encoders indicate that finger motion has stopped against the object. The controller applies a continuous holding duty cycle (e.g., 15% PWM), supplying enough torque to keep the object from slipping without overheating the motor windings or crushing the payload.
Try it
  1. In the interactive PWM simulator above, set the supply voltage to 12 V and adjust the duty cycle slider until the average output voltage displays 6.0 V. Calculate the corresponding 8-bit integer value required by Arduino analogWrite().
  2. Connect a standard hobby servomotor to an Arduino board and write a sketch using myServo.writeMicroseconds(). Sweep pulse widths between 1000 and 2000 microseconds in 100-microsecond increments, observing the resulting shaft angles.
  3. Connect a DC gearmotor to an H-bridge driver and slowly ramp the PWM duty cycle from 0 to 255 in steps of 5. Identify the exact threshold value at which the shaft begins turning; this represents your motor's static friction dead zone.
