1.1 Definition of Robots

Gerald Lê

01. A working definition

Everyone has a picture of a robot in their head, but people rarely agree on exactly which machines count. A good place to start is the dictionary. Merriam-Webster’s first sense of “robot” is “a machine that resembles a living creature in being capable of moving independently (as by walking or rolling on wheels) and performing complex actions (such as grasping and moving objects).” It adds that such a machine is often “built to resemble a human being or animal in appearance and behavior.”[1]

The dictionary also gives two meanings that do not depend on looks at all: “a device that automatically performs complicated, often repetitive tasks (as in an industrial assembly line)” and “a mechanism guided by automatic controls.”[1] Encyclopædia Britannica takes the same practical view: a robot is “any automatically operated machine that replaces human effort,” even if it does not look or act like a person. Britannica calls robotics “the engineering discipline dealing with the design, construction, and operation of robots.”[2]

That practical meaning is the one this course cares about most. The six-axis arm welding car frames on an assembly line does not look like a person, but it moves on its own, performs complex actions and is guided by a program. That makes it a robot, and as the next section shows, the international standard agrees.

02. The standard definition: ISO 8373

Dictionaries describe how a word is used. Engineers need something stricter, so that a factory, a regulator and a statistics office all mean the same thing. That job falls to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). Its vocabulary standard, ISO 8373:2021 Robotics: Vocabulary, is the third edition and replaced the 2012 edition.[3] Clause 3.1 defines a robot as a

ISO 8373:2021, clause 3.1

“programmed actuated mechanism with a degree of autonomy (3.2) to perform locomotion, manipulation or positioning”[3]

Each word in that sentence is a test a machine has to pass:

  • Programmed: its behavior comes from a program, not only from its fixed mechanical shape.
  • Actuated: it has actuators, which ISO defines as a “power mechanism that converts electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic or any energy to effect motion of the robot.”
  • Mechanism: it is a physical machine. The notes to clause 3.1 give a manipulator (an arm), a mobile platform and a wearable robot as examples of its mechanical structure, and say that “A robot includes the control system.”
  • A degree of autonomy: it can do at least part of its job on its own (section 04 explains this).
  • Locomotion, manipulation or positioning: it moves itself, handles objects, or places something (a tool or a part) in a chosen position.[3]

The same standard names the main families of robots. An industrial robot (clause 3.6) is an “automatically controlled, reprogrammable multipurpose manipulator, programmable in three or more axes, which can be either fixed in place or fixed to a mobile platform for use in automation applications in an industrial environment” (clause cross-references left out). A service robot (3.7) is a “robot in personal use or professional use that performs useful tasks for humans or equipment.” A medical robot (3.8) is one “intended to be used as medical electrical equipment or medical electrical systems,” and ISO says it is counted as neither an industrial nor a service robot.[3] The International Federation of Robotics (IFR), which publishes the world’s robot statistics, still counts medical robots among service robots, so the two sources split the categories slightly differently.[4][5] Finally, robotics itself (3.10) is the “science and practice of designing, manufacturing, and applying robots.”[3]

The welding arm from section 01 passes every test in clause 3.6: it is automatically controlled, it can be reprogrammed for a new car model, it has six axes and it works in a factory. The definition is not just paperwork either. The IFR says it uses the ISO 8373 definitions when it counts robots for its World Robotics reports.[6][7]

The standard’s own title shows how the field has grown. The 1994 edition was called Manipulating industrial robots: Vocabulary, the 2012 edition Robots and robotic devices: Vocabulary, and the 2021 edition simply Robotics: Vocabulary.[8][9][3] In under thirty years the definition moved from factory arms to all of robotics.

03. Other definitions you will meet

ISO is not the only voice. Older textbooks often quote the Robot Institute of America (RIA), which in 1979 described a robot as “a reprogrammable, multifunctional manipulator designed to move material, parts, tools, or specialized devices” through programmed motions to do a variety of tasks.[10] Researchers who study mobile and intelligent robots tend to define robots by what they do. George Bekey of the University of Southern California writes, “We frequently define a robot as a machine that senses, thinks, and acts.”[11] He also stresses that a robot “is a physical system situated in the real world, and is not only software residing on a computer,” and that it is not a living organism.[12] IEEE Spectrum’s ROBOTS guide gives a similar version: “A robot is an autonomous machine capable of sensing its environment, carrying out computations to make decisions, and performing actions in the real world.”[13]

SourceWhat the definition rests on
Merriam-Webster (sense 1)Moving and acting like a living creature[1]

Notice the pattern. The RIA definition is really a description of an industrial arm, while the newer definitions add autonomy and sensing. Even experts admit the edges are fuzzy. Joseph Engelberger, who founded Unimation, the company behind the first industrial robot, famously said: “I don’t know how to define one, but I know one when I see one!”[13]

04. Autonomy is a matter of degree

Autonomy is the part of the ISO definition that separates a robot from an ordinary machine. ISO 8373 defines it as the “ability to perform intended tasks based on current state and sensing, without human intervention.” A note adds that, for a given application, the degree of autonomy can be judged by “the quality of decision-making” and the “independence from human.”[3] In other words, autonomy is not yes or no. A machine can have a little or a lot.

What about a machine with no autonomy at all, such as an arm that a person drives with a joystick? ISO gives it a separate name: a robotic device, a “mechanism developed with robotic technology, but not fulfilling all characteristics of a robot.” Its examples are a “Teleoperated remote manipulator, haptic device, end-effector, unpowered exoskeleton.”[3] ISO 8373 itself lists a teleoperated (remotely operated) manipulator as an example of a robotic device rather than a robot.[14] Real robots often mix the two modes. ISO notes that a mobile robot (“robot able to travel under its own control”) can also have a way to be remotely controlled, and the IEEE guide says many robots combine remote control with operation that needs no human.[3][13]

Researchers have built scales to describe how much autonomy a robot has. Jenay Beer, A. D. Fisk and W. A. Rogers proposed “categorizing autonomy along a 10-point taxonomy,” with levels “ranging from teleoperation to fully autonomous systems.”[15] For cars, the engineering society SAE International publishes the J3016 standard. Its April 2021 revision has six levels:[16]

  • Level 0: No Driving Automation
  • Level 1: Driver Assistance
  • Level 2: Partial Driving Automation
  • Level 3: Conditional Driving Automation
  • Level 4: High Driving Automation
  • Level 5: Full Driving Automation

SAE assigns a level to the driving feature that is switched on at a given moment, not to the car as a whole, and it leaves out momentary interventions such as automatic emergency braking.[16] That is a useful habit for robots too: ask how autonomous a machine is while doing a particular task.

05. Edge cases: is it a robot?

No definition settles every case, and well-known roboticists disagree. The IEEE ROBOTS guide notes that the sense, compute, act definition “could very well describe a dishwasher, a thermostat, an elevator, an automatic door, and many other systems.” Gill Pratt considers a thermostat a simple robot, because it measures the temperature, checks a schedule and switches the heating or cooling. Rodney Brooks is not keen on counting dishwashers, and the guide leaves cruise control as an open question.[13]

The verdicts below apply the definitions above. They are this lesson’s reasoning, not rulings from ISO, so feel free to argue with them.

  • Dishwasher or automatic door: each one senses, decides and acts, so it passes the sense, think, act test. It struggles with ISO clause 3.1, though, because a dishwasher does not move itself, handle objects or position anything.
  • CNC machine tool: it is programmed, actuated and positions a cutting tool along several axes, which comes close to an industrial robot. The usual difference is that a CNC machine does one kind of job, while clause 3.6 asks for a “multipurpose” manipulator.
  • Drone: the IEEE guide lists drones among robots that “take to the skies.”[13] Under ISO, a drone flown entirely by a pilot looks more like a robotic device, while one that holds its position or flies a route by itself has the autonomy to be a mobile robot.
  • Chatbot or software “bot”: no. It has no actuated mechanism, and Bekey’s point that a robot is “not only software” rules it out.[12]
  • Self-driving car: yes under clause 3.1 and the mobile robot definition, since it is programmed, actuated and moves itself with some autonomy. The SAE levels show how much autonomy it has.

One more distinction is worth keeping. Every robot is a kind of automation, but not every piece of automation is a robot. A fixed conveyor belt or a machine built for a single job is automated but is not reprogrammable or multipurpose, which are the words ISO uses to mark the line.

06. Where the word came from

The word “robot” is borrowed from the Czech word robota, which means “forced labor.”[17] Merriam-Webster gives “robota compulsory labor,” and the Czech Consulate General in Los Angeles explains that the word is strongly linked to serfdom, the unpaid work a serf owed to a lord.[1][18]

The Czech writer Karel Čapek made the word famous in his play R.U.R. (Rossum’s Universal Robots), but he did not invent it. Čapek first thought of calling his artificial workers “labori,” from the Latin labor. His brother Josef Čapek, a painter, suggested “robots” instead. In Karel’s own memory of the moment, “‘Name them Robots,’ the painter muttered with a brush in his mouth.”[18] Karel published this account in the newspaper Lidové noviny on 24 December 1933.[19]

The dates are often mixed up, so here they are in order. R.U.R. was published by Aventinum in November 1920, with a cover by Josef Čapek. An amateur group in Hradec Králové staged it first, on 2 January 1921, and the official premiere was at the National Theatre in Prague on 25 January 1921, where it stayed in the repertoire for six years. It was first performed in English in 1922, when the Theatre Guild opened it at the Garrick Theater in New York on 9 October 1922, and it reached London in 1923.[18] That is why Merriam-Webster dates the first known use of “robot” in English to 1922.[1]

In the play, a scientist named Rossum discovers how to make humanlike workers and builds a factory to produce them. The robots are built to replace humans in hard and dangerous work and are gradually made more human, for example by giving them the ability to feel pain. In the end the robots rise up and take over from humanity.[17][20]

07. Asimov and the word “robotics”

“Robot” came from a play, and “robotics” came from science fiction too. According to the Oxford English Dictionary, as cited by Wikipedia, Isaac Asimov’s short story “Liar!”, published in Astounding Science Fiction in May 1941, contains the first recorded use of the word.[21] His story “Runaround” (March 1942) was the first to state his Three Laws of Robotics outright; its plot turns on a robot called Speedy caught between two of the laws.[22] As printed in I, Robot (1950), the laws are:[23]

  1. “A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.”
  2. “A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.”
  3. “A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.”

Asimov later added a “Zeroth Law” putting humanity as a whole first.[23] The laws are fiction, not engineering rules, but they started a conversation about robot safety and ethics that this course returns to in Ethics in Robotics. So the word “robot” (1920) is about twenty years older than “robotics” (1941), and both came from stories long before real industrial robots existed.

08. What a robot is made of

ISO 8373 also gives standard names for the parts of a robot. You will meet each of them again later in the course.[3]

  • Mechanical structure: the body. For an arm this is the manipulator, “an arrangement of segments, jointed or sliding relative to one another.” The manipulator includes the actuators but not the tool on the end. Other structures are a mobile platform (a wheeled base, also called an automated guided vehicle or AGV) and a wearable robot.
  • Actuators: the motors or cylinders that turn electrical, hydraulic or pneumatic energy into motion.
  • End-effector: the tool attached to the robot so it can do its task, such as a welding torch. A gripper is an end-effector “designed for seizing and holding.”
  • Control system: a “set of hardware and software components implementing logic and power control, and other functions.” ISO counts it as part of the robot.
  • Task program: the instructions for the job. ISO includes a task program in its definition of an industrial robot system.
  • Sensors: ISO builds sensing into the definition of autonomy itself, which depends on the robot’s “current state and sensing.”

On a welding robot, for example, the six-axis arm is the manipulator and the welding torch is the end-effector. Lesson 1.6 looks at this anatomy in more detail.

09. Sense, think, act

A helpful way to picture any robot, from a hobby rover to an industrial arm, is the definition Bekey uses: a machine that senses, thinks and acts.[11] These three parts form a loop:

  • Sense: sensors such as encoders, ultrasonic range finders and cameras measure the robot and its surroundings.
  • Think: a controller (a microcontroller like an Arduino, or an industrial robot controller) runs a program that decides what to do next.
  • Act: actuators such as motors and grippers carry out the decision, which changes what the sensors see on the next pass.

The Electronics and Control chapter follows exactly this loop: PWM is how a program acts on a motor, encoders are how it senses the result, and PID control is how it thinks about the difference.

10. Does a robot always think before it acts?

The loop above makes “think” look like one tidy step. Real robots organize their thinking in different ways, called control architectures. There are three main families.

Sense, plan, act (hierarchical). The robot senses, updates an internal model of the world, makes a plan, carries out the first step and then senses again. This approach dominated early robotics research.[24] The classic example is Shakey, built at the research institute SRI from 1966 to 1972 and the “first mobile robot to reason about its actions.” Its cameras and touch sensors sent data by radio to remote computers, which used search and planning programs (A* and STRIPS) to make a plan and then sent motion commands back to the robot.[25]

Reactive (behavior-based). In 1986 Rodney Brooks argued for a different design, the subsumption architecture. Instead of one long chain from sensing to planning to motor control, he stacked simple behaviors in layers, each giving the robot a new “level of competence.” Level 0 avoids touching objects, level 1 wanders without hitting things, level 2 explores toward distant places and level 3 builds a map and plans routes. In his words, “Higher-level layers can subsume the roles of lower levels by suppressing their outputs,” yet “lower levels continue to function as higher levels are added.”[26] Reactive control links sensing straight to acting with no global world model. It is fast, modular and cheap, but it is hard to give a reactive robot a specific task or a long-range goal.[24]

Hybrid (three-layer). Many robots combine the two. Erann Gat described a common pattern with three layers: a controller that runs fast feedback loops between sensors and actuators, a sequencer that chooses which behavior to run and handles surprises, and a deliberator that does slow planning and search.[27] Gat’s reasoning was that pure planning is too slow for a changing world, while pure reaction trusts sensors that are often unreliable.[27]

You can map this onto the course. The PID loop you will write in lesson 5.4 is the kind of fast feedback loop that lives in Gat’s controller layer, and the state machines of lesson 5.7 do the job of a simple sequencer.

11. Robots by the numbers

Because the IFR counts robots using the ISO definition, its figures show how many machines meet the industrial robot test from section 02.[6][7] According to World Robotics 2026, released on 24 September 2026, factories worldwide installed 603,307 industrial robots in 2025, an increase of 11%. The number in operation grew 9% to 5,079,078, about 5.08 million robots.[28][29] Asia received 76% of the new robots, and China alone installed 354,226 units.[28]

Another way to measure robots is robot density, the number of industrial robots for every 10,000 manufacturing employees. In the IFR’s most recent density figures (2024 data, from World Robotics 2025), the world average was 132. The Republic of Korea led with 1,220, followed by Singapore (818), Germany (449) and Japan (446). The United States had 307 and China 166.[30] Korea’s figure works out to roughly one industrial robot for every eight factory workers.

Try it
  1. Pick a machine you use every day (a robot vacuum, an automatic door, a dishwasher). Write down what it senses, what decides, and what acts.
  2. Test it against the five parts of ISO 8373 clause 3.1 in section 02: programmed, actuated, mechanism, a degree of autonomy, and locomotion, manipulation or positioning. Which tests does it pass?
  3. Compare your verdict with the Merriam-Webster definition. Do the two agree? If not, which one do you find more useful, and why?
