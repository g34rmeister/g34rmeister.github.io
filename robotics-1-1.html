1.1 What Is a Robot?

Gerald Lê Draft

01. A working definition

According to the Merriam-Webster dictionary, a robot is a machine that resembles a living creature in being capable of moving independently (as by walking or rolling on wheels) and performing complex actions (such as grasping and moving objects). Some robots resemble a human being or an animal in appearance and behavior. Many do not: they can also be a device that automatically performs complicated, often repetitive tasks, as on an industrial assembly line, or a mechanism guided by automatic controls.

That second meaning is the one this course cares about most. The six-axis arm welding car frames on an assembly line does not look like a person, but it moves on its own, performs complex actions and is guided by a program. That makes it a robot.

02. Where the word came from

The word “robot” is borrowed from the Czech word robota, which means “forced labor.” It was made famous by the Czech writer Karel Čapek in his 1920 play R.U.R. (Rossum’s Universal Robots), which was first performed in English in 1922. In the play, robots are built to replace humans in difficult and dangerous work, but they turn out to be resentful and rebellious, much like people would be in their place.

One thing is clear, however: robots are not humans. They do not eat, breathe or reproduce in the way living things do. What they share with living things is the ability to take in information about the world and act on it.

03. Sense, think, act

A helpful way to picture any robot, from a hobby rover to an industrial arm, is as a loop with three parts:

  • Sense: sensors such as encoders, ultrasonic range finders and cameras measure the robot and its surroundings.
  • Think: a controller (a microcontroller like an Arduino, or an industrial robot controller) runs a program that decides what to do next.
  • Act: actuators such as motors and grippers carry out the decision, which changes what the sensors see on the next pass.

Chapter 2 of this course follows exactly this loop: PWM is how a program acts on a motor, encoders are how it senses the result, and PID control is how it thinks about the difference.
