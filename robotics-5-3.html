5.3 Encoders and Shaft Encoders

Gerald Lê

01. Closing the loop: sensing motion and displacement

When a robotics engineer writes a program commanding a mobile robot to drive forward for two seconds, or instructs a robotic arm joint to power on for 500 milliseconds, the system is operating in an open-loop configuration. The microcontroller has commanded electrical power to the actuators, but it possesses zero sensory confirmation of what physical movement actually occurred. If the battery voltage drops, if the floor surface transitions from slick vinyl to thick carpet, or if the wheels encounter a mechanical obstacle, the robot's physical displacement will deviate drastically from the commanded trajectory.[1]

To achieve deterministic precision, robotic systems require closed-loop feedback control.[2] The fundamental sensory component providing this feedback is the rotary encoder (commonly termed a shaft encoder). Mounted directly to motor armatures or wheel axles, an encoder converts mechanical angular displacement into a stream of digital electrical signals. Encoders provide the foundational proprioceptive data that enables mobile robots to track odometry, empowers multi-axis manipulators to hold millimeter-accurate poses, and allows velocity controllers to stabilize speeds under dynamic mechanical payloads.[1][2]

02. Incremental versus absolute encoders

Rotary encoders fall into two overarching architectural categories: incremental and absolute.[1][3] The selection between them determines how a robotic system initializes and recovers from electrical power interruptions:

  • Incremental Encoders: Incremental encoders generate repetitive digital square-wave pulses as the shaft rotates. The microcontroller counts these pulses to compute relative displacement from an arbitrary starting location. Incremental encoders are mechanically simple, low-cost, and capable of extremely high rotational speeds. However, they possess no non-volatile memory of their position: what happens to an incremental encoder's position when the power goes off? It is forgotten, so the robot has to home again. When powered on, an incremental system must execute a mechanical calibration routine, driving its joints until physical limit switches or an optical index pulse (Channel Z) are triggered to re-establish a global coordinate origin.[1]
  • Absolute Encoders: Absolute encoders output a unique digital binary or Gray code value for every distinct angular position across the shaft's 360-degree rotation. The controller queries the encoder over a digital serial bus and receives the exact physical shaft angle instantaneously upon bootup, with zero motion required. Absolute encoders eliminate the need for homing routines entirely.[3]

Absolute encoders are further classified into single-turn and multi-turn devices.[3] Single-turn encoders resolve position within one 360-degree revolution (standard industrial resolutions range from 13-bit to 24-bit, or 8,192 to 16,777,216 distinct angular increments per turn). Multi-turn absolute encoders incorporate internal miniature gear trains or non-volatile magnetic Wiegand-effect wire sensors, tracking both the precise sub-degree angle and the exact count of full revolutions (typically up to 4,096 or 65,536 turns) even if the shaft rotates while main system power is completely disconnected.[3]

03. Quadrature decoding: direction and resolution

If an incremental encoder possesses only a single optical pulse channel (Channel A), the controller can calculate rotational speed by measuring the pulse frequency, but it cannot determine the direction of rotation. Clockwise rotation and counter-clockwise rotation produce identical pulse trains.[1][4]

To solve this, quadrature encoders incorporate two distinct sensing channels, labeled Channel A and Channel B, physically arranged so their electrical square waves are offset by exactly 90 degrees of electrical phase (one quarter of a full cycle).[1][4]

By observing which channel transitions first, the microcontroller determines directional polarity:[4]

  • Clockwise (Forward) Rotation: Channel A leads Channel B by 90 degrees. When Channel A experiences a rising edge (transition from LOW to HIGH), Channel B is currently in a LOW state.
  • Counter-Clockwise (Reverse) Rotation: Channel B leads Channel A by 90 degrees. When Channel A experiences a rising edge, Channel B is already in a HIGH state.

The resolution extracted from a quadrature encoder depends on the software decoding mode implemented in the microcontroller:[1][4]

  • 1x Decoding: The microcontroller triggers an interrupt only on the rising edges of Channel A. The number of registered counts per revolution (CPR) equals the physical Pulses Per Revolution (PPR) of the encoder: \(\text{CPR} = \text{PPR} \times 1\).[1]
  • 2x Decoding: The microcontroller counts both the rising edges and falling edges of Channel A: \(\text{CPR} = \text{PPR} \times 2\).[1]
  • 4x Decoding (Full Quadrature): The microcontroller counts every state transition (both rising and falling edges) across BOTH Channel A and Channel B. Because each complete electrical period contains four discrete state transitions (00 → 10 → 11 → 01 → 00), 4x decoding quadruples the effective angular resolution:[1][4]

\[\text{CPR} = \text{PPR} \times 4\]

Consider a practical design problem: a quadrature encoder is rated at 500 PPR. With 4x decoding, how many counts per revolution does the code see? Using the quadrature multiplier, the calculation yields:[1]

\[\text{CPR} = 500 \times 4 = 2000\text{ counts per revolution}\]

Terminology across commercial datasheets requires careful scrutiny. In standard definitions, PPR denotes Pulses Per Revolution (electrical cycles on one channel), while CPR denotes Counts Per Revolution (the decoded transitions, which is \(4 \times \text{PPR}\) under 4x decoding). However, several major manufacturers use "CPR" to mean Cycles Per Revolution, setting CPR equal to PPR. If an engineer assumes a 1,000 "CPR" encoder yields 1,000 counts per turn, but the software runs full 4x decoding, the calculated robot travel distance will be off by an exact factor of four. Engineers should always physically rotate a newly mounted wheel exactly one full turn by hand while logging raw count increments to verify the true multiplier.[1]

05. Physical sensing technologies

How an encoder physically detects shaft movement dictates its environmental durability and operating envelope:[5]

Transducer Technology Physical Operating Principle Engineering Trade-Offs
Optical Encoders An infrared LED projects light through a patterned glass or etched metal disk featuring microscopic radial lines onto a matched photodiode array.[5] Highest angular resolution and precision. However, glass disks are fragile under heavy shock, and contamination from oil, airborne dust, or condensation will blind the optical path.[5]
Magnetic Encoders A diametrically magnetized multi-pole permanent magnet wheel spins adjacent to a Hall-effect or Giant Magnetoresistive (GMR) sensor IC.[5] Extremely rugged, completely sealed against moisture, dust, and cutting fluid. However, vulnerable to strong external magnetic fields from high-power motor windings or dynamic brake coils.[5]
Capacitive Encoders An asymmetric conductive rotor pattern modulates high-frequency electrical capacitance between transmitter and receiver PCB stators.[5] Impervious to airborne dust, oil, and magnetic fields. Low current consumption, high temperature tolerance, and highly reliable for industrial field robotics.[5]

06. Kinematic calculations: from ticks to linear distance and velocity

In mobile robotics, encoders convert raw integer count increments (“ticks”) into physical SI units of linear displacement and angular velocity.[2]

Given a wheel of outer diameter \(d\) (in meters) and an encoder with a verified resolution of \(\text{CPR}\) counts per revolution, the physical distance traveled by the wheel contact patch is:[2]

\[\text{Distance } s = \left(\frac{\text{ticks}}{\text{CPR}}\right) \times (\pi \cdot d)\]

To calculate wheel rotational velocity in discrete-time control loops running at a fixed sampling interval \(\Delta t\) seconds, the change in encoder counts \(\Delta\text{ticks} = \text{ticks}_{curr} - \text{ticks}_{prev}\) is converted to angular velocity \(\omega\) in radians per second:[2]

\[\omega = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}}{\text{CPR} \times \Delta t}\right) \times 2\pi\]

The linear velocity \(v\) of the wheel rim in meters per second is then directly determined by multiplying angular velocity by the wheel radius \(r = d / 2\):[2]

\[v = \omega \cdot r = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}}{\text{CPR} \times \Delta t}\right) \times (\pi \cdot d)\]

07. Microcontroller decoding: interrupts and state tables

In real robotic hardware, motor armatures spin at thousands of revolutions per minute. Consider a DC gearmotor running at 3,000 RPM whose rear armature shaft carries a modest 500 PPR encoder running full 4x decoding. The resulting pulse transition frequency is:[4][6]

\[f_{interrupt} = \left(\frac{3000\text{ RPM}}{60\text{ s}}\right) \times 500\text{ PPR} \times 4 = 100,000\text{ transitions/sec (100 kHz)}\]

If a programmer attempts to sample encoder pins inside an Arduino loop() function using digitalRead(), the execution loop (which takes hundreds of microseconds per pass) will miss tens of thousands of pulses, causing disastrous positioning drift.[6]

Microcontrollers must process encoder transitions using hardware interrupts.[6] When an electrical transition occurs on an interrupt-enabled pin, the microcontroller CPU immediately freezes its main execution thread, jumps to an Interrupt Service Routine (ISR) to increment or decrement the count variable, and resumes execution within microseconds:[6] 

volatile long encoderCount = 0;
const byte pinA = 2;
const byte pinB = 3;

void IRAM_ATTR encoderISR() {
    // Read current state of both channels
    byte a = digitalRead(pinA);
    byte b = digitalRead(pinB);
    if (a == b) {
        encoderCount++;
    } else {
        encoderCount--;
    }
}

void setup() {
    pinMode(pinA, INPUT_PULLUP);
    pinMode(pinB, INPUT_PULLUP);
    attachInterrupt(digitalPinToInterrupt(pinA), encoderISR, CHANGE);
}

On an Arduino Uno R3, external hardware interrupts via attachInterrupt() are legally restricted to pins 2 and 3 only.[6] On a two-wheeled differential robot, only one channel from each wheel's encoder can be connected to hardware interrupts, restricting the Uno R3 to 1x or 2x decoding unless complex Pin Change Interrupts (PCINT) are configured. In contrast, modern 32-bit microcontrollers such as the Arduino Uno R4 (RA4M1) feature dedicated hardware Quadrature Encoder Interface (QEI) timers. The hardware timers automatically decode quadrature pulses and update an internal 32-bit hardware register with zero CPU interrupt overhead.[7]

08. Absolute encoders in industrial robotics: multi-axis arms and serial protocols

In 6-axis industrial articulated arms (such as those manufactured by ABB, FANUC, KUKA, and Universal Robots), joint feedback is handled almost exclusively by high-resolution multi-turn absolute encoders.[3][8]

Why do 6-axis industrial robot arms use absolute multi-turn encoders on their joints rather than incremental encoders? Because absolute encoders allow the arm to know its exact physical pose immediately on power-on without requiring dangerous homing movements through the cell.[3]

Consider an industrial robot cell where an arm holds a 200-kilogram welding fixture inside an automobile chassis. If incremental encoders were employed, any emergency stop, safety gate breach, or factory power outage would erase joint coordinates. To re-home, the massive robot would have to blindly swing its axes until each joint struck a homing limit switch, destroying the car body and ripping tooling fixtures apart. With absolute encoders, the controller reads the exact angular position of every joint within microseconds of power restoration, allowing safe resumption of production.[3]

Industrial absolute encoders communicate with motion controllers over high-speed, noise-immune digital serial fieldbus protocols:[3][8]

  • BiSS-C (Bidirectional Synchronous Serial): An open-source, point-to-point digital protocol operating over RS-422 differential pairs at clock rates up to 10 MHz. BiSS-C provides cyclic position transmission with embedded Cyclic Redundancy Checks (CRC) and internal sensor health telemetry.[3]
  • SSI (Synchronous Serial Interface): A legacy industrial master-slave protocol that streams position bits synchronously on clock edges, widely used in factory automation.[3]
  • EnDat 2.2 (Heidenhain): A proprietary high-speed bidirectional interface capable of transferring absolute position data along with encoder diagnostics, electronic calibration datasheets, and temperature monitoring.[8]

09. Engineering pitfalls: backlash, frequency limits, and electrical noise

Integrating encoders into dynamic robotic hardware requires avoiding several classic failure modes:[1][2]

  • Gearbox Backlash: Mounting an encoder directly to the high-speed armature shaft of a DC gearmotor provides excellent resolution, but the encoder cannot see mechanical play ("backlash") in the planetary or spur gear teeth. When the motor reverses direction, the motor shaft turns several degrees before the gearbox teeth re-engage the output wheel, introducing unobservable deadband in position control. When sub-millimeter precision is required, encoders must be mounted on the final output shaft or load side.
  • Processor Interrupt Saturation: Specifying an encoder with excessively high PPR on a high-speed shaft can trigger interrupts faster than an 8-bit microcontroller can execute its ISR. The CPU spends 100% of its execution time inside the interrupt handler, starving the main loop and causing the robot to lock up.
  • Electromagnetic Interference (EMI): Encoder signals are high-speed, low-voltage digital pulses. Routing unshielded encoder wires parallel to high-current PWM motor cables induces capacitive noise spikes, causing false counts. Encoders must use shielded, twisted-pair cabling with the cable shield grounded at a single chassis point.
