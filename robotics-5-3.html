      5.3 Robots and Society

      Gerald Lê
      Chapter 5, lesson 3 of 5 ← Previous: 5.2 Ethics in Robotics (Hardware, Software) Next: 5.4 Making a Robot Cell Safe →

      01. Beyond the factory: how robots affect society

      Robots no longer stay locked inside factory cages. Mobile robots haul pallets across warehouses, robot vacuums map living rooms, and surgical robots assist doctors inside operating rooms. When robots move into everyday workplaces and homes, your engineering choices affect whole communities, not just a single operator.

      The scale is already huge. In 2025, factories worldwide installed more than 600,000 new industrial robots, according to the International Federation of Robotics (IFR).[1] By that year, a record 5 million industrial robots were operating globally.[1]

      Earlier lessons looked at engineering ethics and machine safety inside a factory work cell. Once robots enter wider society, three bigger questions come up: how do robots change jobs and wages? How do you protect privacy when moving robots carry cameras and laser sensors (lidar)? And when an autonomous machine makes a harmful mistake, who is responsible? This lesson looks at the real-world evidence and the rules that help you design robots responsibly.

      02. What the data says about robots and jobs

      Public debates about robots often swing between two extremes. One side promises effortless abundance, while the other warns of mass unemployment. Labor economists try to replace that guesswork with real measurements.[2]

      Daron Acemoglu at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and Pascual Restrepo at Boston University ran a landmark study on how industrial robots affect jobs in the United States (US), published in 2020.[2] They compared US local job markets (commuting zones) from 1990 to 2007.[2] Some areas had industries that added many robots, while other areas added few. Before 1990, those areas did not show different job trends.[2] After 1990, the effect of robots stood out from the effect of other machines and technologies.[2]

      In those local US job markets, adding one more robot per thousand workers reduced the share of people with jobs (the employment-to-population ratio) by 0.2 percentage points and lowered wages by 0.42%.[2]

      Keep in mind what headline numbers like these show. They are averages across many regions. They describe local job markets with heavy robot exposure, not every individual worker or every country.

      For the curious: Study method and earlier working-paper numbers

      Acemoglu and Restrepo published the 2020 study in the Journal of Political Economy.[2] They measured each area's exposure to robots from industry-level advances in robotics and each area's mix of industries.[2] Earlier, their 2017 working paper estimated a range of 0.18 to 0.34 percentage points for employment and 0.25 to 0.5 percent for wages.[3] That working paper's summary described one new robot arriving in a local labor market as coinciding with an employment drop of 5.6 workers.[3]

      03. How automation removes, grows, and creates tasks

      A robot rarely replaces an entire human job all at once. Instead, a factory automates specific tasks, such as spot-welding a car frame. Think of making a product as a list of tasks: machining parts, routing circuit boards, scheduling deliveries, and fixing errors. Either a person or a machine can do each task.[4]

      To explain why automation pulls employment in different directions, Acemoglu and Restrepo built a task-based framework.[4] Instead of treating production as an abstract mix of “capital” and “labor,” their framework tracks individual tasks.[4]

      As automation improves, it pushes on employment through three competing effects:[4]

      • 1. The displacement effect: Machines take over tasks that people used to do. For example, replacing human spot-welders with robot arms removes the welding task from workers. Automation always shrinks workers' share of value added (the net economic output left after subtracting material costs).[4] It can also lower demand for workers even while raising output.[4]
      • 2. The productivity effect: Automation cuts costs and raises productivity. That growth can increase demand for workers in the remaining tasks that are not automated.[4]
      • 3. The reinstatement effect: New technology creates new, complex tasks and jobs in which humans have a comparative advantage (where people outperform machines).[4] New tasks always raise workers' share of output and increase labor demand.[4]

      Robotics itself creates many of these reinstated tasks. People are needed to integrate robot cells, program robots offline in simulation, maintain and troubleshoot hardware, and check that cells meet safety standards. Whether automation helps or hurts overall employment depends on how those three effects balance out.

      For the curious: Why US job growth slowed over three decades

      Acemoglu and Restrepo published the task-based framework in the Journal of Economic Perspectives.[4] They used it on US data to explain slower employment growth over the last three decades.[4] Their breakdown points to three causes: a faster displacement effect (especially in manufacturing), a weaker reinstatement effect, and slower productivity growth than in earlier decades.[4]

      04. Where robots are used most: global robot density

      A large country with many factories naturally buys more robots than a small country. To compare automation fairly between countries, you need to compare robot counts against the size of the manufacturing workforce. That ratio shows where robots are actually concentrated.

      The IFR tracks the scale of industrial automation each year in its World Robotics reports. In 2025, new installations grew 11% to more than 600,000 units.[1] The total number of operating industrial robots grew 9% to a record 5 million units.[1]

      To compare how heavily countries automate, the IFR measures robot density: the number of working industrial robots per 10,000 employees in manufacturing. In 2023, the global average reached a record 162, though individual countries varied widely:[5]

      Country Robot density in 2023 (robots per 10,000 manufacturing employees)
      Republic of Korea 1,012
      Singapore 770
      China 470
      Germany 429
      Japan 419
      United States 295

      Source: IFR, World Robotics 2024.[5] China's robot density jumped from 402 in 2022 to 470 in 2023, passing Germany and Japan.[5] The IFR credits this jump to China's push to use automation technology.[5]

      Density figures show where robots are used most. On their own, however, they cannot tell you what robots do to jobs and wages. Answering that question takes studies like Acemoglu and Restrepo's, which compare areas with different robot exposure over time.[2]

      05. How jobs change and how to help workers adapt

      Automation does not simply wipe out jobs or leave them untouched. Most often, it reshapes what workers do during the day. If you build or deploy robots, you can see which jobs face the biggest shifts and how teams can support the people affected.

      In 2018, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) studied how automation could affect jobs across 32 member countries.[6][7] Using data from its Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC), the OECD estimated that:[6][7]

      • About 14% of jobs are at high risk: These jobs have a probability of automation above 70%.[7]
      • A further 32% are likely to change significantly: These jobs have a probability of automation between 50% and 70%, so many of their tasks could change even if the job itself stays.[7][6]

      So far, automation has mainly caused labor market polarization rather than the disappearance of work. Economist David Autor tracked wage patterns in the United States over recent decades.[8] He found that wage gains went disproportionately to workers at the top and bottom of the skill and income ladder, not to those in the middle.[8]

      Computers replace routine, rule-based tasks most easily. At the same time, they boost the advantage of workers who bring problem-solving skills, adaptability, and creativity. Autor also argues that this polarization is unlikely to continue very far into the future.[8]

      Easing the human cost of this transition takes steady investment in apprenticeships, retraining, and adult education. As an engineer, you can help too. A robot cell that operators can retool, troubleshoot, and reprogram creates skilled work around the robot instead of only removing tasks. For example, teams can support this work with lead-through teaching or simple graphical programming.

      06. Privacy risks: robots see and map private spaces

      In regular software, privacy risks mostly come from cookies, search histories, and online purchases. Robots bring privacy risks straight into physical rooms. To navigate safely, a mobile robot has to see and remember the spaces around it.[9]

      A mobile robot is essentially a bundle of sensors on wheels. To find its way, avoid crashes, and build maps, it may collect:

      • Color (red, green, and blue, or RGB) and depth cameras: Pictures of living rooms, office desks, whiteboards, and people going about their lives, as the Roomba case below shows.[9]
      • Lidar point clouds and maps: Detailed 3D geometry of rooms and furniture, which reveals the layout of a home or facility.
      • Microphone arrays: Audio of the room, recorded to pick up voice commands.

      A December 2022 report showed how serious these risks can get.[9] MIT Technology Review reported that development versions of iRobot's Roomba J7 robot vacuum had taken intimate photos inside homes in 2020.[9] One photo showed a woman on the toilet.[9]

      Those images were sent to Scale AI, where contract workers in Venezuela labeled them to train the robot's artificial intelligence (AI).[9] Some workers shared screenshots in private groups on Facebook, Discord, and other platforms.[9] iRobot stated that the photos came from special development robots given to paid data collectors and employees.[9] Those testers had signed written agreements noting that video and other data would be sent back for training.[9]

      This case shows that robot data can travel through long supply chains across multiple companies. Even when the person who owns or tests a robot signs an agreement, other people who appear in the images have not.[9] Contractors and cloud pipelines can expose private moments far from where they were recorded.[9]

      07. Designing for privacy: keep less data, process on the robot

      The strongest defense against leaks is data minimization. Imagine a robot vacuum's camera trying to dodge a shoe on the floor. You do not need to save or upload a photo of the whole living room to steer around it. Instead, you collect only what the task needs, process it on the robot when possible, and keep it no longer than necessary.[10] You must also tell people clearly what is recorded.[10]

      Both privacy law and engineering ethics back up this approach. The European Union (EU) General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), Europe's main data privacy law, requires that personal data be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.”[10] The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Code of Ethics also lists protecting the privacy of others as a duty.[11]

      Four technical design patterns turn data minimization into working software and hardware:

      • On-device edge computing: Run vision models and mapping on the robot's own onboard computer. Once the robot extracts what it needs (such as an obstacle's position), delete the raw camera frame instead of storing or uploading it.
      • Features instead of raw pixels: Send compact summaries—such as occupancy-grid maps or obstacle coordinates—to remote servers instead of streaming raw images.
      • Hardware privacy controls: Put physical lens covers and microphone disconnect switches on consumer and healthcare robots so users can see that recording is impossible.
      • Short-lived, protected logs: Keep diagnostic logs only for a limited time, encrypt them, and strip personal information before sending them anywhere.
      For the curious: The GDPR legal clause behind data minimization

      In the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the data minimization rule appears in Article 5(1)(c).[10]

      08. Who is responsible when an autonomous robot fails?

      When a human-controlled machine hurts someone, it is usually fairly clear who made the mistake. When an autonomous system causes harm, responsibility is spread across many people.[12] That group includes the perception software engineer, the people who prepared the training data, the systems integrator, the company running the system, and the operator on shift.[12]

      Think of the crumple zone on the front of a car, which is built to absorb the force of a crash. In 2019, Madeleine Clare Elish of the Data & Society Research Institute introduced the idea of a moral crumple zone.[12] It describes how blame for a failure can get pinned unfairly on a human operator who had only limited control over an automated system.[12] When the wider system breaks down, the operator ends up absorbing the moral and legal blame.[12]

      Elish drew this idea from major accidents involving complex automated systems. Her cases include the Three Mile Island nuclear accident, the loss of Air France flight 447, and the 2018 crash of an Uber self-driving test vehicle in Tempe, Arizona.[12] In accidents like these, operators tend to get blamed for failing to step in on time. That happens even though people are poorly suited to watching an automated system passively for long stretches and taking over at a moment's notice.

      To avoid creating moral crumple zones, you need to design human oversight on purpose. The EU's High-Level Expert Group on AI names three ways to keep people in control:[13]

      • Human-in-the-loop (HITL): A person takes part in every decision cycle. For example, a surgical robot proposes a cut, and the surgeon must confirm it before the robot acts.[13]
      • Human-on-the-loop (HOTL): The system acts on its own. A person can intervene in its design and monitor its operation.[13]
      • Human-in-command (HIC): People oversee the system's overall activity and wider impact. They decide when and how it is used, including deciding not to use it.[13]

      09. Five questions to ask before you build

      Before you place an autonomous robot into a shared workspace, a public street, or a home, step back and check the big picture. Standards and laws give you a checklist so you do not overlook job impacts, privacy risks, or unclear accountability.

      Two frameworks help organize that thinking. IEEE 7000 is an engineering standard that provides a process for gathering stakeholders' values and tracing them into system requirements.[14] In Europe, the EU AI Act regulates AI by risk.[15] It bans some AI practices outright, sets rules for high-risk systems (risk management, technical documentation, record-keeping, and human oversight), and adds transparency duties for certain other AI systems.[15] A 2026 update also moves AI inside machinery, such as industrial robots, under the EU Machinery Regulation.[16]

      Before deploying an autonomous robot into a shared workspace, a public space, or a home, ask five questions:

      • 1. People: Whose daily tasks and livelihoods will change because of this robot? How will they learn new tasks, and what career paths will they have?
      • 2. Data: What does the robot sense and store? Is all of it needed? Can it be processed on the robot? Where does it go and who can see it?
      • 3. Oversight: Can a person understand what the robot is about to do, and stop or override it in time?
      • 4. Responsibility: If the robot causes damage or injury, who is accountable? Are its decisions logged so that someone can find out what happened?
      • 5. Rules: Which safety standards (such as ISO 10218 and ISO 13849) and laws (such as the EU Machinery Regulation and the EU AI Act) apply where it will be used? (See 5.2.)
      For the curious: Standard edition and EU regulation numbers

      The full identifier for the IEEE values-in-design standard is IEEE 7000-2021.[14] The EU AI Act is officially Regulation (EU) 2024/1689.[15] In 2026, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 moved AI in machinery (listed in Annex I, Section B of the AI Act) under the EU Machinery Regulation, which is set to add the AI requirements through delegated acts by 2 August 2028.[16]

      Try it
      1. Pick a commercial autonomous mobile robot (such as an automated hospital delivery bot or a warehouse order picker) and check its sensor suite against GDPR data minimization rules.
      2. Break down an automated factory upgrade using the task framework: list two manual tasks that robots replace (displacement), one non-automated task that grows (productivity), and two new technical tasks for workers (reinstatement).
      3. Draft a design proposal for an autonomous drone delivery system that removes the “moral crumple zone.” Define clear handoffs between the flight software and the remote human dispatchers, and list what gets logged at each handoff.
