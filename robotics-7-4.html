7.4 3D Sensing

Gerald Lê

01. Direct 3D sensing versus 2D inference

In 2.8 Vision-Guided Picking, we compared 2D and 3D guidance. Planar 2D vision works effectively when workpieces lie flat on a known, flat surface at a constant height. However, modern factory automation increasingly demands handling parts jumbled inside deep storage totes, overlapping boxes on pallets, or unoriented castings in shipping containers. In these unstructured environments, cameras must measure not only horizontal and vertical position, but also true physical depth and three-dimensional spatial orientation.

Industrial automation divides three-dimensional perception into two distinct technological approaches: direct physical sensing and learned visual inference. Direct 3D sensing resolves range in hardware, using LiDAR pulses for optical precision or radar waves to penetrate dust, rain, and fog.[1] These sensors measure distance directly, either by timing an emitted signal or by triangulation.

Structured light and stereo vision qualify as 3D sensing because they calculate depth through deterministic geometry from a calibrated physical baseline, whereas neural network inference predicts 3D structure from 2D images without geometric anchors.[1] When an algorithm infers depth from a single 2D image, it guesses distance based on learned patterns like shading and perspective cues. That can work well on scenes like the ones it was trained on, but an unfamiliar object or lighting change can throw the estimate off. Direct sensing computes distance from a known, calibrated geometry instead.

02. Stereo vision principles and disparity calculation

Stereo vision mimics biological depth perception by mounting two calibrated cameras separated by a fixed horizontal distance, known as the physical baseline. When both cameras capture an identical object, the object appears at slightly different horizontal pixel positions in the left and right sensor frames. This horizontal displacement between corresponding image points is termed disparity.

Disparity and depth are linked by simple geometry. In stereo vision triangulation, depth Z is inversely proportional to disparity according to the relationship disparity equals baseline B times focal length f divided by depth Z.[2] Expressed algebraically: 

disparity = (baseline * f) / depth
depth = (baseline * f) / disparity

When a part is situated very close to the stereo camera, the horizontal shift between the two camera views is large, resulting in a large disparity value. As the part moves farther away toward infinity, the light rays entering the lenses become nearly parallel, and disparity approaches zero.

To generate a complete depth map, stereo matching algorithms compare rectangular pixel blocks across scanlines in the left and right images. However, matching fails if a surface is completely smooth and featureless, such as unpainted polished sheet metal. In OpenCV stereo block matching, texture_threshold discards regions that lack sufficient texture to support reliable correspondence calculation.[2] Leaving those regions empty is better than filling them with guessed depths.

System designers must balance measurement depth range against computational execution time. Increasing the number of disparities in stereo matching expands the range of visible scene depths at the expense of requiring more computation.[2] Searching across more disparity steps enables the vision system to reconstruct objects that sit both very close to and far from the sensor. However, every additional disparity level requires more pixel matching calculations, increasing latency on the robot controller.

03. Point cloud filtering: pass-through, voxel grids, and outlier removal

The primary output of direct 3D sensing devices—whether stereo cameras, structured light projectors, or laser line scanners—is a point cloud. A point cloud is a set of points in 3D space, each an (x, y, z) coordinate on a surface the sensor saw. Before these spatial points can guide a robot manipulator, the raw point cloud must be conditioned using specialized software libraries such as the Point Cloud Library (PCL).

The first conditioning stage removes points outside the robot work volume. Cameras in a cell also see fences, the floor and other equipment. The PCL PassThrough filter discards point cloud data whose coordinates along a specified axis fall outside user-defined minimum and maximum limits.[3] By specifying strict spatial bounds along the X, Y, and Z axes, the vision software trims away irrelevant background structures and isolates only the bin or conveyor zone where parts arrive.

A 3D camera can return a point for nearly every pixel, so one frame can hold hundreds of thousands of points, and every later step slows down with that many. The PCL VoxelGrid filter downsamples point clouds by partitioning 3D space into voxel boxes and replacing all points inside each box with their centroid.[4] A voxel represents a volumetric cube in 3D space. By calculating the center of mass of all points inside each voxel cube, the filter preserves the underlying physical shape of the object while cutting the number of points.

The scale of spatial reduction is controlled by configuring the physical size of each voxel element. In the PCL VoxelGrid filter, the setLeafSize method configures the physical length, width, and height of the 3D voxel boxes used for spatial downsampling.[4] Setting a leaf size of 0.01 meters, for example, creates 1 cm cubic bins, producing an evenly spaced, lightweight point cloud suitable for fast spatial calculations.

Reflections, dust and sensor noise add stray points that float above or below actual workpiece surfaces. PCL StatisticalOutlierRemoval computes the mean distance from each point to its neighbors and trims points falling outside an interval defined by the global mean and standard deviation.[5] The algorithm models point neighbor distances as a Gaussian distribution. Any point whose average neighbor distance exceeds the normal threshold is identified as an isolated noise artifact and discarded.

Denoising behavior is configured through two core parameters. In PCL StatisticalOutlierRemoval, setMeanK sets the neighborhood sample size and setStddevMulThresh sets the standard deviation threshold for removing noise.[5] Increasing setMeanK evaluates a wider local neighborhood, while adjusting the standard deviation multiplier controls how aggressively isolated points are pruned from the dataset.

04. Planar segmentation and table surface extraction

In many industrial pick-and-place tasks, workpieces rest on top of a flat table, conveyor belt, or bin bottom. Before an algorithm can isolate individual parts, it must separate the supporting planar surface from the objects lying on top of it.

PCL finds the plane with Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC). In PCL SACSegmentation, setModelType specifies geometric shapes like SACMODEL_PLANE while setDistanceThreshold defines how close points must be to the model to qualify as inliers.[6] RANSAC iteratively selects random points, estimates mathematical plane coefficients, and counts how many points fall within the defined distance threshold.

Once the dominant table plane is determined, its inlier points are extracted and subtracted from the scene. Removing the supporting plane leaves an isolated cloud containing only the elevated workpieces. Without this step, parts resting on the table would all be joined to it and to each other in the clustering step. 

// C++ snippet demonstrating planar RANSAC segmentation in PCL
pcl::SACSegmentation<pcl::PointXYZ> seg;
pcl::PointIndices::Ptr inliers(new pcl::PointIndices);
pcl::ModelCoefficients::Ptr coefficients(new pcl::ModelCoefficients);

seg.setOptimizeCoefficients(true);
seg.setModelType(pcl::SACMODEL_PLANE);
seg.setMethodType(pcl::SAC_RANSAC);
seg.setDistanceThreshold(0.01); // 1 cm distance threshold

seg.setInputCloud(filtered_cloud);
seg.segment(*inliers, *coefficients);

05. Euclidean cluster extraction for object segmentation

After removing supporting table planes and noise artifacts, the remaining point cloud contains points belonging to multiple separate parts. The vision pipeline must segment these unorganized points into distinct clusters, where each cluster corresponds to one physical object.

To partition 3D space, PCL provides spatial clustering algorithms. Euclidean cluster extraction segments unorganized point clouds into distinct object clusters using nearest-neighbor searches structured similar to a flood fill algorithm.[7] It continuously expands the cluster until no further neighbors are found within the defined radius, so each separate item ends up in its own cluster.

Cluster extraction behavior is controlled by distance and volume bounds. In PCL EuclideanClusterExtraction, setClusterTolerance defines spatial proximity for grouping points while setMinClusterSize and setMaxClusterSize discard clusters outside valid point count bounds.[7] If two parts are separated by a physical gap larger than the cluster tolerance, they are recognized as distinct items. Furthermore, rejecting clusters that have fewer points than setMinClusterSize eliminates remaining sensor noise, while capping setMaxClusterSize keeps large leftover structures from being treated as parts.

06. Industrial bin picking and piece picking requirements

In picking, the goal of 3D vision is a pick pose the robot can reach without collisions. Vision guidance systems use 3D point clouds for object segmentation and robot picking pose estimation via CAD model matching or geometry matching.[8] By aligning a pre-loaded CAD model against an extracted cluster or analyzing local surface geometry, the vision software chooses a grasp point and reports the target's 6-DoF pose (X, Y, Z, Roll, Pitch, Yaw) to the robot controller.

Production demands impose strict timing constraints on 3D perception pipelines. In industrial random bin picking, robot cycles generally range from 10 to 15 seconds, allowing camera time budgets between 700 ms and 1500 ms.[8] The longer cycle, with the arm reaching into a deep bin and clearing its walls, gives the 3D vision system up to 1.5 seconds to capture high-density scans and compute collision-free grasp trajectories.

Warehouse piece picking runs much faster. In robotic piece picking applications, robot cycles typically range between 3 and 4 seconds, achieving throughput rates exceeding 1,000 items per hour per robot.[9] To sustain this rapid picking pace, the camera and vision computer must acquire and process point cloud data while the robot arm is returning from its previous drop-off location.

That pace shortens the time allowed for image acquisition and point cloud processing. The allocated camera vision acquisition and processing time budget in automated piece picking typically ranges from 350 ms to 700 ms.[9] If 3D scanning or point cloud filtering exceeds 700 ms, the robot can end up waiting at its approach pose, which lowers hourly throughput.

In addition to speed constraints, piece picking handles a wide variety of items, spanning flexible plastic polybags, shiny packaging, and deformable cardboard cartons. Suction cup grippers are commonly chosen in piece picking because their mechanical compliance tolerates point cloud inaccuracies and prevents collision damage.[9] When a point cloud exhibits minor surface depth errors or holes, a rigid mechanical finger gripper might collide or fail to seat properly. A flexible suction cup deforms against an uneven surface and can still seal, which makes up for small perception errors.
