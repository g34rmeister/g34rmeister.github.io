5.5 Sensors for Robots

Gerald Lê

01. The “sense” in sense–think–act

Every robot runs the same basic cycle described in 1.1: sense the world, decide what to do, then act. This lesson is about the first step. A robot can only be as smart as the information its sensors give it, and every sensor gives an incomplete, noisy picture.

Robotics textbooks sort sensors into two groups:

  • Proprioceptive sensors: measure the robot’s own internal state, such as motor speed, joint angles, wheel rotation or battery voltage. The encoders from 5.3 are the classic example.
  • Exteroceptive sensors: measure the environment, such as the distance to a wall, the brightness of the floor or what a camera sees. Their readings have to be interpreted before they mean anything.

Sensors can also be passive (they measure energy already present, like a camera) or active (they emit energy and measure what comes back, like an ultrasonic or infrared range finder).

02. Ultrasonic range finders

The HC-SR04 is the usual first distance sensor. A 10 µs pulse on its trigger pin makes it send out a burst of ultrasound, and its echo pin then stays HIGH for as long as the sound took to travel out and back. Lesson 5.1 showed how to turn that time into a distance; the datasheet gives the same result as distance_cm = echo_us / 58.

  • Range: about 2 cm to 4 m, according to its datasheet.
  • Wide beam: the measuring angle is about 15°, so the sensor reports the nearest thing anywhere in that cone, not just straight ahead.
  • Surfaces: the datasheet asks for a target that is smooth, flat and at least about 0.5 m². Sound hitting a smooth wall at a steep angle can bounce away (specular reflection) and never return, or return by a longer path, so the robot sees a wall as farther than it is. Soft or uneven objects, such as fabric, do not meet that condition and may not return a usable echo.
  • Speed: the datasheet recommends at least 60 ms between measurements so one echo does not interfere with the next.

03. Infrared distance sensors

Infrared (IR) distance sensors such as the Sharp GP2Y0A21YK0F shine an IR LED at the target and measure where the reflected spot lands on a position-sensitive detector. This is triangulation. Its datasheet gives a range of 10 to 80 cm and says triangulation makes it fairly insensitive to the target’s reflectivity. The output is an analog voltage that is not linear in distance (it is roughly proportional to 1/distance), so you read it with analogRead() and convert it with a calibration curve. The beam is much narrower than an ultrasonic cone, which makes IR sensors better for spotting a narrow gap.

04. Inertial measurement units (IMUs)

An IMU combines several motion sensors on one chip:

  • Accelerometer: measures acceleration, including gravity. When the robot is still, it tells you which way is down, and so the robot’s tilt.
  • Gyroscope: measures rotation rate in degrees per second. Integrating it gives an angle.
  • Magnetometer: measures the Earth’s magnetic field, like a compass. Nearby motors and steel easily disturb it.

The popular MPU-6050 has a 3-axis accelerometer and a 3-axis gyroscope (no magnetometer) and talks to an Arduino over I²C at up to 400 kHz. Its gyroscope ranges are selectable from ±250 to ±2000 °/s and its accelerometer ranges from ±2 g to ±16 g.

Gyro drift

Every gyroscope has a small bias: it reads slightly nonzero even when still. When you integrate the rate to get an angle, that bias is integrated too, so the heading error keeps growing the longer the robot runs. Gravity lets the accelerometer correct tilt, but nothing in a 6-axis IMU corrects heading (yaw), which is why IMU heading slowly wanders.

05. Line and color sensors

Line-following robots use reflectance sensors: an IR LED and a phototransistor side by side, pointed at the floor. A white surface reflects much more IR than black tape. Arrays such as the Pololu QTR-8RC put eight of these pairs in a row, so the robot can tell not just whether it sees the line but how far the line is to the left or right. They only work very close to the floor: Pololu gives an optimal distance of about 3 mm and a maximum of about 9.5 mm.

Color sensors such as the TCS34725 measure red, green, blue and clear (unfiltered) light, have an IR-blocking filter, and report the values over I²C. They are useful for sorting objects or reading colored markers, but their readings change with the room lighting, so most robots shine their own light on the target and calibrate.

06. Cameras and LiDAR

A camera gives far more information than any other sensor here: color, shape, texture, and with two cameras or a depth camera, distance. The cost is computation. A single image is many thousands of pixel values, and turning it into “there is a person 2 m ahead” needs computer vision or machine learning running on something much more powerful than an Arduino Uno, such as a single-board computer. Cameras are also sensitive to lighting.

A LiDAR measures distance with laser light, often by timing the reflected pulse. A spinning 2D LiDAR sweeps a beam around the robot and returns hundreds or thousands of distance readings per turn, which is ideal for building maps and makes it a common sensor for ROS navigation. It costs more than the sensors above and only sees the plane it scans.

08. Noise, and why one sensor is never enough

No sensor reading is exact. Electrical noise makes readings jitter, a sensor may have a small constant offset (bias), and some failures, like a missed ultrasonic echo, produce a reading that is simply wrong. Sensors also fail in different ways: encoders are smooth but drift when wheels slip, an ultrasonic sensor does not drift but is noisy, and a gyroscope is fast but slowly wanders.

That is the reason real robots carry several sensors and combine them. A good rule is to pair a sensor that is accurate in the short term with one that is reliable in the long term. The next lessons build exactly that: 5.6 Odometry estimates position from encoders, and 5.8 Kalman Filters shows how to fuse it with other measurements.

Try it
  1. Point an ultrasonic sensor at a hard wall, then at a cushion, then at the wall at a steep angle. Compare the readings and explain the differences.
  2. Leave an IMU still on the desk, add up its gyroscope rate for a minute, and watch the heading drift.
