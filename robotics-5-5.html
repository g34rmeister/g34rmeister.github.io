5.5 Sensors for Robots

Gerald Lê

01. The sensory foundation of autonomous robotics

At the heart of every robotic system lies the fundamental cybernetic loop: Sense, Think, Act. An autonomous robot can execute algorithms only as effectively as the sensory data feeding its processors. If sensory inputs are corrupted by electrical noise, delayed by communication latency, or distorted by physical blind spots, the most sophisticated trajectory planners and artificial intelligence models will command erroneous motor actions.[1]

Sensors are transducers: physical devices that convert environmental phenomena (such as acoustic pressure waves, photon intensity, magnetic flux, or kinetic acceleration) into measurable electrical voltages or digital bitstreams. Understanding the exact physical principles, datasheet limits, and noise characteristics of common robotic sensors is essential for building robust autonomous machines.[1]

02. Classification of robotic sensors

Robotics engineering classifies sensors along two primary functional axes:[1]

  • Proprioceptive Sensors: Measure the internal physical state of the robot itself. Examples include motor shaft encoders (measuring rotational speed and joint position), battery voltage monitors, motor current-sense shunts, and internal temperature thermistors. Proprioceptive measurements are self-referential and independent of external environmental conditions.[1]
  • Exteroceptive Sensors: Measure physical properties of the external world relative to the robot chassis. Examples include ultrasonic distance finders, laser rangefinders, LiDAR scanners, ground reflectance sensors, and vision cameras. Exteroceptive readings require mathematical interpretation to reconstruct obstacle boundaries or map landmarks.[1]
  • Passive Sensors: Detect ambient physical energy naturally present in the surrounding environment without emitting any artificial signals. Examples include passive infrared (PIR) thermal sensors, ambient light photodiodes, and standard RGB cameras capturing natural sunlight.[1]
  • Active Sensors: Emit controlled electromagnetic or acoustic energy into the surrounding environment and measure the resulting reflected signature. Examples include ultrasonic transducers emitting acoustic pulses, infrared proximity sensors emitting modulated infrared light, and LiDAR units emitting laser pulses. Because they supply their own energy, active sensors can work in complete darkness.[1]

03. Acoustic ranging: HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor

The classic entry-level distance sensor in mobile robotics is the HC-SR04 ultrasonic rangefinder.[2] The module has two ultrasonic transducers: a transmitter and a receiver.[2]

The sensor operates on the acoustic Time-of-Flight principle:[2]

  1. The microcontroller sends a 10-microsecond digital HIGH pulse to the sensor's Trigger pin.
  2. The onboard controller automatically commands the transmitter to emit an 8-cycle ultrasonic burst at a frequency of 40 kHz (well beyond the human hearing limit of 20 kHz).
  3. Simultaneously, the sensor pulls its Echo output pin HIGH.
  4. When the reflected sound waves strike the receiver transducer, the sensor pulls the Echo pin LOW. The duration of the HIGH pulse represents the round-trip transit time of the acoustic wave.

The microcontroller converts the echo time to distance with \(d = (v_{sound} \times t_{echo})/2\), halving because the pulse travels out and back; at about 340 m/s that is roughly 29 µs per centimetre each way.[3] On Arduino the echo is usually timed with pulseIn(), which returns 0 if no echo arrives before its timeout (one second by default), so code should treat 0 as “no reading”, not “distance zero”.[4]

The technical specifications from the manufacturer datasheet establish strict operational boundaries:[2]

  • Operational Range: 2 cm to 400 cm (0.02 m to 4.0 m). The datasheet also lists a ranging accuracy of 3 mm, a 5 V supply and a 15 mA working current. Outside the 2 cm to 4 m range, readings should not be trusted.[2]
  • Measuring Angle: According to its datasheet, roughly how wide is the HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor's measuring angle? The datasheet specifies a measuring angle of about 15 degrees. The sensor does not emit a laser-thin pencil beam; it projects an expanding conical acoustic envelope. Any physical obstacle located anywhere within that 15-degree cone will reflect an echo, and the sensor reports only the distance to the nearest reflecting surface within that cone.[2]
  • Cycle Interval: The datasheet recommends a measurement cycle of more than 60 milliseconds between trigger pulses, so that the next trigger does not overlap the previous echo. If a new ping is fired too quickly, late echoes from the last ping can be mistaken for a nearby obstacle.[2]
  • Specular Reflection and Absorption: The datasheet says the target should be a flat surface of not less than 0.5 m², as smooth as possible.[2] When a sound pulse strikes a smooth wall at a glancing angle, it can bounce away like light off a mirror (specular reflection) and never return to the receiver, so the robot may see the wall as an open corridor. Soft materials such as curtains or clothing absorb much of the sound and also return weak or no echoes.[1]

04. Optical distance sensing: triangulation versus laser Time-of-Flight

Optical rangefinding overcomes the wide-beam limitations of acoustics by utilizing focused infrared light:[5][6]

  • Optical Triangulation (Sharp GP2Y0A21YK0F): An infrared emitting diode (about 870 nm) projects a spot onto the target, and the reflected light is focused onto a Position Sensitive Detector (PSD).[5] Where the light lands on the PSD depends on the target's distance, so the datasheet notes that reflectivity has little influence on the reading because of the triangulation method, although colour boundaries on the target and mirror-like surfaces can still cause errors. The sensor operates across a range of 10 cm to 80 cm. However, its analog voltage output is non-linear (roughly inversely proportional to distance, \(V_{out} \propto 1/d\)), requiring multi-point calibration tables in software. Readings outside the 10 cm to 80 cm range are not specified and should not be trusted.[5]
  • Laser Time-of-Flight (STMicroelectronics VL53L1X): A modern solid-state sensor that uses an invisible 940 nm Class 1 laser emitter (a Vertical-Cavity Surface-Emitting Laser, VCSEL) and a receiving array of Single-Photon Avalanche Diodes (SPADs).[6] What primary advantage does a Time-of-Flight laser distance sensor have over an infrared optical triangulation sensor? The ToF sensor measures photon transit time, making its distance measurement largely independent of target color and surface reflectance.[6] The datasheet states it gives an absolute distance whatever the target colour and reflectance, although the maximum range still drops on dark targets (in the dark, about 360 cm on a white 88% target versus 170 cm on a grey 17% target in long mode). It measures up to 400 cm at up to 50 Hz over I2C, with a typical ranging error of about ±20 mm in the dark and a 27° field of view that can be narrowed to 15° by selecting a smaller region of interest. Strong ambient light shortens its range: with direct sunlight on the sensor, the long-mode range on a white target falls to about 73 cm.[6] In software, the VL53L1X trades range for robustness: short mode resists sunlight better, and a longer timing budget per reading improves accuracy at the cost of update rate.[7]

05. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs): MPU-6050 and ICM-20948

An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) packages Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) motion sensors into a single integrated circuit to track dynamic body orientation and acceleration:[8][9]

  • MEMS Accelerometers: Measure specific force (acceleration minus gravitational acceleration) along three orthogonal axes (\(a_x, a_y, a_z\)). When a robot is stationary, the accelerometer reads the specific force due to gravity alone, so an axis pointing straight up reads +1 g (9.81 m/s²). ROS states this as a convention in REP 145, a draft proposal for IMU drivers.[10] This provides an absolute physical reference for computing static pitch (\(\theta\)) and roll (\(\phi\)) tilt angles:[8]

    \[\phi = \text{atan2}(a_y, a_z), \quad \theta = \text{atan2}\left(-a_x, \sqrt{a_y^2 + a_z^2}\right)\]

  • MEMS Gyroscopes: Measure instantaneous angular velocity (\(\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z\)) in degrees per second (°/s) or radians per second about each axis. In a vibratory MEMS gyroscope, rotation produces a Coriolis effect that causes a small extra vibration, which is picked up capacitively and converted to a digital rate reading.[8]
  • Magnetometers: Measure local ambient magnetic flux density in microteslas (\(\mu\text{T}\)), serving as a digital compass to detect magnetic North. The ICM-20948's magnetometer has a full-scale range of ±4900 μT.[9] Magnetometers are less reliable references than accelerometers, because nearby magnetic fields and iron (motor currents, steel structures) distort what they read.[11][12][13]

The popular 6-axis InvenSense MPU-6050 integrates a 3-axis accelerometer and a 3-axis gyroscope with an onboard Digital Motion Processor (DMP, an internal processing unit that computes orientation angles on-chip from raw sensor readings), communicating over I2C at 400 kHz.[8] The accelerometer features user-programmable full-scale ranges of ±2g, ±4g, ±8g, and ±16g, with a noise spectral density of 400 \(\mu\text{g}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\). The gyroscope provides full-scale ranges from ±250°/s to ±2000°/s, with a noise spectral density of 0.005°/s/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\).[8]

The 9-axis TDK InvenSense ICM-20948 packages a 3-axis accelerometer and 3-axis gyroscope with a second die holding the Asahi Kasei AK09916 3-axis magnetometer.[9] It talks over I2C at up to 400 kHz or SPI at up to 7 MHz. Its accelerometer ranges (±2g to ±16g) and gyroscope ranges (±250 to ±2000°/s) match the MPU-6050, while its listed noise densities are 230 \(\mu\text{g}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\) for the accelerometer and 0.015°/s/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\) for the gyroscope.[9]

06. The mathematics of gyroscope drift

In mobile robotics, why does the heading from a gyroscope slowly wander even when the robot's motion is measured correctly? The fundamental cause is that its small bias is integrated along with the rotation rate, so the error keeps growing.[8][11]

Every physical MEMS gyroscope has a non-zero zero-rate output bias (\(b_{gyro} \neq 0\)): its reading is not exactly zero when it is not rotating, and that offset also shifts with temperature.[11][12][13] The MPU-6050 datasheet, for example, allows an initial zero-rate output tolerance of ±20°/s at 25°C, plus up to ±20°/s of variation over its −40°C to +85°C range, which is why the bias is measured and subtracted at start-up.[8] Even after that calibration, a robot sitting perfectly still may still report a small residual rate (in this lesson's example, \(+0.3^\circ/\text{s}\)).

To track heading (yaw angle \(\psi\)), the robot controller must mathematically integrate the angular velocity over time:[11]

\[\psi(t) = \psi(0) + \int_0^t \omega_z(\tau) \, d\tau = \psi(0) + \int_0^t (\omega_{true}(\tau) + b_{gyro} + n(\tau)) \, d\tau\]

This is the standard IMU noise model: \(n\) is fast white noise and \(b_{gyro}\) is a slowly varying bias.[14] The noise densities in Section 05 (such as 0.005°/s/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\)) describe the fast term \(n\); the slow bias wanders as a random walk, which is why it must be re-estimated rather than calibrated once.[14]

Integrating the constant bias offset \(b_{gyro}\) gives an error that grows linearly with time, so the computed heading keeps getting worse for as long as the robot runs:[11]

\[\text{Error}_{\psi}(t) = b_{gyro} \times t\]

In the lesson's example, after 100 seconds a bias of \(0.3^\circ/\text{s}\) produces a 30-degree heading error. Accelerometers can correct pitch and roll drift because gravity gives them an absolute reference, but gravity does not change as the robot turns about the vertical axis, so the yaw axis is the most sensitive to drift.[10] A 6-axis IMU alone therefore cannot correct heading drift; it needs help from a magnetometer, wheel encoders, LiDAR landmarks, or zero-rate updates whenever the robot is known to be stationary.[11] The open-source Fusion library, for example, detects stationary periods automatically and slowly updates its gyroscope offset during them.[12]

07. Surface reflectance and color detection

Autonomous ground robots frequently follow visual ground tracks or inspect colored workpieces:[15][16]

  • Infrared Reflectance Arrays (Pololu QTR-8RC): Pair an infrared emitter LED with an infrared phototransistor pointed directly downward toward the floor.[15] White surfaces reflect much more infrared light back to the phototransistor than dark matte tape, so the reading tells the robot whether it is over the line. The QTR-8RC places eight emitter and phototransistor pairs at a 9.525 mm (0.375 in) pitch. Pololu gives an optimal sensing distance of 3 mm (0.125 in) and a maximum recommended sensing distance of 9.5 mm (0.375 in); mounted higher than that, the contrast between line and floor becomes unreliable.[15]
  • Color Sensors (ams TCS34725): Feature a 3 × 4 photodiode array made of red-filtered, green-filtered, blue-filtered and clear (unfiltered) photodiodes.[16] The colour photodiodes are coated with an on-chip IR-blocking filter, which reduces the infrared part of the incoming light so it does not skew the colour readings. Four integrating ADCs convert the channels to 16-bit values, read over I2C at up to 400 kHz.[16]

08. High-dimensional perception: 2D/3D LiDAR and cameras

When robots must map large indoor spaces or recognize complex objects, they transition to high-bandwidth optical sensors:[1]

  • 2D LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging): Spins a laser rangefinder to measure distances around a single horizontal plane. As one real example, the Slamtec RPLIDAR A3 scans at a typical 10 Hz (adjustable from 5 Hz to 15 Hz), takes up to 16,000 samples per second (about 1,600 points per revolution at 10 Hz), and reaches up to 25 m on white targets (10 m on dark ones), with an accuracy of about 1% to 2.5% of the range.[17] 2D LiDAR is a common sensor for Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), but it is “blind” to obstacles above or below its scan plane, such as table overhangs or descending staircases.[1]
  • Vision Cameras (Monocular, Stereo, RGB-D): Monocular cameras capture rich 2D color and texture information but lack direct depth perception. Stereo cameras combine two calibrated lenses to calculate depth via stereoscopic disparity matching. RGB-D cameras add a depth image to the colour image. The Intel RealSense D435, for example, uses active infrared stereo depth sensing and delivers depth at up to 1280 × 720 resolution and up to 90 frames per second, with a working range from about 0.3 m to roughly 10 m depending on the scene and lighting.[18] Processing camera streams takes considerable computing power, and passive cameras struggle in low light.[1]

09. Comparing common sensors and multi-sensor fusion

Every sensor technology has its own trade-offs. The table summarises the sources cited in the sections above:[1][2][5][6][11][15][17][18]

Sensor Technology Primary Measurement Major Strength Primary Failure Mode
Wheel Encoders Wheel rotation (proprioceptive) Simple, high update rate, low cost Accumulates error when wheels slip
Ultrasonic (HC-SR04) Acoustic distance (2 cm to 4 m) Low cost, functions in complete darkness 15° wide beam, specular reflection on angled walls
Optical Triangulation (Sharp) Infrared spot position (10 cm to 80 cm) Narrow optical beam, sharp obstacle detection Short range, highly non-linear output voltage
Laser ToF (VL53L1X) Photon transit time (up to 4 m) Direct flight timing, independent of surface color Range reduced in direct outdoor sunlight
MEMS IMU (MPU-6050) Acceleration and angular velocity Fast update rates, self-contained Unbounded gyroscope heading drift over time
Reflectance Array (QTR-8RC) Ground optical reflectance Excellent for line following Must be mounted close to the floor (3 mm optimal, 9.5 mm max)
RGB-D Camera Dense 3D point clouds and color images Richest spatial data representation High data rate and computational load
2D Scanning LiDAR Planar distance scan (360 degrees) Long range (up to 25 m for the RPLIDAR A3), well suited to SLAM Expensive, restricted to single horizontal slice

Because no single sensor is free from error, professional robotics systems rely on multi-sensor data fusion. By pairing sensors with complementary strengths (such as pairing fast, smooth wheel encoders that drift during wheel slip with an IMU gyroscope that resists slip but drifts over time), algorithms like the Kalman filter combine them into a better state estimate than either sensor gives alone.[1] To fuse sensors, software needs each reading's uncertainty as well as its value; ROS IMU messages therefore carry a covariance matrix with every reading.[19]

Try it
  1. Mount an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor on an Arduino. Measure distance to a flat wooden board, then slowly rotate the board to a 45-degree angle. Identify the critical angle where specular reflection causes the sensor to drop the echo.
  2. Leave an MPU-6050 IMU stationary on a table. In your Arduino sketch, integrate the raw z-axis gyroscope readings at 50 Hz for 60 seconds and plot the accumulating heading drift. Calculate the sensor's average zero-rate bias offset.
  3. Compare the distance reported by an optical triangulation sensor (Sharp GP2Y0A21YK0F) and a laser Time-of-Flight sensor (VL53L1X) when pointed at a white target versus a black matte target at a distance of 30 cm.
