1.7 The Industrial Robot Cell

Gerald Lê

01. From robot arm to robot cell

In the last lesson we looked at the arm itself. An arm bolted to the floor with nothing around it cannot do useful work: it has no part to pick up, no tool, no way of knowing when the next part has arrived, and nothing to protect a person who walks too close. To make it productive, engineers build a robot cell around it.

The International Federation of Robotics (IFR) uses the ISO 8373:2021 definition of an industrial robot: an “automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator, programmable in three or more axes,” which can be fixed in place or mobile and is used in industrial automation.[1] That definition covers the machine you buy from a robot maker. The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) widens the view. Its Technical Manual describes an industrial robot system as the robot itself, the end-effector attached to the manipulator, the computers, processors and programs that make up the control system, power sources, sensors, and input/output interfaces. OSHA names four major components of the robot: the manipulator, the control system, the teach pendant and the end-effector. Adding the end-effector is what makes it an industrial robot system.[2]

A cell goes one step further: it is the robot system plus everything an integrator adds so that it can do one job safely and repeatably, from part feeding and sensors to a coordinating controller and guarding. This lesson covers those pieces, how they talk to each other, who builds a cell, three common cell types, and the basics of cycle time and cost.

02. Robot, robot system, robot cell, robot line

The safety standard that governs cells is ISO 10218. Part 1 covers the robot itself, which is supplied as partly completed machinery. Part 2, revised as ISO 10218-2:2025, covers the safety of industrial robot applications and robot cells across their whole life: integration, commissioning, operation, maintenance and decommissioning.[3][4] One technical summary describes it as addressing the hazards of robots “integrated and installed in industrial robot cells and lines.”[5]

The standards define these terms precisely, but their exact wording is behind a paywall and is not reproduced here. For learning purposes, a simple nested picture works well. Treat it as a teaching paraphrase, not as quoted definitions:

  • Robot: the arm, its controller and its teach pendant, as delivered by the robot maker.
  • Robot system: the robot plus its end-effector and any equipment, devices or sensors it needs to do its task, in line with the OSHA description above.[2]
  • Robot cell: one or more robot systems together with their safeguarding, the machines they work with, and the protected space around them.
  • Robot line: several cells or machines linked together to carry out one production process, such as a line of welding cells that builds a car body.

The 2025 edition puts weight on the application: the robot together with its tool, its workpiece and its task. The same arm can be low-risk moving a small plastic part slowly and dangerous swinging a heavy welding gun, so risk is assessed for the whole application.

03. What is inside a typical cell

Most cells contain the same families of parts. Use this list as a checklist:

  • 1. Manipulator: the arm that moves the tool.[2]
  • 2. Robot controller: the cabinet that holds the drives and the computer that runs the robot program.[2]
  • 3. Teach pendant: the handheld unit used to jog, teach and test the robot,[2] with an emergency stop button and an enabling device (see below).
  • 4. End-of-arm tooling (EOAT): the end-effector that does the work,[2] such as a gripper, a vacuum cup, a weld torch or a tool changer.
  • 5. Part presentation: the hardware that brings parts to the robot in a known position and takes them away, such as infeed and outfeed conveyors, fixtures, nests, bowl feeders and pallet stations.
  • 6. Sensors: contact sensors such as limit switches (for example part-present or door position switches) and often an optical, camera-based sensor that locates parts.[2]
  • 7. Cell PLC and HMI: a programmable logic controller that coordinates the sequence, and a human-machine interface (a touch screen) where operators start, stop and see alarms.
  • 8. Network: an industrial Ethernet switch or fieldbus that links the PLC, robot and other devices.
  • 9. Safety controller: a safety PLC or safety relays that decide whether motion is allowed at all.
  • 10. Safeguarding: fences, an interlocked gate, light curtains, area scanners and emergency stop buttons.
  • 11. Process equipment: the machine the robot serves or the process it carries out, such as a CNC machine, a welding power source or a positioner.

The teach pendant deserves a closer look because it is how people work near the robot. Its enabling device is “a manually operated control device which, when continuously activated and used in conjunction with a separate actuating (start) control, will allow the machine to function in manual operating mode.”[6] In other words, holding the enabling switch allows motion but does not start anything; a separate start or jog command is still needed.[6] Most enabling switches have three positions. If the operator does not hold the switch in its middle (set) position, the manipulator cannot move.[7]

Robot makers build these requirements into their products. Universal Robots' manuals, citing ISO 10218-2, state that some of its larger models (the UR15, UR20, UR30, UR8 Long and UR18) need an external enabling device or a three-position-enabling (3PE) teach pendant whenever someone programs or teaches within reach of the robot application.[8] Mode keys are not a substitute for lockout. In one fatal case investigated in Michigan, the gate key was meant to put the robot in manual mode before anyone entered, but the key system did not work properly and the gate had no interlock. A foreman entered without locking out the robot, which was still in automatic mode, and was pinned when the arm moved.[9]

04. Safeguarding: keeping people out of harm's way

The safeguards are what separate a robot cell from a robot on a bench. As summarized from the OSHA Technical Manual, the options include mechanical limiting devices, nonmechanical limiting devices, presence-sensing safeguarding devices, fixed barriers and interlocked barrier guards.[10][11] During automatic operation all safeguards should be active, and no part of the operator's body should be inside the safeguarded area.[10][11] An OSHA interpretation letter lists acceptable controls as “fixed barriers that are not easily defeated, barrier guards with interlocks that immediately stop machine motion, or presence-sensing devices that immediately stop machine motion,” and points to the ANSI/RIA R15.06-1999 robot safety standard.[12]

  • Fence and interlocked gate: a fixed fence keeps people out. The gate has a safety switch, so opening it stops the robot.
  • Light curtain: a row of infrared beams across an opening, such as the place where an operator loads parts. Breaking any beam sends a stop signal.
  • Area (laser) scanner: a scanner on the floor that watches configured zones. Depending on how it is set up, a person entering a zone can trigger a warning, a slower speed or a stop.
  • Emergency stop buttons: red mushroom buttons on the pendant, the HMI and around the fence.

A light curtain only protects people if it is far enough from the hazard. When a hand breaks the beams, the robot still needs some time to stop, and the hand keeps moving during that time. ISO 13855 gives the minimum safety distance as:[13][14] 

S = K × T + C

S = minimum distance from the light curtain to the hazard (mm)
K = approach speed of the body part (mm/s)
T = total stopping time: light curtain response + machine stopping time (s)
C = extra distance that depends on the curtain's resolution d (mm)

For a curtain fine enough to detect a finger or hand, K = 2000 mm/s and C = 8 × (d − 14 mm). If S comes out above 500 mm, the calculation is repeated with K = 1600 mm/s.[13][14] In a worked example from Keyence, T = 0.1157 s and C = 88 mm give S = 2000 × 0.1157 + 88 ≈ 319 mm.[13] (C = 88 mm corresponds to a 25 mm resolution, since 8 × (25 − 14) = 88.) The vendor guides do not agree on the minimum distances that apply when S is small, so a real installation must be checked against ISO 13855 itself.[13][14] A slow-stopping robot needs its light curtain farther away; a faster stop allows a more compact cell.

05. How the parts talk: PLC, robot and handshakes

Inside a cell, devices exchange two very different kinds of information. One kind says what to do: run job 3, the part is in place, the cycle is done. The other kind says whether motion is allowed at all: the emergency stops are not pressed and the gate is closed. Keeping these two apart is the most important idea in cell wiring.

In the most common architecture the cell PLC is in charge. In PROFINET terms, a Siemens PLC acts as the IO-Controller and an ABB IRC5 robot controller acts as an IO-Device. PROFINET does not use the older PROFIBUS words “master” and “slave,”[15] although many engineers still say them, as in a forum request for a “Siemens PLC as master and IRC5 as slave” example project.[16] Status and command bits are exchanged cyclically, and one tutorial suggests a 1 ms update time as a starting point.[15] In smaller single-robot cells, such as a cobot tending one machine, the robot controller often runs the logic itself and reads simple inputs from the gripper, sensors and machine directly.

Because the robot and the PLC run separate programs, they coordinate through handshake signals. Control.com explains that handshakes are needed because external devices such as robots and CNC machines “do not have direct access to the internal programs of the control system or PLC.”[17] A simple handshake for one job looks like this (the signal names are examples):

Step Sender Signal Meaning
1 Robot Ready = 1 The robot is at home and can accept a job.
2 PLC Job number = 3, Start = 1 Run program 3.
3 Robot Busy = 1 The robot has accepted the request and is moving.
4 Robot Busy = 0, Job complete = 1 The job is finished.
5 PLC Start = 0 The PLC clears its request, and the robot clears Job complete.

Each side waits for the other's answer, so neither runs ahead if a signal is lost. The safety interlocks travel separately. Signals such as “E-stop OK” and “guard door closed” are either hard-wired from a safety PLC or safety relays, or sent over a safety protocol such as CIP Safety or PROFIsafe (safety-rated industrial network extensions designed to guarantee fail-safe communication over fieldbuses).[17] A safety protocol can share the same cable as ordinary data, but it is a separately rated path. Never make a person's safety depend on a normal handshake bit.

Which network carries the data depends on the plant. FANUC lists EtherNet/IP, EtherCAT, PROFINET and PROFIBUS among its robot communication options. It positions EtherNet/IP for flexibility and broad integration, EtherCAT for high-precision motion, and PROFINET for complex automation and IT integration.[18] EtherCAT is a master/slave protocol on a logical ring that processes frames “on the fly” as they pass each device, which keeps delay low. EtherNet/IP uses a producer/consumer model.[19]

06. Who does what: robot maker, integrator, end user

Three groups share the work of getting a cell into production:

  • Robot maker (OEM): companies such as FANUC, ABB, KUKA, Yaskawa and Universal Robots supply the robot. Under ISO 10218 the robot is partly completed machinery, not a finished, safe machine on its own.[3][20]
  • System integrator: designs, builds, programs, safeguards and validates the cell, the integration and commissioning phases that ISO 10218-2:2025 covers.[3] One guide notes that the integrator must verify and validate that the finished cell performs the way its risk assessment assumed.[21]
  • End user: the factory that runs and maintains the cell. Operation and maintenance are also within the scope of ISO 10218-2, so the end user keeps the safeguards working for the life of the cell.[3]

How does a factory know an integrator is competent? In North America, the Association for Advancing Automation (A3) runs a voluntary Certified Robot Integrator program. Integrators must already show “a high level of proficiency, experience and success” to be considered, and certification involves “an on-site audit, practical assessment of key personnel, safety training, and other important criteria.”[22] Certification lasts two years before it must be renewed.[22] The process includes pre-qualification, the on-site audit, a robot safety risk assessment and an exam.[23] The U.S. O*NET occupational database lists it as a credential that requires an exam and more than two years of experience.[24]

07. How a cell gets built: from idea to production

A cell project follows a fairly standard path, although each integrator names and splits the steps a little differently. In general practice it runs: application study with real part samples and a cycle-time estimate; concept layout and quote; detailed mechanical, tooling, electrical and controls design; risk assessment; simulation and offline programming; build and debug at the integrator's shop; the formal acceptance tests described below; and finally training, documentation and support.

Simulation saves time on the factory floor. In one software vendor's case study, a welding integrator programmed and validated its cells offline with a digital twin and avoided “tedious and time-consuming teach-in” at the robot.[25] Treat vendor savings claims with care.

Commissioning a turnkey cell usually has two formal checkpoints.[26]

  • Factory acceptance test (FAT): held at the integrator's site before shipment. It verifies build quality, controls, safety logic and baseline process performance, and ends with a report of open points still to fix.[26] It also reviews wiring, I/O mapping, interlocks and alarms.[27]
  • Site acceptance test (SAT): held after installation at the customer's plant. It proves safe operation, output and quality targets, and that the customer's operators and maintenance staff can run the cell without the integrator.[26]

A fuller sequence runs FAT, site preparation and installation, SAT, ramp-up, and finally handover to the customer.[26] Problems found in either test are logged on a punch list, which must be closed before final approval.[27]

08. Three typical cells

Machine tending

In a machine-tending cell the robot loads raw blanks into a CNC machine and unloads finished parts. The robot and the CNC exchange I/O, either discrete signals or a standard such as MTConnect, so that loading and unloading stay in step with the cut.[28] The interlocks must be based on evidence, not on timing. A “door open” state should come from a proven door position, not from the time elapsed since the door output was switched. Before each step the cell needs proof, such as spindle stopped and door confirmed open before the robot enters, and the correct program loaded before cycle start.[29]

The robot is rarely the slow part here. Cycle time in machine tending is driven by machining time, the door and chuck sequence, and reliable part presentation, not by the robot's top speed.[30] On an older machine, the robot can open and close the CNC door itself instead of the cell fitting an automatic door driven by a signal; Robotiq reports this works well in many integrations, while for a new machine an automatic door is generally worth its cost.[31]

Palletizing

A palletizing cell stacks cases onto pallets at the end of a packaging line. Pearson's RPC cell is a typical example: an empty pallet is dispensed onto a three-strand drag chain conveyor and carried to the loading position, and the robot places slip sheets (protective separator sheets placed between stacked layers for load stability) between layers when needed. Pearson rates it at 20 cases per minute steady state, with surges to 30.[32] Arpac's LT palletizer uses a “smart” infeed conveyor that separates the products, has a fully interlocked operator door and a light curtain, and is rated at up to 15 cases per minute.[33] Grippers are often vacuum plates.[34] The Arpac LT, for example, uses a dual-zone vacuum plate gripper that handles one or two cases at a time, and a light curtain guards the opening where full pallets leave the cell.[35] These are vendor ratings, not guarantees.

Arc welding with a two-station positioner

In a welding cell the operator's loading time can hide inside the robot's welding time. ABB's twin-station positioner (IRBP R) lets the robot weld on one side while the operator unloads and loads the other, with a screen between the two sides to block the arc light.[36] When both jobs are done, the positioner swaps the sides. Yaskawa states that the 250 kg version of its ArcWorld IV-6200SL positioner indexes between stations in 1.6 s and the 750 kg version in 2.2 s.[37] An earlier announcement gave 1.75 s and 2.5 s, so check the current datasheet before using a figure.[38]

09. Cycle time basics

Cycle time is the time between successive good parts leaving the cell. It is set by the bottleneck, the slowest activity in the loop: robot handling, the process itself (machining or welding), or the operator's loading. Takt time is the pace the customer demands: available production time divided by the number of parts required. The cell is fast enough only if its cycle time is less than or equal to takt time.

Designers shorten cycle time by overlapping work so that a short activity hides inside a long one. A two-station positioner, a double gripper (unload the finished part and load the blank in one visit) and a buffer conveyor all do this. Here is a worked example with made-up numbers chosen only to show the arithmetic:

  • Given: welding takes 90 s, operator unload and load takes 60 s, and the positioner index takes 2 s.
  • One station: the robot waits while the operator works, so the cycle is about 90 + 60 = 150 s.
  • Two stations: the operator works while the robot welds, so the cycle is about max(90, 60) + 2 = 92 s.
  • Result: the robot is now busy about 90 of every 92 s (roughly 98%), and output rises by about 60% with the same robot.

10. What a cell costs

The robot is often the smaller part of the bill. Published estimates vary widely and most are secondary or dated, so treat them as a range:

  • About a quarter: a 2010 editorial in the journal Industrial Robot said that “various studies have concluded that an industrial robot represents around 25 percent of the total cost of implementation,” without naming the studies.[39]
  • Four to six times the robot: a Yaskawa Motoman director, quoted by Engineering.com, gave as a rule of thumb that integration “including safety, tooling, and programming will cost about four to six times the cost of the robot.”[40]
  • A third to under half: an automation integrator's 2026 guide says a robot “represents just 30–45% of the total project cost.”[41]
  • About double: a collaborative-robot vendor, which has an interest in making traditional integration look expensive, says integration “can double your robot costs.”[42]

These figures do not agree exactly, and none comes from a transparent, current survey. What they share is the direction: tooling, safeguarding, controls, programming and commissioning usually cost as much as the robot or several times more. The higher multiples tend to describe more complex cells, the lower ones simpler cells.

Try it
  1. Label a cell layout. On paper or in a drawing tool, sketch a top view of a machine-tending cell about 4 m by 4 m and place and label these components: robot arm on a base; robot controller cabinet (outside the fence); teach pendant; gripper (EOAT) on the robot; CNC machine with its door facing the robot; infeed drawer for raw blanks; outfeed tray for finished parts; part-present sensor on the infeed; vision camera above the infeed; inspection gauge station; cell PLC and HMI panel (outside the fence); network switch; safety controller; perimeter fence; interlocked access gate; light curtain across the operator's loading opening; three emergency stop buttons. Then draw two kinds of lines: solid lines for data (PLC to robot, PLC to CNC, PLC to HMI) and dashed lines for safety signals (gate, light curtain and E-stops to the safety controller, safety controller to robot and CNC).
  2. Trace one part through your cell. Write a numbered list of each step a single blank goes through, from the infeed drawer to the outfeed tray. For each step, name the device that acts and the signal that proves the step is complete. A starting point: (1) operator loads the drawer outside the light curtain; (2) the part-present sensor turns on; (3) the camera locates the blank; (4) the robot picks it with one side of a double gripper; (5) the robot waits for the CNC to report cycle done, spindle stopped and door confirmed open; (6) the robot unloads the finished part and loads the blank; (7) the robot leaves, reports clear, and the CNC closes its door and starts; (8) the robot places the finished part on the gauge, then on the outfeed tray. Mark which steps happen at the same time as machining.
  3. Use the safety-distance formula from section 04 to find S for a hand-detecting light curtain with 14 mm resolution and a total stopping time of 0.2 s. Then explain in one sentence what would happen to S if the robot stopped faster.
