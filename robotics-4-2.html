4.2 Ethics in Robotics (Hardware, Software)

Gerald Lê

01. Physical safety as an ethical obligation

In software engineering, an unhandled exception or an algorithmic deadlock crashes a process or corrupts a database record. In robotics, a software bug or an improperly bypassed hardware interlock can command an articulated steel arm carrying a 50-kilogram payload to swing at three meters per second, crushing an operator against a machine fixture. Physical safety is not merely a technical compliance checkbox: it is the primary ethical obligation an engineer owes to human beings sharing physical space with autonomous machinery.[1]

The core standard governing this domain is ISO 10218, published in two comprehensive parts: ISO 10218-1:2025 covers the safety requirements for industrial robots themselves, while ISO 10218-2:2025 governs robot systems, integration, and manufacturing cells.[1][2] In the United States, these consensus standards are harmonized directly as ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025. Engineering ethics requires understanding that safety cannot be added as an afterthought in software. Instead, a dependable robot architecture requires defense in depth across physical hardware safeguards, dedicated functional safety controllers, and strict supervisory software layers.[2]

02. Hardware safeguards and emergency stops

The foundation of robot cell safety rests on deterministic physical hardware barriers and electromechanical interlocks that operate independently of high-level microprocessors.[2] The standard hierarchy of safeguarding hardware includes:

  • Perimeter guarding: Rigid physical fencing and mechanical barriers that enclose the robot hazard zone, designed per ISO 14120 to withstand workpiece ejection and prevent physical entry during automatic cycling.[2]
  • Interlocked access gates: Guard doors equipped with safety-rated interlock switches and solenoid-driven guard locking. When a technician opens an interlock gate, power to the actuator motors is instantly severed before a person can reach the moving hazard.[2]
  • Presence-sensing electro-sensitive equipment: Optical safety light curtains, safety mats, and time-of-flight laser area scanners that project invisible optical beams across access zones. If an operator breaks a light curtain beam, the output signal switching devices (OSSDs) open within milliseconds, triggering an immediate stop.[2]

Every industrial robot cell must feature dedicated emergency stop (E-stop) controls conforming to ISO 13850:2015.[3] An emergency stop control must be a mushroom-headed red palm button set against a high-contrast yellow background, featuring a mechanical latching detent mechanism that requires a deliberate manual twist or pull to reset.[3]

ISO 13850 and IEC 60204-1 establish distinct functional stop categories:[3][4]

  • Stop Category 0: An uncontrolled stop achieved by immediately removing electrical power from the machine actuators. Mechanical spring-applied brakes engage instantly. This stop category is employed when an electrical fault occurs or when power retention introduces risk.[3]
  • Stop Category 1: A controlled stop where electrical power is deliberately maintained to the motor drives so that dynamic electrical braking decelerates the arm rapidly along its programmed path to a complete standstill, after which power is removed and mechanical brakes latch. This prevents heavy arms from tumbling under gravity or swinging wildly outside the safety cell.[3]
  • Stop Category 2: A controlled stop where drive power remains continuously supplied to the motors while the robot is held at a monitored standstill. This category cannot serve as an emergency stop, but is used in collaborative applications such as safety-rated monitored stops.[4]

03. Safeguards while teaching: the enabling device

During production, human operators remain outside the safety fence. However, during programming, trajectory calibration, and maintenance, technicians must enter the active workspace while holding a portable teach pendant.[1] Under these conditions, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) and ISO 10218-1 mandate strict manual mode safeguards.[1][5]

The primary safeguard is the three-position enabling device (often referred to colloquially as a deadman switch), built into the grip of the teach pendant per IEC 60204-1:[1][4]

  • Position 1 (Released): The operator is not gripping the switch. The internal safety circuit is open, actuator power is disconnected, and the robot cannot move.[4]
  • Position 2 (Intermediate Center): The operator gently squeezes the switch to its middle detent position. The dual-channel safety contacts close, permitting manual jog commands to move the robot axes.[4]
  • Position 3 (Fully Squeezed Panic): If the robot makes an unexpected movement, human physiological panic causes muscles to either release or clench tightly. If the technician squeezes the switch past the center detent, the contacts snap open immediately, engaging mechanical brakes and halting all motion. The switch cannot re-enable motion as it is released: it must return completely to Position 1 before entering Position 2 again.[4]

In addition to the enabling device, manual teaching mode (commonly labeled T1 mode) enforces a hardware-supervised speed limitation: the tool center point (TCP) is legally restricted to a maximum velocity of 250 mm/s.[1][5] This speed limit provides human reflexes sufficient time to observe an unexpected trajectory deviation and react before contact occurs.[5]

04. Functional safety architectures and safety PLCs

Modern robot safety circuits cannot rely on standard consumer microcontrollers or generic desktop operating systems. If a regular PC crashes, its output pins might latch in a high-voltage state, keeping a pneumatic gripper pressurized or a conveyor motor running indefinitely.[6]

Instead, functional safety is governed by ISO 13849-1 and IEC 62061.[6] ISO 13849-1 classifies safety functions into Performance Levels ranging from PL a (lowest reliability) to PL e (highest reliability). Industrial robot cells standardly require PL d or PL e with Category 3 or Category 4 designated architectures:[6]

  • Dual-channel redundancy: Two completely separate electrical signal lines carry every safety signal (such as an E-stop button or gate interlock). If one wire snaps, shorts to ground, or welds a relay contact shut, the second redundant channel still opens the circuit, preserving the safety function.[6]
  • Cross-monitoring diagnostics: Dedicated safety PLCs (programmable logic controllers) continuously pulse test signals through both channels. If channel A and channel B disagree for more than a few milliseconds, the safety controller flags a discrepancy fault and locks the system in a safe standstill.[6]
  • Mean time to dangerous failure: Components certified to PL e possess a Mean Time to Dangerous Failure (MTTFd) exceeding 100 years and diagnostic coverage exceeding 99 percent.[6]

05. Software safety layers: watchdogs and geofencing

Above the electrical hardware safety circuits sits the software safety supervisory layer. Robotic systems depend on complex software stacks containing hundreds of thousands of lines of code, including ROS 2 nodes, neural networks for object pose estimation, and numerical inverse kinematics solvers. None of this complex user software is formally verified as bug-free. Therefore, a strict architectural separation must exist between the unverified application code and the certified safety kernel.[1]

The most essential software safety mechanism is the watchdog timer.[1] What does a watchdog timer do? A watchdog timer triggers a safe stop if the main program stops responding.[1]

In hardware, a watchdog timer is an independent digital timing circuit with a countdown register. Under normal operating conditions, the robot's real-time trajectory execution thread must periodically refresh, or "kick," the watchdog register (for example, every 5 milliseconds) before the countdown reaches zero. If the main software deadlocks, enters an infinite loop, or hangs on an unhandled exception, it fails to service the watchdog. When the timer expires, the hardware circuit immediately de-energizes the safety output relays, halting robot motion via a Stop Category 0 or Category 1 condition.[1]

Software safety also enforces volumetric geofencing and virtual safety zones.[1] Rather than hoping a Python path planner does not plot a trajectory through a safety fence or into an operator workstation, dedicated safety processors calculate cartesian positions in real time using dual-channel optical encoder feedback. Leading robot manufacturers implement this safety layer through proprietary, safety-rated controllers:

  • FANUC Dual Check Safety (DCS): Redundant microprocessors cross-check joint angular positions and 3D tool positions 1,000 times per second, cutting motor torque if the arm attempts to cross a virtual bounding box.
  • ABB SafeMove: Certified functional safety software that monitors cartesian zones, orientation limits, and tool speeds independently of the RAPID motion controller.
  • Universal Robots Safety System: Dual safety CPUs running dedicated safety firmware verify tool speed, joint momentum, and boundary planes configured in PolyScope.

06. Sensor privacy and data harvesting

While physical safety protects human bodies from mechanical kinetic injury, the rapid expansion of autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) into hospitals, retail stores, public sidewalks, and private domestic residences introduces grave privacy and surveillance challenges.[7]

A modern autonomous robot is fundamentally an array of mobile surveillance sensors. To perform simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) and collision avoidance, mobile robots carry high-resolution RGB-D color cameras, multi-beam 3D LiDAR scanners, thermal infrared sensors, and directional audio microphones.[7] Unlike stationary security cameras that people can choose to avoid, an autonomous robot patrols dynamic environments, capturing high-resolution spatial maps and intimate imagery of private life.[7]

The ethical hazard of sensor harvesting was demonstrated vividly in December 2022 by an MIT Technology Review investigative report.[7] Development versions of consumer robot vacuums (iRobot Roomba J7 series) recorded detailed household photos during customer testing. These images, including private photographs of a woman seated on a toilet, were uploaded to cloud servers and shared with third-party data annotation contractors in Venezuela employed by Scale AI. Contractors posted several sensitive screenshots to private discussion groups on Facebook and Discord.[7]

Even when RGB images are omitted in favor of 3D LiDAR point clouds, high-density point clouds can be mathematically reconstructed to reveal human body contours, recognize gait patterns, and blueprint floor plans of homes or sensitive industrial facilities. To uphold the IEEE Code of Ethics duty to protect individual privacy, robotics engineers must practice data minimization:[7]

  • Edge computing: Process video streams and obstacle classification locally on the robot's onboard GPU (e.g., NVIDIA Jetson), discarding raw video frames immediately after extracting obstacle bounding boxes or feature points.
  • Physical privacy shutters: Incorporate mechanical lens covers or hardware disconnect switches for microphones and cameras when privacy-sensitive operations occur.
  • Anonymization: Apply real-time blurring to human faces, license plates, and sensitive documents prior to logging diagnostic telemetry.

07. Algorithmic bias in robotics perception

When robots rely on machine learning models for perception, object recognition, and human detection, they inherit the systematic biases embedded in training datasets.[8]

Landmark research by Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru (2018) exposed deep demographic disparities in commercial computer vision classifiers, demonstrating that error rates on darker-skinned female faces were dramatically higher than on lighter-skinned male faces.[8] In social robotics, automated delivery bots, and autonomous vehicles, algorithmic bias creates immediate physical safety hazards:

  • Pedestrian detection disparities: Deep neural networks trained primarily on balanced datasets of upright, walking adults frequently fail to identify individuals in wheelchairs, children, pedestrians using walkers, or workers wearing bulky high-visibility reflective coats.
  • Facial analysis in human-robot collaboration: Service robots designed to follow human gesture commands or interpret facial cues struggle when interacting with users across diverse ages, ethnicities, and facial structures.

Engineers have a moral responsibility to rigorously audit training data, evaluate false-negative detection rates across diverse demographic subsets, and ensure that safety-critical obstacle detection relies on multi-modal sensor fusion (such as radar and ultrasonic sensors) rather than camera vision models alone.[8]

08. Autonomous weapons and meaningful human control

The most profound ethical boundary in modern robotics concerns the application of autonomous mobility and computer vision to lethal military force. The United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) has hosted deliberations through its Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) regarding Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS).[9]

Lethal autonomous weapons are robotic systems that, once activated, can select and engage targets with lethal force without human intervention.[9] The international ethical and legal consensus against fully autonomous targeting rests on several fundamental principles:

  • Human dignity and the Martens Clause: Delegating the decision to take a human life to an automated mathematical algorithm reduces human existence to sensor signals and numerical probabilities, violating human dignity under international humanitarian law.[9]
  • Accountability gap: When an autonomous drone strikes a civilian wedding party due to a misclassified sensor reading, who bears criminal liability? The remote commander who launched the platform, the software engineer who wrote the neural network, or the company that integrated the sensors? Autonomous targeting creates an accountability void.[9]
  • Flash warfare and destabilization: When autonomous military machines engage one another at algorithmic millisecond timescales, tactical escalation can outpace human cognitive comprehension, triggering uncontrollable armed conflicts before diplomatic intervention is possible.[9]

To counter these risks, international humanitarian law requires Meaningful Human Control (MHC): humans must maintain situational awareness, context comprehension, and positive command authorization over every lethal decision.[9] Under Article 36 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions, states are legally obligated to conduct comprehensive weapon reviews to ensure that emerging autonomous systems comply with the laws of armed conflict.[9]

09. Governance frameworks: IEEE 7000 and the EU AI Act

To ensure that ethical reflection is integrated into software and hardware lifecycles, international standards organizations and legislative bodies have created formal governance frameworks.[10][11]

In 2021, the IEEE Standards Association published IEEE 7000-2021, titled IEEE Standard Model Process for Addressing Ethical Concerns During System Design.[10] IEEE 7000 establishes a structured methodology for value-based engineering:

  • Concept of operations exploration: Explicitly identifying stakeholders, including operators, bystanders, and affected communities.[10]
  • Ethical value elicitation: Uncovering core human values (such as transparency, bodily integrity, privacy, and justice) relevant to the project.[10]
  • Ethical Value Requirements (EVRs): Translating abstract philosophical values into testable engineering specifications, complete with verification criteria and architectural traceability matrices.[10]

In 2024, the European Union established the world's first binding comprehensive horizontal legal framework for artificial intelligence: the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).[11] The AI Act categorizes AI and robotic control systems into four distinct risk tiers:[11]

Risk Tier Description and Regulatory Requirements Robotics and AI Examples
Unacceptable Risk Strictly prohibited across the European Union. Violates fundamental human rights and dignity. Cognitive behavioral manipulation, social scoring systems, biometric categorizations, untargeted facial scraping.
High Risk Permitted only after satisfying stringent conformity assessments, comprehensive risk management, data bias mitigation, automatic event logging, and human oversight. Safety components of industrial machinery, collaborative robots, surgical robotic systems, autonomous public transport.
Specific Transparency Risk Permitted with mandatory disclosure obligations so users are aware they are interacting with automated systems. Conversational tutoring bots, synthetic voice interfaces, emotion recognition systems.
Minimal or Low Risk Permitted with no additional regulatory obligations beyond standard product safety rules. Basic industrial trajectory interpolation, rule-based vacuum route planners, video game non-player characters.

Under the EU AI Act, how are safety components of industrial machinery and collaborative robotics classified? They are classified as high risk, requiring formal risk management, rigorous data governance to mitigate algorithmic bias, full technical logging, and guaranteed human oversight.[11] By aligning hardware safety with ethical governance, engineers ensure that autonomous machines enrich human society without sacrificing human safety or dignity.[11]

Try it
  1. Inspect a commercial robotic device (such as a robotic lawn mower, an automated warehouse AMR, or a 3D printer) and identify its hardware safety controls, including physical stops, interlocks, and presence sensors.
  2. Diagram a dual-channel safety circuit containing an emergency stop button, an enabling switch, and a safety PLC, explaining how a single broken wire is detected without disabling the safety stop function.
  3. Review the sensor suite of a mobile robot and design an edge computing pipeline that fulfills GDPR data minimization principles by extracting navigation obstacles while discarding raw camera footage.
