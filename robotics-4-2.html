      4.2 ROS 1 vs. ROS 2

      Gerald Lê
      Chapter 4, lesson 2 of 10 ← Previous: 4.1 Definition and Introduction Next: 4.3 Installing ROS on Your Local Environment →

      01. Why the robotics community rebuilt ROS from scratch

      Picture software designed for one research robot, and then imagine people running it on factory arms, self-driving cars, and commercial cleaning machines. That is what happened to the Robot Operating System (ROS). A design built for one setup could not easily stretch to fit teams of robots or weak wireless links. Learning why the community started fresh helps you see why ROS 2 works the way it does today.

      Work on ROS began in November 2007 as the software environment for Willow Garage’s PR2 robot.[1] That early design assumed a single robot with powerful workstation computers on board. It also assumed fast, steady wired or close-range wireless networks, no strict real-time timing, and mostly academic research work.[1]

      In practice, people pushed ROS 1 far past those early limits. By the time ROS 2 was planned, ROS was running on wheeled robots of all sizes, legged humanoids, industrial arms, self-driving cars, and aerial and surface vehicles. ROS-based products were also reaching markets in manufacturing, agriculture, and commercial cleaning.[1]

      The ROS team listed five new demands it had never planned for: teams of multiple robots, small embedded computers, real-time systems, poor or lossy networks, and production environments.[1]

      Meeting those needs meant deep changes to how nodes find one another, move data, and expose their application programming interfaces (APIs). The team thought about building those changes into ROS 1. Changing a system that so many people already relied on proved too risky. Instead, they built ROS 2 as a new, parallel set of packages. It is not API-compatible with ROS 1, though bridges let the two systems work together.[1]

      02. How nodes find each other without a central master

      Before robot programs can share sensor data or motor commands, they first have to find each other on the network.[2] Picture two programs starting on your robot, such as a camera node and a navigation node. In ROS 1, both programs had to check in with one central coordinator first. In ROS 2, nodes discover one another directly with no central boss.

      ROS 1 relied on that central coordinator, called the ROS master, which you started with the roscore command.[2][3] Nodes found each other through the master. Their messages then traveled over ROS 1’s own TCPROS and UDPROS wire rules (built on Transmission Control Protocol, or TCP, and User Datagram Protocol, or UDP).[2] The same roscore process also held the global parameter store.[3]

      Relying on one central master brought two well-known problems:

      • Central point of failure: Discovery depended on one master process. In ROS 2, discovery is spread across the network by default, so “there is no central point of failure.”[2]
      • Multi-robot headaches: Running multiple robots was possible in ROS 1, but there was no standard approach. The ROS team described those setups as “all somewhat of a hack on top of the single-master structure of ROS.”[1]

      ROS 2 removes roscore and the central master altogether.[3][2] Instead, nodes find each other automatically through Data Distribution Service (DDS).[4][2] DDS is an industry standard for sharing real-time data across networked programs without a central coordinator.[2][5]

      Here is how automatic discovery works. When a ROS 2 node starts, it advertises its presence to other nodes in the same ROS domain. Nearby nodes reply with details about themselves so connections can form. Nodes keep advertising on a schedule, so late arrivals are still found, and they announce when they go offline.[4] There is no central server to crash and no master you have to start first.[2]

      For the curious: DDS standards, the RTPS wire protocol, and multicast discovery

      The Data Distribution Service (DDS) standard was created by the Object Management Group (OMG) and is implemented by several vendors.[2][5]

      Different DDS implementations talk to one another using the Real-Time Publish-Subscribe (RTPS) wire protocol. RTPS specifies the exact format of messages sent across the network.[5][6]

      Under the hood, node discovery traffic travels over network multicast.[7]

      03. Swapping communication engines with the RMW layer

      Different robots often need different networking tools. A single lab robot and a fleet running over rough wireless links have very different needs. Instead of locking you into one vendor’s communication engine, ROS 2 places a clean adapter layer in the middle. That design lets you swap the underlying messaging engine without rewriting your robot code.

      The ROS 2 core talks to the middleware through a C interface called ROS Middleware (RMW), written rmw in code. Each middleware has an adapter package that implements that interface, such as rmw_fastrtps_cpp for Fast DDS or rmw_zenoh_cpp for Zenoh.[8]

      The RMW layer sits right below the client libraries you program with (rclcpp for C++ and rclpy for Python).[8] Both C++ and Python nodes read an environment variable named RMW_IMPLEMENTATION. Setting that variable picks the middleware at run time, so the same application code runs on different engines.[9]

      You can choose from four main RMW options in ROS 2 Lyrical Luth:[5][10]

      • rmw_fastrtps_cpp: Built on eProsima Fast DDS. It is the default RMW in Lyrical Luth and comes packaged with the binary releases.[5][10]
      • rmw_cyclonedds_cpp: Built on Eclipse Cyclone DDS. It is also fully supported and packaged with the binary releases.[5][10]
      • rmw_connextdds: Built on RTI Connext DDS, which uses a commercial or research license. ROS binary releases include its adapter support, while you install Connext itself separately.[5][10]
      • rmw_zenoh_cpp: Built on Eclipse Zenoh, a non-DDS protocol that gained top-tier support in 2025.[11][10] It is a lighter alternative to DDS for tough network conditions and uses a Zenoh router to help nodes find one another.[5][12]

      To switch middleware on your machine, install the matching RMW package and set the environment variable in your terminal:[13] 

      sudo apt install ros-lyrical-rmw-cyclonedds-cpp
export RMW_IMPLEMENTATION=rmw_cyclonedds_cpp
      For the curious: RMW licenses, support tiers, and Zenoh routing

      In Lyrical Luth, rmw_fastrtps_cpp uses the Apache 2 license and has Tier 1 support, while rmw_cyclonedds_cpp uses the Eclipse Public License 2.0 and is also Tier 1.[5][10]

      rmw_connextdds holds Tier 1 support in Lyrical Luth on all architectures except arm64.[5][10]

      rmw_zenoh_cpp became Tier 1 in Kilted Kaiju (2025) and remains Tier 1 in Lyrical Luth.[11][10] The documentation describes Zenoh as a more lightweight alternative to DDS whose low wire overhead and flexible routing suit challenging network conditions.[5][12]

      04. Tuning how messages travel with Quality of Service (QoS)

      Not every message on a robot should follow the same delivery rules. Sometimes you want guaranteed delivery so no command or service call is lost. Other times, such as with live sensor readings over Wi-Fi, you want the newest data right away instead of waiting for old packets. ROS 2 gives you Quality of Service (QoS) policies so you can pick the right balance for each connection.

      ROS 1 mostly supported only TCP, and its default TCPROS transport enforced reliable delivery.[14] TCP guarantees that every byte arrives in order. To keep that promise, it holds back newer messages while lost packets are sent again.

      Picture an autonomous vehicle whose Wi-Fi link drops a few packets of laser scan data. A transport that insists on resending the missing pieces delays the newer scans behind them. By the time the old frames arrive, they may be too stale to help avoid an obstacle. For sensor data, the ROS documentation notes, getting readings quickly usually matters more than receiving every single one.[14]

      In everyday words, ROS 2 lets you choose anywhere from guaranteed delivery to fast, best-try delivery on each topic or service. As the documentation puts it, QoS policies let a connection be “as reliable as TCP or as best-effort as UDP.” You can set separate QoS profiles for every publisher, subscription, service server, and client.[14] The main policies are:[14]

      • History and Depth:
        • Keep Last: Stores only up to N recent samples, where N is the queue depth. The default profile uses a depth of 10.[14]
        • Keep All: Stores all samples, up to the resource limits configured in the underlying middleware.[14]
      • Reliability:
        • Best Effort: Tries to deliver samples, though some may be lost if the network is weak. The built-in sensor data profile uses Best Effort with a small queue, because getting the latest sample quickly matters more than getting every sample.[14]
        • Reliable: Guarantees that samples arrive, retrying several times if needed. This is the default for publishers, subscriptions, and services, and even reliable delivery in ROS 2 runs over UDP.[14]
      • Durability:
        • Volatile: Keeps no old samples, so a subscriber only gets messages sent after it connects. This is the default.[14]
        • Transient Local: The publisher saves samples for “late-joining” subscriptions, such as an RViz display window opened ten minutes after the robot started. Paired with a queue depth, it works much like “latched” topics in ROS 1 and fits data such as a static map.[14]
      • Lifespan and Deadline: Lifespan is the longest time a message can spend between being published and received before it is dropped as stale. Deadline is the expected maximum time between consecutive messages. If a publisher or subscription misses it, your application can be notified with a “deadline missed” QoS event callback.[14]

      05. Why mismatched QoS settings cause silent topics

      One common puzzle for newcomers to ROS 2 is the “silent topic.” Your publisher is running and your subscriber is listening, yet no messages ever arrive. A frequent cause is a QoS incompatibility. A connection forms only if the publisher and subscription use compatible QoS profiles.[14]

      Compatibility follows a “Request vs Offered” model.[14] Think of it as a minimum standard versus a promise: the subscriber sets the lowest quality it will accept, and the publisher states the highest quality it can deliver.

      The Rule of QoS Compatibility

      A subscription requests the minimum quality it will accept. A publisher offers the maximum quality it can provide. A connection is made only if no requested policy is stricter than the offered one: Offered QoS ≥ Requested QoS.[14]

      The documentation’s compatibility tables give these results for Reliability and Durability:[14]

      Publisher Offers Subscriber Requests Connection Status Technical Explanation
      Reliable Reliable Compatible Both endpoints agree on delivery acknowledgments.
      Reliable Best Effort Compatible Publisher offers higher quality than requested; subscriber receives data.
      Best Effort Best Effort Compatible Both endpoints accept that some samples may be lost.
      Best Effort Reliable INCOMPATIBLE Subscriber demands delivery guarantees that the publisher cannot provide. No connection is made.
      Volatile Transient Local INCOMPATIBLE Subscriber asks for old (latched) samples, but the publisher keeps none. No communication.

      The QoS compatibility calculator needs JavaScript. With it on, you can test publisher and subscriber QoS policy combinations to check compatibility.

      If your subscriber receives no data, check the topic from the command line: 

      ros2 topic info /my_topic --verbose

      With --verbose, this command lists every publisher and subscriber on the topic along with their QoS profiles. You can compare what each side offers and requests to spot a mismatch quickly.[15]

      06. Controlling startup order with lifecycle nodes

      An ordinary node starts working the moment you create it. In a complex robot cell, that rush can cause startup races. For example, a planner may start sending commands before a motor driver is ready. Drivers for cameras, lidars, and motors need to be started, configured, and shut down in a controlled order.[16]

      To solve this, ROS 2 introduced managed nodes, also called lifecycle nodes. In C++, they are implemented by the rclcpp_lifecycle package.[16] A managed node follows a state machine with four primary states:[17]

      • Unconfigured: The state right after the node is created, and the state it may return to after an error. It is expected to hold no stored state.[17]
      • Inactive: The node is paused and does no processing. Here you can configure its parameters, publishers, and subscriptions while it does not read topics, process data, or answer functional service requests.[17]
      • Active: The main state. Here the node processes data, answers service requests, and produces output.[17]
      • Finalized: The ending state just before the node is destroyed. It stays visible to inspection and debugging tools.[17]

      Moving out of a primary state requires a command from an external supervisor process, except after an error in Active. The supervisor uses transitions such as configure, activate, deactivate, cleanup, and shutdown.[17]

      For instance, a supervisor can bring every sensor driver to Inactive first. It then calls activate on the cell only after each driver reports its hardware ready. That ensures hardware is used only when ready and is released safely on shutdown.[16][17]

      07. Starting many nodes at once with launch files

      A ROS system usually runs many nodes, and starting each one by hand quickly becomes tedious. Instead of opening terminal after terminal, you can use the launch system. It starts many nodes with one command, passes them their settings, and monitors the processes it started.[18]

      In ROS 1, launch files were written in Extensible Markup Language (XML).[19] In ROS 2, you can write launch files in XML, YAML (a human-readable data format), or Python (*.launch.py).[18][19]

      For most applications, the format comes down to developer preference.[19] Python gives you the most flexibility because you can use the whole language and its libraries. Since the launch system itself is written in Python, a Python launch file can reach features that XML and YAML do not expose. The trade-off is that Python launch files can be more complex and verbose.[19]

      Here is a Python launch file that starts the Turtlesim simulator and a keyboard teleoperation node together: 

      from launch import LaunchDescription
from launch_ros.actions import Node
from launch.actions import DeclareLaunchArgument
from launch.substitutions import LaunchConfiguration

def generate_launch_description():
    use_sim_time = LaunchConfiguration('use_sim_time', default='false')

    return LaunchDescription([
        DeclareLaunchArgument('use_sim_time', default_value='false', description='Use simulation clock'),
        Node(
            package='turtlesim',
            executable='turtlesim_node',
            name='sim',
            parameters=[{'use_sim_time': use_sim_time}]
        ),
        Node(
            package='turtlesim',
            executable='turtle_teleop_key',
            name='teleop',
            output='screen'
        )
    ])

      In this script, generate_launch_description() returns the list of actions to start. It declares a use_sim_time argument and launches both turtlesim_node and turtle_teleop_key.

      Launch files also support event handlers that react while the system runs. For example, an event handler can shut everything down when one important node exits.[20] Launch files also support launch arguments, substitutions, and starting composable nodes inside a container.[18] Here, a container means a shared operating system (OS) process that hosts several composable nodes, not a Docker container.[18]

      08. Running ROS 2 on more devices and keeping it secure

      ROS 1 mostly assumed your robot ran on a Linux workstation on a trusted lab network. Today, teams write code on Windows laptops, deploy onto tiny microcontrollers, and connect robots over shared networks that need protection. Beyond messaging and discovery, ROS 2 expanded the core system on three fronts:

      • Cross-platform support: ROS 2 adds official support for Windows 11 and macOS alongside Linux. Windows users get official binary packages and can run ROS 2 directly on Windows workstations.[10][21]
      • Built-in security (SROS 2): ROS 2 can protect traffic between nodes. It can encrypt data in transit, verify who is sending data, check data integrity, and enforce domain-wide access rules.[22]
      • Microcontroller integration (micro-ROS): The ROS 2 designers wanted small computers, including bare-metal microcontrollers, to be first-class participants instead of sitting behind a device driver.[1] The micro-ROS project “puts ROS 2 onto microcontrollers” and supports several real-time operating systems.[23]
      For the curious: Lyrical Luth platform tiers and SROS 2 certificates

      For Lyrical Luth, Ubuntu 26.04 (amd64 and arm64) and Windows 11 (amd64) are Tier 1 platforms, RHEL 10 is Tier 2, and macOS is Tier 3.[10]

      SROS 2 security services come from the security plugins defined by the OMG DDS Security specification. Node identities are managed with certificates signed by an identity certificate authority.[22]

      09. Choosing a ROS version today

      If you are starting a robotics project today, you might wonder whether you still need to learn ROS 1. The short answer is no: ROS 1 has officially retired, and new robotics work targets ROS 2. Knowing how releases are scheduled helps you pick a version that stays supported for your whole project.

      For several years, ROS 1 and ROS 2 lived side by side. Developers connected older ROS 1 nodes to ROS 2 systems with the ros1_bridge package.[24]

      That transition period is now over. ROS Noetic Ninjemys, the last ROS 1 distribution, was marked End-of-Life (EOL) on May 31, 2025.[25][26] ROS 1 has reached the end of development with no new releases planned, while ROS 2 ships a new distribution every year.[26]

      Long Term Support (LTS) releases have appeared every two years in May, each supported for five years.[27] Recent LTS releases include ROS 2 Humble Hawksbill (supported until May 2027)[28] and ROS 2 Jazzy Jalisco (supported until May 2029).[29] Our target platform is ROS 2 Lyrical Luth, released on May 22, 2026 for Ubuntu 26.04 LTS and supported until May 2031.[30][10]

      For the curious: Noetic final sync and earlier LTS dates

      After ROS Noetic Ninjemys’s final sync on May 29, 2025, no new Noetic changes were accepted and regressions in binary packages could no longer be fixed.[25][26]

      Earlier five-year LTS releases pair with Ubuntu LTS releases: ROS 2 Humble Hawksbill targets Ubuntu 22.04 (May 2022 to May 2027), and ROS 2 Jazzy Jalisco targets Ubuntu 24.04 (May 2024 to May 2029).[27][28][29]

      Try it
      1. Why is Best Effort reliability preferred over Reliable for high-resolution video streams from a moving mobile robot? What happens on lossy Wi-Fi if you use Reliable?
      2. Suppose your laser scanner publishes with Best Effort reliability, and your autonomous navigation node subscribes using Reliable reliability. Will the navigation node receive laser scans? Explain why or why not using the QoS compatibility rule.
      3. What is the key difference between Transient Local durability and Volatile durability? Which one would you choose for a map topic that only publishes once every ten minutes?
