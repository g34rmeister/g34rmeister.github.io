4.2 Ethics in Robotics (Hardware, Software)

Gerald Lê

01. Physical safety as an ethical obligation

In ordinary software, an unhandled exception or a deadlock crashes a process or corrupts a record. In robotics, a software bug or a bypassed interlock can let a heavy steel arm move while a person is within reach, and OSHA's accident cases show workers struck, or crushed against posts and fixtures, in exactly this way.[1] Physical safety is therefore not just a compliance checkbox: it is the first ethical obligation an engineer owes to the people who share space with a machine.

The core standard for industrial robots is ISO 10218, revised in 2025 in two parts: ISO 10218-1:2025 covers the industrial robot itself, and ISO 10218-2:2025 covers industrial robot applications and robot cells.[2][3] In the United States, ANSI and A3 published ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025, adapted from both parts.[4] The 2025 revision greatly increased the number of safety functions it describes and made functional safety requirements more explicit.[4][5] A useful way to think about this is defense in depth: physical safeguards, dedicated safety controllers, and supervisory software layers, each able to stop the robot if the others fail.

02. Hardware safeguards and emergency stops

The foundation of robot cell safety is a set of physical safeguards and safety devices that do not depend on the robot's main application software. OSHA's technical manual lists the main types:[1]

  • Fixed barrier and perimeter guards: Fences and barriers that prevent access to, and contact with, moving parts during automatic operation.[1]
  • Interlocked barrier guards: Guard doors with interlock switches, so that opening the door stops the hazardous motion.[1]
  • Presence-sensing devices: Light curtains, safety mats, safety laser scanners and safety vision systems that detect a person entering the safeguarded space and trigger a stop.[1]

Machines also need an emergency stop (E-stop) function, whose design principles are set out in ISO 13850:2015.[6] A typical E-stop device is a large red mushroom-head push button on a yellow background. Electrical E-stop devices need direct-opening contacts with mechanical latching, so the button stays pressed, and the machine must not restart until it has been deliberately reset.[7]

IEC 60204-1 defines three stop categories, and ISO 13850 limits which of them an emergency stop may use:[8][9]

  • Stop Category 0: Stopping by immediate removal of power to the machine actuators (an uncontrolled stop).[9]
  • Stop Category 1: A controlled stop with power available to the actuators to achieve the stop, then removal of power when the stop is achieved. For a heavy arm, this lets the drives brake the motion under control before the brakes hold it.[9]
  • Stop Category 2: A controlled stop with power left available to the actuators. ISO 13850 limits emergency stops to Category 0 or 1, so Category 2 may be used only for normal stopping functions.[9]

03. Safeguards while teaching: the enabling device

During production, people stay outside the safeguarded space. During programming (teaching) and some maintenance, however, a technician may need to be close to the robot while holding a teach pendant, so extra safeguards apply.[1][2]

The main safeguard is the enabling device on the pendant (sometimes called a "deadman switch"). OSHA notes that it is typically a three-position device, and that the worker must hold it in the center-ON position, otherwise the robot's motion is inhibited:[1]

  • Position 1 (released): The operator is not gripping the switch, and the robot cannot move.[1]
  • Position 2 (center): The operator holds the switch in its middle position, which permits jogging the robot.[1]
  • Position 3 (fully squeezed): In a fright, people tend either to let go or to clench. The third position covers the clench: squeezing past the middle position also stops the motion. (This is the usual design rationale; check the manufacturer's manual for the exact behaviour of a given pendant.)

While teaching, OSHA recommends that robot speeds be reduced to 250 mm/s (10 in/s) or less on any part of the application.[1] The low speed gives the person time to see an unexpected movement and get out of the way, or release the enabling device.

04. Functional safety architectures and safety PLCs

Safety functions should not depend on ordinary microcontrollers or desktop operating systems. If a normal PC crashes, its outputs can freeze in whatever state they were in, leaving a gripper pressurized or a conveyor running. Safety functions are therefore built from safety-rated parts designed to a functional safety standard.

For machinery, the main functional safety standard is ISO 13849-1:2023, which gives a method for designing the safety-related parts of control systems, including their software.[10] It rates each safety function with a Performance Level from PL a (lowest) to PL e (highest), defined by its probability of a dangerous failure per hour; for PL e this is between 10−8 and 10−7 per hour.[11] The PL achieved depends on the architecture category (B, 1, 2, 3 or 4), the reliability of the parts, the diagnostic coverage and protection against common-cause failures.[12] Robot safety controllers are commonly certified to PL d, Category 3, as the ABB and Universal Robots examples below show.[13][14]

  • Dual-channel redundancy: Categories 3 and 4 use two redundant channels for each safety signal (such as an E-stop or a door interlock).[15] If one channel fails, for example through a broken wire or a welded relay contact, the other can still stop the machine. As Universal Robots puts it, two independent safety channels monitor each other, so a single fault cannot lead to loss of the safety function.[14]
  • Diagnostics: Safety controllers test their channels and compare them. Category 4 needs high diagnostic coverage, at least 99%.[15][12] If the channels disagree, the controller treats it as a fault and holds the machine in a safe state.
  • Mean time to dangerous failure: ISO 13849-1 grades the reliability of each channel by its mean time to dangerous failure (MTTFD). "High" means between 30 and 100 years; diagnostic coverage is "high" at 99% or more.[12]

05. Software safety layers: watchdogs and geofencing

Above the hardware safety circuits sits a software layer. Robot software stacks are large: middleware such as ROS 2, neural networks for pose estimation, inverse kinematics solvers. None of this application code is proven free of bugs, so a sound architecture keeps it separate from the safety-rated functions that can stop the robot.

One of the most important protections is the watchdog timer. What does a watchdog timer do? It triggers a safe stop if the main program stops responding.

A watchdog is a hardware counter that counts down from a starting value to zero. The software must restart it regularly ("kick the dog") before it reaches zero. If the software hangs, it stops kicking, the counter reaches zero and the watchdog acts: in a simple embedded system it resets the processor.[16] In a robot, the watchdog output is wired into the safety circuit, so that a hung control program also removes the robot's permission to move and brings it to a Category 0 or Category 1 stop.

Safety software also enforces geofencing: virtual safety zones that limit where the robot and its tool may go and how fast. Rather than trusting a path planner not to send the arm through a fence or into an operator's workstation, a safety-rated controller checks position and speed independently. Leading manufacturers offer this as safety-rated software:

  • FANUC Dual Check Safety (DCS): Monitors robot motion with redundant safety processors. It enforces safe zones and speed-limited areas, and removes motor power immediately if position limits or speed thresholds are exceeded.[17]
  • ABB SafeMove: Supervises tool position against defined zones, tool orientation, tool speed and standstill, using a two-channel safety controller in the robot controller that checks the main computer's motion data. ABB states that it complies with PL d under ISO 13849-1.[13]
  • Universal Robots safety system: Provides joint position and speed limits, a tool (TCP) speed limit, force and momentum limits, and up to eight safety planes, certified to PL d, Category 3 by TÜV Rheinland.[14]

06. Sensor privacy and data harvesting

Physical safety protects people's bodies. As robots move into homes, hospitals and shops, their sensors also raise questions of privacy and surveillance.[18]

To map a space and avoid obstacles, mobile robots often carry cameras, depth sensors, lidar and microphones. Unlike a fixed security camera, a robot moves through private rooms, and the images and maps it collects can travel far from where they were taken.[18]

In December 2022, an MIT Technology Review investigation showed how. Images taken in 2020 by development versions of iRobot's Roomba J7 series, including one of a woman on the toilet, had been sent to Scale AI, a company whose contract workers in Venezuela labeled them to train the robot's AI. Some of those workers posted the images in private groups on Facebook, Discord and other platforms. iRobot said the images came from special development robots given to paid data collectors and employees who had signed agreements about the data, not from consumer products.[18] The case still shows that people in the images are not always the people who agreed.

The IEEE Code of Ethics includes a duty to protect the privacy of others.[19] For robots, a practical way to honor it is data minimization: collect only what the task needs. Common design patterns include:

  • Edge computing: Process camera images on the robot's own computer, keep the obstacle positions or features needed for navigation, and discard the raw frames.
  • Physical privacy controls: Provide lens covers or hardware switches that disconnect cameras and microphones when they are not needed.
  • Anonymization: Blur faces, number plates and documents before any image is logged or uploaded for diagnostics.

07. Algorithmic bias in robotics perception

When robots rely on machine learning for perception and person detection, they can inherit biases from their training data.[20][21]

In a landmark 2018 study, Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial gender-classification systems and found that darker-skinned women were the most misclassified group, with error rates of up to 34.7%, while the maximum error rate for lighter-skinned men was 0.8%.[20] Similar problems matter for robots and vehicles that must detect people in order to avoid them:

  • Pedestrian detection disparities: A 2023 test of eight deep-learning pedestrian detectors found that the proportion of undetected children was 20.14% higher than for adults, and found significant gender bias at night.[21]
  • Interaction across diverse users: Service robots that read faces or gestures may work less well for groups that were under-represented in their training data, which is the pattern the Gender Shades study revealed for face analysis.[20]

Engineers therefore have a responsibility to audit training data, measure detection errors separately for different groups of people, and avoid relying on a single camera-based model for safety-critical person detection.

08. Autonomous weapons and meaningful human control

The deepest ethical boundary in robotics concerns the use of autonomy for lethal force. States have discussed lethal autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) within the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), although there is no single, universally accepted definition of LAWS.[22] In 2023 the UN Secretary-General recommended that states conclude, by 2026, a legally binding instrument to prohibit lethal autonomous weapon systems that function without human control or oversight.[23]

The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) describes autonomous weapon systems as systems that select and apply force to targets without human intervention: after activation, they strike in response to sensor data matching a general "target profile."[24] The main concerns raised against them are:

  • Human dignity: The ICRC argues that these systems raise fundamental ethical concerns for humanity, because life-and-death decisions would be handed from people to sensors, software and machine processes.[24]
  • Accountability gap: Human Rights Watch and Harvard Law School's International Human Rights Clinic argue that programmers, manufacturers and military personnel could all escape liability for unlawful deaths caused by a fully autonomous weapon, and that commanders often could not foresee or prevent such an attack.[25]
  • Unpredictability and escalation: The ICRC warns of dangers of conflict escalation, and calls for weapons whose effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained to be ruled out.[24]

Campaigners use the phrase meaningful human control for the level of human judgment they believe must be kept over the use of force.[25] The ICRC has recommended new legally binding rules: prohibiting unpredictable autonomous weapons and those designed or used to apply force against persons, and limiting all others, including requirements for human supervision with timely intervention and deactivation.[24] Existing law already requires states party to Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions to review any new weapon, means or method of warfare during its study, development, acquisition or adoption (Article 36).[26]

09. Governance frameworks: IEEE 7000 and the EU AI Act

To build ethical reflection into design work itself, standards bodies and legislators have created formal frameworks.[27][28]

On 15 September 2021 the IEEE published IEEE 7000-2021, IEEE Standard Model Process for Addressing Ethical Concerns during System Design.[27][29] It builds on Value-based Engineering and gives engineers a way to turn stakeholder values into system requirements:[29]

  • Concept and context exploration: Management, engineers and selected stakeholders communicate openly about the system and its intended use.[27]
  • Ethical values elicitation and prioritization: Identifying and ranking the values at stake, such as privacy, safety, transparency and fairness.[27][29]
  • Ethical requirements and risk-based design: Turning those values into requirements and tracing them through the concept of operations (a document describing how the system is intended to operate in its working environment) into the system design, with attention to harms to values as well as physical harm.[27][29]

In 2024 the European Union adopted the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), a horizontal legal framework for artificial intelligence.[28] The European Commission describes four levels of risk:[30]

Risk level What the AI Act requires Examples given by the European Commission
Unacceptable risk Prohibited in the EU. Harmful manipulation and deception, social scoring, untargeted scraping of images to build facial recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools, biometric categorization to deduce certain protected characteristics.[30]
High risk Allowed only with risk assessment and mitigation, high-quality data sets, activity logging, technical documentation, information for deployers, appropriate human oversight, and robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy.[30] AI-based safety components of products (such as AI in robot-assisted surgery), AI safety components in critical infrastructure such as transport, CV-sorting for recruitment, emotion recognition outside the banned settings.[30]
Transparency risk Allowed, with disclosure duties so that people know they are dealing with AI.[30] Chatbots, deepfakes, AI-generated text published to inform the public.[30]
Minimal or no risk No specific rules under the AI Act.[30] AI-enabled video games, spam filters.[30]

Under the EU AI Act, how are safety components of industrial machinery and collaborative robotics classified? An AI system that is a safety component of a product covered by EU product-safety law listed in Annex I, such as machinery, is classified as high risk, with requirements for risk management, data governance, logging and human oversight.[28][30] In 2026, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the "Digital Omnibus on AI") changed how this works for machinery: the Machinery Regulation moved to Section B of Annex I, so only a few AI Act articles (including the Article 6(1) classification) apply directly, and the detailed high-risk requirements will be added to the Machinery Regulation through delegated acts due by 2 August 2028. High-risk rules for AI in other regulated products apply from 2 August 2028.[31][30] By combining hardware safety with this kind of governance, engineers can make autonomous machines that serve people without sacrificing their safety or dignity.

Try it
  1. Inspect a commercial robotic device (such as a robotic lawn mower, an automated warehouse AMR, or a 3D printer) and identify its hardware safety controls, including physical stops, interlocks, and presence sensors.
  2. Diagram a dual-channel safety circuit containing an emergency stop button, an enabling switch, and a safety PLC, explaining how a single broken wire is detected without disabling the safety stop function.
  3. Review the sensor suite of a mobile robot and design an edge computing pipeline that fulfills GDPR data minimization principles by extracting navigation obstacles while discarding raw camera footage.
