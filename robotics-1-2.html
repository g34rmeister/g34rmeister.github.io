1.2 History and Evolution of Robots

Gerald Lê

01. Ancient and medieval automata

People were trying to build machines that move on their own long before electricity. An automaton (plural automata) is a mechanical object that is “relatively self-operating after” it has “been set in motion.”[2] None of these machines could sense the world and change its plan, so they are not robots in the modern sense (see 1.1 Definition of Robots), but they introduced ideas that robots still use.

In the first century AD, Hero of Alexandria described automata such as temple doors that opened by themselves. A fire lit on the altar heated the air inside, and the expanding air pushed water through a siphon into a vessel whose weight swung the doors open.[1]

Between 1204 and 1206 the engineer Ismail al-Jazari wrote his Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices for Nasir al-Din, the Artuqid sultan of Diyarbakr, in the upper Tigris region of what is now eastern Turkey.[3] The book, completed in 1206, describes dozens of mechanisms, including water clocks and musical automata. A historian quoted by Muslim Heritage calls al-Jazari’s automata “a significant milestone in the sequential programming of machines,” which is why some describe them as early programmable devices.[4] One thing to keep in mind: the illustrations you see in books and museums are later copies. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s page, for example, comes from a copy finished in 1315, more than a century after al-Jazari wrote, and shows a donkey-powered machine for raising water.[3]

At about the same time, the Song dynasty official Su Song built an astronomical clock tower in Kaifeng, China. Britannica describes it as a tower about 11 m (35 ft) high, “powered by a waterwheel and chain drive,” using techniques Europe would not use for centuries.[5] A wooden model was finished in 1088 and the tower was complete by 1094. An escapement, a device that lets a power source move forward in small, regular steps, drove an armillary sphere (a rotating model of the sky), and an endless iron chain carried power through the machine. Its 133 small figures appeared at doors or rang bells, struck gongs and beat drums to announce the time.[6] In today’s terms, it was a machine that performed a timed sequence of actions with no person in the loop.

02. Renaissance and clockwork automata

While working in Milan for Ludovico Sforza, Leonardo da Vinci designed a mechanical knight, moved by pulleys, cables and worm gears. His notes suggest a prototype around 1495, but nobody knows whether he ever built it. His notes survived only as fragments, which the scholar Carlo Pedretti identified in the 1950s. Mark Rosheim built a working reconstruction, which the BBC filmed in 2002, and he later drew on the design for a humanoid robot commissioned by NASA.[7]

In the 1700s, clockwork automata became popular in Europe. Between 1768 and 1774 Pierre Jaquet-Droz, his son Henri-Louis and Jean-Frédéric Leschot built three famous dolls: the Writer, the Draughtsman and the Musician. Britannica dates the Writer to about 1772.[2] All three are now at the Musée d’Art et d’Histoire in Neuchâtel, Switzerland.[8] The Writer has more than 6,000 parts, and the Draughtsman can draw four different pictures.[9] The Writer uses a goose-feather quill to write any custom text of up to 40 letters. The text is coded on a wheel where the characters are selected one by one, and cams guide the hand to form each letter, so the text can be changed by resetting the wheel.[8] That is the key step: the same machine could do different work without being rebuilt.

03. From the loom to numerical control

In 1804, Joseph-Marie Jacquard introduced a loom attachment that used chains of punched cards to control the pattern being woven. Swapping the cards changed the pattern without rebuilding the machine.[10] That idea, a machine whose behavior is set by a stored program, is the ancestor of every robot program.

Jacquard’s punched cards spread quickly: by 1812 about 11,000 Jacquard looms were in use in France, even though some silk weavers in Lyon resisted the machine and burned looms. Charles Babbage later adopted Jacquard’s cards for his proposed Analytical Engine, and punched cards remained a way to feed programs and data into computers until the mid-20th century.[10][11] The question of how automation affects workers is still part of the conversation about robots today (see Ethics in Robotics).

The next step came from machine tools. MIT founded its Servomechanisms Laboratory in 1940, with Gordon Brown as its first director. In the 1950s the lab developed automatic programming for continuous-path control of machine tools, and in the 1960s its APT language grew into a computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) system.[12] This kind of numerical control, where numbers stored on a medium tell a machine where to move, is the bridge between the loom and the robot. The first industrial robot stored its program on a magnetic drum.[13] Across all three technologies, “swapping the cards” became more and more literal.

04. The idea arrives before the machine

The word “robot” first appeared in Karel Čapek’s play R.U.R., published in November 1920 and first staged in January 1921; the name itself was suggested by his brother Josef.[14] The word “robotics” first appeared in Isaac Asimov’s story “Liar!” in 1941, according to the Oxford English Dictionary as cited by Wikipedia.[15] Both names came from fiction, decades before anyone built an industrial robot. Lesson 1.1 tells this story in full.

05. Unimate, the first industrial robot

The American inventor George Devol filed a patent for a “Programmed Article Transfer” on 10 December 1954. It was granted on 13 June 1961 as U.S. Patent 2,988,237.[16][17] The patent describes a fairly simple arm whose three links slide in and out (engineers call these prismatic joints). The robot that Devol and his business partner Joseph Engelberger actually built, the Unimate, was a hydraulic arm with five degrees of freedom that differed significantly from the patent drawing.[16][18] A degree of freedom is one independent way the arm can move, such as one joint turning.

The International Federation of Robotics (IFR) lists the Unimate under 1959 as the first industrial robot. It weighed two tons, was driven by hydraulics and stored its program on a magnetic drum. Each one cost $65,000 to make but sold for $18,000.[13]

In 1961 the first Unimate went to work at General Motors, at GM’s plant in Ewing Township near Trenton, New Jersey. The plant was then run by the Ternstedt division of GM’s Fisher Body unit (it was later called Inland Fisher Guide), which is why sources call it the Ternstedt plant.[13][19][20] The job was handling hot die-cast parts. The arm, about 4,000 lb (1,800 kg), lifted die-cast pieces of up to about 40 lb and dropped them into cooling pools, a hot and dangerous job that three shifts of workers had done before.[19] After roughly 100,000 hours of work the robot was donated to the Smithsonian in 1971, and the Unimate joined the Robot Hall of Fame in 2003.[19][16]

06. Robots spread to Europe and Japan

Other companies followed quickly. In 1962 American Machine and Foundry (AMF) installed six Versatran robots, the first cylindrical robots, at Ford’s factory in Canton.[13] In 1967 the first robot in Europe, a Unimate, started work at Svenska Metallverken in Upplands Väsby, Sweden, and the same year a Versatran became the first robot at a Japanese production site.[13][21]

The year 1969 was busy. Kawasaki built the first industrial robot made in Japan, the Kawasaki-Unimate 2000, under a licence from Unimation. The Norwegian company Trallfa made the first commercial painting robot. And at GM’s Lordstown plant, the first spot-welding robots helped automate more than 90% of body welding.[13] Note that this, not the 1961 Unimate, was the start of robot welding on car bodies.

Also in 1969, Victor Scheinman at Stanford University designed the Stanford Arm, with six degrees of freedom, six DC electric motors and a PDP-6 computer as its controller.[21][22] The Computer History Museum calls it “the first successful electrically-powered, computer-controlled robot arm.” By 1974 it could assemble a Ford Model T water pump, guiding itself with optical and contact sensors.[23] From 1973 Scheinman’s company Vicarm Inc. sold the design commercially, controlled by a minicomputer.[13][23]

In 1971 Japan set up the world’s first national robot association, JIRA, and KUKA built Europe’s first robot welding line, using hydraulic robots, for Daimler-Benz in Sindelfingen. In 1972 FIAT and Nissan installed spot-welding lines.[13] Then came a run of firsts in quick succession, many of them from Europe and Japan:

ABB’s own history of its robots adds that IRB 6 development began in 1970 and that the first one was delivered in 1974 to Magnusson i Genarp, an engineering workshop in southern Sweden. ABB calls it “the world’s first all electric industrial robot.”[25] That claim and KUKA’s claim for FAMULUS are each company’s own, and they describe different firsts: KUKA’s is about six electrically driven axes, ASEA’s about an all-electric robot with microprocessor control. The IRB 6 stayed in production for more than 20 years.[21] The T3 went to work in Volvo plants in Sweden.[21] The SCARA has three parallel rotating joints and one sliding joint.[21] Lesson 1.3 Types of Robots explains where it is used.

Looking at the table, you can see a thread running through it: the move from hydraulic power (Unimate in 1961, KUKA’s 1971 line) to electric motors (the Stanford Arm, FAMULUS, IRB 6 and Nachi’s welders).

07. From the 1980s to collaborative robots

In the 1980s Japan led the world in making robots, with more than 40 robot companies, and SCARA arms were widely used in its consumer-electronics factories. Robot installations had already been growing by more than 30% a year in the second half of the 1970s.[21] A few milestones from the next three decades:

  • 1981–84, new kinds of arm: Carnegie Mellon built the first direct-drive arm prototype (a design where the motors connect straight to the joints). PaR Systems built the first industrial gantry robot. IBM released AML, a robot programming language, in 1982, and in 1984 Adept introduced the AdeptOne, the first direct-drive SCARA.[21][13]
  • 1992–98, Delta robots: the Delta robot, developed by Reymond Clavel at EPFL in Switzerland, was first used at Demaurex, where six of them packed pretzels. ABB’s FlexPicker (1998), a Delta-type robot, made 120 picks per minute.[13][21]
  • 1996, PC control and the first cobots: KUKA built the first robot controller based on a PC.[13] J. Edward Colgate and Michael Peshkin at Northwestern University presented the first “cobots”, passive devices that guided a person’s motion.[26]
  • 1999, surgery: Intuitive Surgical brought the da Vinci surgical robot to market.[13]
  • 2006–08, light and heavy: KUKA and the German aerospace center DLR made a lightweight robot weighing 16 kg that could carry 7 kg. At the other extreme, the KUKA KR 1000 Titan (2007) carried 1,000 kg and the FANUC M-2000iA (2008) almost 1,200 kg.[13]
  • 2008, the UR5: in December 2008 Universal Robots, founded in 2005 in Odense, Denmark, sold its UR5 to Linatex, a Danish maker of technical plastics and rubber, to tend a CNC machine. It is generally described as the first commercially viable collaborative robot, and it needed no safety cage. By December 2018 the company had sold more than 27,000 cobots.[27][13]
  • 2012–16, cheaper and smaller: Rethink Robotics launched Baxter, a two-armed robot priced at $22,000, on 18 September 2012.[28] Precise Automation introduced the first collaborative SCARA, the PF400, in 2013, and in 2016 Mecademic’s Meca500 became the smallest six-axis industrial robot, at 4.6 kg.[13]

08. A shared software platform: ROS

Hardware was only half the story. For decades, every lab and company wrote its own robot software from scratch. In 2007 the Robot Operating System (ROS) began as a shared software platform for research robots, developed at the company Willow Garage.[29] Willow Garage released ROS for the first time in 2009, as an open-source collection of software meant to reduce programming effort.[13] So both dates are correct: 2007 is when development started and 2009 is the first public release. ROS has since become a standard across the field, and you will use it in the ROS chapter.

09. Robots that move around

While arms stayed bolted to factory floors, other researchers built robots that could move through the world.

  • 1948–49, Grey Walter’s tortoises: at the Burden Neurological Institute in Bristol, England, William Grey Walter built two small robots named Elmer and Elsie. They followed light and had bump sensors linked to the drive motor, so they steered around obstacles. Three new tortoises were shown at the 1951 Festival of Britain.[30]
  • 1966–72, Shakey: a project started by Charles Rosen at SRI and later led by Nils Nilsson, Bertram Raphael and Peter Hart. The IFR calls Shakey the first robot able to navigate its environment intelligently using robot vision. Its spin-offs include the A* search algorithm (1968), a practical version of the Hough transform (an image-processing method), and the STRIPS planner. Shakey is now on display at the Computer History Museum in Mountain View, California.[31][13]
  • 1988, HelpMate: Transitions Research Corp. put the first autonomous mobile robot into a hospital in Danbury, Connecticut, where it carried items as a courier. It was later used in more than 100 hospitals.[13]
  • 1997, Sojourner: NASA’s 23 lb rover rolled onto Mars on 6 July 1997. It drove about 330 ft in total, never more than 39 ft from its lander, and worked for 83 days instead of the planned 7.[32] The Mars Exploration Rovers, Spirit and Opportunity, followed in 2003.[13]
  • 2002, Roomba: iRobot released the first commercially available autonomous vacuum cleaner.[13]
  • 2004–07, DARPA challenges: in the first Grand Challenge (13 March 2004), no self-driving vehicle finished the roughly 150-mile desert course; the best, Carnegie Mellon’s Sandstorm, covered 7.32 miles. Just 19 months later, on 8 October 2005, Stanford’s Stanley won a 132-mile course in 6 hours 54 minutes. In the 2007 Urban Challenge, Carnegie Mellon’s Boss won a 60-mile course through city-like streets.[33]
  • 2005–12, Kiva Systems: Kiva’s robots carry whole shelves to the workers who pick orders, instead of workers walking the aisles.[13] On 19 March 2012 Amazon announced it would buy Kiva for about $775 million in cash.[34]
  • 2011–14, humanoids: NASA’s Robonaut 2 became the first humanoid robot in space, on the International Space Station, and SoftBank and Aldebaran released the Pepper robot in 2014.[13]
  • 2026, plans for factory humanoids: in January 2026 Hyundai Motor Group announced plans to make 30,000 Boston Dynamics Atlas humanoid robots a year by 2028 and to start using them in 2028 at its plant near Savannah, Georgia.[35] These are announced plans, not results yet.

Put together, these milestones tell a story you will see again in this course. Grey Walter’s tortoises reacted directly to what they sensed. Shakey sensed, planned and then acted. Sojourner and the DARPA vehicles took that kind of autonomy out into the field, Roomba and Kiva turned it into products sold by the thousands, and humanoids are the current frontier, mostly still at the pilot or planning stage.

10. Where robots stand today

Sixty-five years after one Unimate started work in New Jersey, the IFR’s World Robotics 2026 report, released on 24 September 2026, counts 603,307 industrial robots installed in 2025, up 11% on the year before. The number of industrial robots in operation worldwide grew 9% to 5,079,078, about 5.08 million.[36][37] Asia received 76% of the new robots, and China alone installed 354,226 units, up 20%.[36] Europe installed 73,054 robots and the Americas a record 57,044.[36]

There is a historical echo here. Japan went from importing a Versatran in 1967 and licensing the Unimate in 1969 to leading world robot production in the 1980s.[13][21] China’s share of new installations today plays a similar role in this decade.

Try it
  1. Pick two milestones from this lesson, one before 1970 and one after 2000, and write one sentence on what the later one could do that the earlier one could not.
  2. Jacquard’s loom changed its pattern by swapping cards, and the Unimate stored its program on a magnetic drum. What is the equivalent of “swapping the cards” on a modern industrial robot or cobot?
  3. Look back at the 1970s table. For each robot, decide whether its new feature was about power (hydraulic or electric), control (what computer runs it) or shape (how the arm is built).
