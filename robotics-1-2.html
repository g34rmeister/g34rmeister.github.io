1.2 History and Evolution of Robots

Gerald Lê

01. Prehistoric attempts at automation

People were trying to build machines that move on their own long before electricity. In the first century AD, Hero of Alexandria described automata such as temple doors that opened by themselves: a fire lit on the altar heated the air inside, and the expanding air pushed water through a siphon into a vessel whose weight swung the doors open. In 1206, the engineer Ismail al-Jazari described dozens of mechanisms in his Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices, including water clocks and musical automata; some historians describe his machines as early programmable devices.

In the 1700s, clockwork automata became popular in Europe. Pierre Jaquet-Droz’s Writer (c. 1772) is a doll that writes a short text with a quill, and the text can be changed by resetting a wheel of cams. In 1804, Joseph-Marie Jacquard introduced a loom attachment that used chains of punched cards to control the pattern being woven. Swapping the cards changed the pattern without rebuilding the machine. That idea, a machine whose behavior is set by a stored program, is the ancestor of every robot program.

02. From the loom to the computer

Jacquard’s punched cards spread quickly: by 1812 about 11,000 Jacquard looms were in use in France, even though some silk weavers in Lyon resisted the machine and burned looms. Charles Babbage later adopted Jacquard’s cards for his proposed Analytical Engine, and punched cards remained a way to feed programs and data into computers until the mid-20th century.

The question of how automation affects workers is still part of the conversation about robots today (see Ethics in Robotics).

03. Modern forms of automation

  • 1961, the first industrial robot: Unimate, developed by George Devol and Joseph Engelberger, went to work at General Motors’ Ternstedt plant in Ewing Township, New Jersey, handling hot die-cast parts.
  • 1969 and 1978, electric arms: Victor Scheinman’s Stanford Arm (around 1969) was an early all-electric, computer-controlled arm, and his later PUMA arm (1978) brought that approach to assembly work.
  • 1978, SCARA: Hiroshi Makino at Yamanashi University proposed the SCARA arm for fast assembly; commercial SCARA robots followed in 1981 (see Types of Robots).
  • 1996 and 2008, cobots: J. Edward Colgate and Michael Peshkin at Northwestern University presented the first “cobots” in 1996, passive devices that guided a person’s motion. Universal Robots sold its UR5 in December 2008, generally described as the first commercially successful collaborative robot.
  • 2007 onward, ROS: the Robot Operating System began as a shared software platform for research robots and became a standard across the field (see ROS).

Today automation ranges from fixed industrial arms behind safety fences to mobile robots in warehouses and cobots on the same bench as their operators.

04. From the first arm to the assembly line

Unimate did not appear overnight. George Devol filed his patent for a programmable arm in 1954, and he and Joseph Engelberger built a prototype in 1958 before the first production robot started work at General Motors in 1961.

The arms that followed got smarter. By 1974 the Stanford Arm could assemble a Ford Model T water pump using optical and contact sensors, and from 1973 Scheinman sold it commercially as the Vicarm. In 1984 Adept introduced the AdeptOne, the first direct-drive SCARA, and in 2013 Precise Automation introduced the first collaborative SCARA.

05. Robots that move around

  • 1972, Shakey: built at SRI, described by the International Federation of Robotics as the first robot able to navigate its environment intelligently using robot vision.
  • 1988, HelpMate: the first autonomous mobile robot in a hospital, able to plan and follow its own path.
  • 2005, Kiva Systems: warehouse robots that bring whole shelves to the workers who pick orders, instead of workers walking the aisles.
