7.5 Inspection and Measurement

Gerald Lê

01. Quality assurance and automated inspection

In industrial automation, manufacturing lines rely heavily on machine vision to verify product quality at production speed. Before an automated system is introduced, manual visual inspection by human operators is often the standard method. However, human visual fatigue and shifting attention create inconsistent quality checks over long shifts. Automated inspection reduces manufacturing visual inspection errors, which comprise missing existing defects and false alarms that trigger unnecessary scrap and production waste.[1]

Both types of inspection errors carry substantial industrial costs. A missed defect allows an incomplete, misaligned, or fractured component to move downstream into final assembly or ship to a customer. When that happens, the failure can cause costly recalls or halt an assembly line. Conversely, a false alarm incorrectly flags an acceptable part as defective. Rejecting good parts wastes raw materials, discards already completed machining labor, and lowers cell throughput.

Robotic cells frequently combine part handling with automated visual inspection. In Lesson 2.8 Vision-Guided Picking, we explored how cameras guide robot end-effectors by locating parts on tables or conveyors. Inspection builds upon those same physical setups. A camera mounted above a fixture (eye-to-hand) or on the robot wrist (eye-in-hand) captures an image of the part before or after manipulation. The robot program then checks dimensions or presence. Based on the vision verdict, the arm sorts the part into an accept bin, places it on an outbound conveyor, or routes it into a quarantine station for human review.

Industrial inspection tasks broadly fall into four categories: dimensional gauging, presence verification, surface flaw detection, and code reading. Classical machine vision solves these tasks with deterministic geometric algorithms, edge detectors, and thresholding. Where parts have complex, organic, or unpredictable cosmetic surface textures, learned vision algorithms may be applied instead, as discussed in Lesson 4.5 AI in Robotics: Uses and Limits.

02. Spatial resolution and the Nyquist limit

Every visual measurement begins with optical sampling. When an engineer selects a camera and lens for a gauging station, they must determine whether the system can physically resolve the smallest required tolerance. A simple measure of sensor resolution is the number of pixels per millimeter.[2] For example, if a camera sensor has 2,400 pixels along its horizontal axis and images an inspection field of view that is 120 mm wide, the optical system delivers exactly 20 pixels per millimeter in object space.

However, sensor pixel count alone does not guarantee that fine edges or small defects can be resolved clearly. Optical resolution is fundamentally defined as a spatial frequency measured in line pairs per millimeter, where one line pair represents alternating black and white bars in object space.[3]

As the alternating bars become narrower and closer together, the spatial frequency increases. No lens passes fine detail perfectly, and how well it keeps the bars apart is measured as contrast. Contrast is defined as how well black can be distinguished from white at a given resolution, quantified by the difference between maximum and minimum intensities divided by their sum.[4] When contrast drops to zero, adjacent bars blend into an indistinguishable solid gray field.

Even if a lens can transmit high spatial frequencies, the electronic image sensor must sample that pattern without aliasing. The Nyquist limit represents the highest spatial frequency a sensor can sample and equals two pixels per line pair.[3] Sampling requires at least one pixel on the bright bar and one pixel on the dark bar. If the physical detail in object space is smaller than two sensor pixels, the vision system cannot reliably detect it or establish its boundary location.

03. Dimensional gauging and telecentric optics

Dimensional gauging is the process of measuring physical geometric features of a part, such as hole diameters, shaft lengths, slot widths, or thread pitches. In an automated gauging station, software locates feature boundaries and converts pixel coordinates into calibrated real-world millimeters. For precise gauging, ordinary lenses have a problem: perspective error.

Conventional lenses possess an angular field of view that introduces parallax error where perceived magnification changes with object distance.[5] In a factory environment, parts moving along a conveyor or resting inside a fixture exhibit small height variations due to mechanical tolerances or vibrations. Under a conventional lens, a part positioned slightly closer to the camera appears larger, while a part farther away appears smaller.

To eliminate perspective error, precision gauging systems employ telecentric lenses. Telecentric lenses eliminate parallax error in precision dimensional gauging because their non-angular field of view keeps magnification constant across changes in object distance.[5] As a result, moving an object toward or away from the lens within its depth of field changes neither its apparent size nor its measured dimensions on the image sensor.

As 7.1 explained, they also help when a part drifts slightly out of focus: Telecentric lenses blur symmetrically across focus variations because they have no angular field of view component, allowing edges to retain their centroid location.[5] Sub-pixel edge detection therefore still finds the true edge.

Lighting matters as much as the lens (see 7.2). With a telecentric lens, in-line illumination is the only method to achieve reflected brightfield illumination across the entire field of view.[6] And shadowing caused by improper lighting can create false edge calculations that degrade measurement accuracy.[7]

04. Optical distortion and software calibration

Even when high-grade optics are selected, real-world lenses deviate from ideal mathematical pinhole models. These deviations are known as optical aberrations. One specific aberration that directly affects dimensional measurement is geometric distortion.

Distortion is an optical aberration that misplaces image information geometrically without blurring or discarding information, allowing it to be calibrated and corrected in software.[8] Every physical point in object space maps to a crisp point on the sensor matrix, but its spatial coordinate is displaced from its expected collinear projection.

Optical distortion is classified into negative barrel distortion where points appear displaced toward the optical center, and positive pincushion distortion where points appear displaced away from the center.[8] Barrel distortion makes the sides of a square bow outward; pincushion distortion pinches them inward.

To measure and correct this aberration, vision engineers use precision calibration targets. Geometric distortion percentage is calculated using a dot target by comparing the actual distance AD of an imaged point from center to its predicted distance PD.[8] As introduced in Lesson 2.8, intrinsic camera calibration models this distortion using calibration targets. Software maps each distorted image pixel back to its true rectilinear position. Once the distortion coefficients are computed, vision algorithms undistort the image stream before calculating distances, ensuring that straight mechanical edges remain straight in pixel coordinates.

05. Presence verification and flaw detection

Presence verification confirms that all required components are present before a sub-assembly leaves an automated cell. Common presence checks include verifying that an O-ring is seated in its groove, checking that a retaining clip is installed, or confirming that adhesive beads have been dispensed continuously. Flaw detection inspects surfaces for voids, burrs, scratches, or flash.

Both tasks rely on segmenting the acquired image into foreground features and background regions using thresholding. Once a binary image is formed, individual disconnected regions must be quantified. The cv::connectedComponentsWithStats function segments a binary image into labeled connected foreground components and computes bounding box geometry and pixel area for each label.[9]

The CC_STAT_AREA statistic output in OpenCV connected components represents the total area in pixels of the labeled component.[9] In a presence inspection application, software evaluates CC_STAT_AREA against upper and lower threshold limits established during process qualification. If a required gasket has an expected area of 4,500 pixels, an observed component with an area under 3,000 pixels indicates a torn or missing gasket, triggering an immediate robot reject routine. 

import cv2 as cv
import numpy as np

# Load preprocessed binary inspection image (foreground = 255, background = 0)
binary_mask = cv.imread('inspected_part_bin.png', cv.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)

# Segment connected components and extract statistics
num_labels, labels, stats, centroids = cv.connectedComponentsWithStats(binary_mask)

# Component statistics columns: [LEFT, TOP, WIDTH, HEIGHT, AREA]
min_expected_area = 4000
max_expected_area = 5000
inspection_passed = False

for label_idx in range(1, num_labels):  # Skip label 0 (background)
    area = stats[label_idx, cv.CC_STAT_AREA]
    cx, cy = centroids[label_idx]
    
    if min_expected_area <= area <= max_expected_area:
        inspection_passed = True
        print(f"Component found: Area={area} px at ({cx:.1f}, {cy:.1f})")
        break

if not inspection_passed:
    print("REJECT: Part missing, damaged, or out of tolerance.")

06. Barcode verification and 2D symbol reading

Many plants must trace each part. Robots must scan identification marks to verify part variants, confirm serial numbers, and log quality records into manufacturing execution systems. Marking methods range from printed paper labels to direct part marking (DPM) etched onto metal or plastic surfaces.

Reading a barcode and verifying it are different jobs. A standard barcode scanner simply attempts to decode the data string. If the code is degraded, a standard scanner may fail intermittently without indicating why. Barcode print quality is standardized by ISO/IEC 15416 for linear barcodes and ISO/IEC 15415 for two-dimensional symbols, which optical verifiers use to grade readability.[10] A verifier reports a grade, so a declining print process can be caught before codes become unreadable.

For compact industrial components, two-dimensional matrix symbols provide higher data density than linear barcodes. Data Matrix barcodes can be formatted in square form to encode maximum data capacity or in rectangular form with limited height for high-speed printing and narrow spaces.[11] Markings on parts that go through machining, heat or chemical baths can get scratched or partly covered. Data Matrix ECC 200 is the only Data Matrix version that utilizes Reed-Solomon error correction to detect damage locations and reconstruct corrupted codewords.[11] Its solid L-shaped finder pattern lets the reader locate the symbol.[11]

In OpenCV, vision applications decode barcodes and matrix symbols using built-in detection classes. In OpenCV, cv::barcode::BarcodeDetector provides detectAndDecodeWithType to simultaneously localize bounding quadrangles and extract decoded text and symbology type.[12] The BarcodeDetector detect method returns bounding vertices for each detected barcode ordered as bottomLeft, topLeft, topRight, and bottomRight.[12] By extracting these four bounding corners, a robot can calculate the planar orientation of the code and align its suction gripper or dispensing tool with the part.

For cylindrical parts, cans, or flexible packages, symbols are frequently printed on non-planar surfaces. Standard planar decoders fail when perspective curvature distorts grid spacing. OpenCV cv::QRCodeDetector includes detectAndDecodeCurved to detect and decode QR codes applied to curved packaging or cylindrical surfaces.[13]
