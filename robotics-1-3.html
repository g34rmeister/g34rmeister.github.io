1.3 Types of Robots

Gerald Lê Draft

01. Industrial

Industrial robots are the backbone of manufacturing. A typical example is a six-axis robotic arm on an automotive line performing spot welding: it follows a precisely programmed path and must be repeatable, safe and predictable. Industrial robots are usually programmed with a teach pendant or offline in simulation (see Types of Programming).

02. Cobot (collaborative robot)

Collaborative robots, or cobots, are designed to work alongside people in shared spaces. Their speed and force limits are configured as part of their setup, and they are usually programmed by lead-through: the operator physically guides the arm through the waypoints of a task. Universal Robots’ e-Series, programmed through PolyScope 5, is a common example.

03. Mobile robot

Mobile robots, such as warehouse robots and planetary rovers, move through their environment instead of staying bolted to one spot. Because those environments are less predictable than a factory cell, mobile robots rely on sensors to build a map, work out where they are on it, and plan a path around obstacles. This kind of software is commonly built with ROS.

04. SCARA

SCARA stands for Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm. It was proposed in 1978 by Hiroshi Makino at Yamanashi University, and commercial models appeared in 1981. A SCARA robot usually has four axes: two rotating joints that swing the arm in a horizontal plane, a vertical axis that moves the tool up and down, and a rotation at the tool. It is stiff vertically but slightly compliant sideways, which suits pushing parts into holes.

SCARA robots are fast and compact, so they are widely used for pick-and-place, assembly of small parts, screw driving and packaging, mostly on flat work surfaces.

05. Autonomous Guided Vehicles (AGVs)

Automated (or autonomous) guided vehicles move materials around factories and warehouses along predefined routes. The first, Barrett Electronics’ Guide-O-Matic of about 1953–54, was a towing tractor that followed a wire in the floor. AGVs still follow guidance built into the facility, such as buried wires, magnetic tape, floor markers or laser reflectors, and stop when an obstacle blocks their path.

Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) are their more flexible relatives: instead of following a fixed route, they map their surroundings and plan their own path around obstacles, as described under Mobile robot above.

06. AGV or AMR?

The choice between an AGV and an AMR depends on the job. AGVs are guided in one of several ways:

  • Magnetic tape: on the floor; extra pieces of tape can mark stopping points.
  • Inductive wire: buried in the floor, which the vehicle follows by its signal.
  • Visual markers: colored tape or printed markers such as AprilTags that a camera tracks.
  • Laser guidance: a 360-degree scanner on the vehicle locates itself from reflectors installed around the facility.

An AMR instead makes an initial mapping run with lidar or depth cameras, after which operators can mark keep-out and speed zones on the map. When something blocks its way, an AGV stops and waits, while an AMR plans a route around it. AGVs suit high-volume, repetitive routes and need their markers installed and maintained. AMRs need no changes to the building but usually cost more up front, and their travel times are less predictable.

Try it
  1. For each job, choose a robot type and say why: screwing small parts into a circuit board, welding a car frame, moving pallets around a warehouse that changes layout every week, packing boxes next to a human worker.
  2. Find a photo of a real robot online and identify which type it is from its shape.
