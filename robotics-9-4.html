      9.4 Electrical and I/O Faults

      Gerald Lê
      Chapter 9, lesson 4 of 6 ← Previous: 9.3 Mechanical Problems Next: 9.5 Calibration, Backups and Recovery →

      01. Power supplies and multimeter safety

      Many cell faults are electrical: a sensor that does not switch, a missing supply voltage, or a network link that drops. This lesson builds on the I/O in 2.5 End-of-Arm Tooling and I/O and the wiring in 8.1 From Wires to Networks.

      Before opening an enclosure, apply the lockout/tagout steps from 9.2; measurements on live circuits are only for people trained and authorized to make them.

      The main tool is a digital multimeter (DMM). Technicians should ohm out multimeter test leads before testing to verify the resistance measurement is close to zero.[1] To do this, set the meter to resistance and touch the probe tips together.

      Control panels use DC power supplies fed from the AC mains. If devices lose power, Fluke's procedure starts by checking the AC input. Measuring AC input voltage on a DC power supply requires placing the black probe on neutral and the red probe on line.[1] The meter should show the input AC voltage (120V or 240V), within the range the supply accepts.[1]

      Then check the DC output. Disconnecting or powering off devices attached to a DC power supply output prevents sudden voltage spikes during measurement.[1]

      A DMM has limits. Digital multimeters take snapshots in time and cannot detect transient events, electrical noise, or waveform variations without an oscilloscope.[1] A supply can read correctly on a DMM and still have short dips that upset the devices it feeds.

      02. Measuring DC voltages and testing continuity

      When setting up a multimeter for DC voltage measurements, the black probe connects to COM, the red probe connects to the VΩ jack, and probes are removed in reverse order.[2] "Reverse order" means the red probe comes out first, then the black.[2]

      Touching modern digital multimeter test probes to opposite polarity terminals displays a negative symbol without damaging the meter.[2]

      Using the wrong jacks is dangerous. Plugging test probes into current jacks rather than voltage jacks will create a short circuit when attempting to measure voltage.[2]

      A continuity test checks for an unbroken path, such as an intact wire. Continuity testing must be performed on de-energized circuits with the component isolated from other circuit components.[3]

      A functioning electrical fuse displays continuity when tested with a digital multimeter.[3] A blown fuse shows no continuity; fix whatever caused the overcurrent before replacing it.

      A digital multimeter triggers an audible continuity beep when component resistance drops below a threshold such as 40 ohms on a 400 ohm range setting.[3]

      03. Field sensor wiring and protection limitations

      Sensors are a common fault point. 8.1 From Wires to Networks explains sinking (NPN) and sourcing (PNP) sensors and why the input module has to match.

      Most industrial sensor failures come from incorrect wiring, in which the sensor output is connected directly to the power supply or AC line.[4]

      Fuses will not save a sensor from a wiring mistake. Fast-acting fuses and electronic current-limiting circuits operate too slowly to protect solid-state sensors from short circuits or reverse polarity wiring.[4]

      So check a replacement sensor's wiring against its data sheet before switching the power back on.

      04. Solid-state switching, leakage, and signal timing

      Some sensors switch AC with a solid-state device called a TRIAC.

      A switched-off TRIAC still lets a little current through. High off-state leakage current exceeding 1 mA makes TRIAC outputs unsuitable as inputs for programmable logic controllers.[4] The input may then never see the sensor turn off.

      Two-wire sensors take their power through the load circuit, which limits how they can be combined. Their higher voltage drops and operating power requirements typically limit a series connection to two two-wire sensors.[4]

      A very short sensor pulse can also be missed if it starts and ends between two PLC scans. High-speed sensors often include an off-delay pulse stretcher, which keeps a fast signal on long enough for the slower PLC scan cycle to detect it.[4]

      05. Controller I/O capacity, isolation, and noise mitigation

      The robot controller's own I/O has limits too.

      When adding devices, check the current budget. The controller's internal 24V supply supports up to 2A of total I/O current consumption, whereas an external power supply can deliver up to 6A.[5] If the internal supply is not enough, UR says an external supply can be connected; the user manual gives the details.[5]

      Noise from other equipment can also cause false signals.

      Electrical noise from external equipment can be reduced with galvanic isolation through an intermediate relay.[5] Galvanic isolation means there is no direct electrical connection between the two circuits. In UR's example, a robot digital output drives a relay, and the relay's contact switches a signal from an external power supply to a frequency converter.[5]

      06. Diagnosing network and fieldbus faults

      8.3 Lab: Connecting a Robot to a PLC showed Modbus TCP, and 8.6 Network Design, Security and Diagnostics showed how to watch traffic in Wireshark.

      A watchdog is a timer that raises an error when expected messages stop arriving. Modbus TCP communication watchdogs time out when program execution stops on signals configured with zero hertz update frequency.[6]

      For EtherCAT, the ETG's diagnosis guide starts with the status lights. An unlit Link/Activity LED indicates a permanent interruption of the physical network link to an industrial Ethernet device.[7] Check the cable, connector and the power to the neighboring device.

      Intermittent drops are harder to see. EtherCAT slave hardware has dedicated Lost Link Counter registers that increment whenever the physical link is interrupted.[7] They are registers 0x0310 to 0x0313, one per port, so reading them shows which port lost its link.[7]

      In EtherCAT networks, an unexpected Working Counter in a returned frame indicates a slave communication failure, and the master discards the input data.[7]

      07. Check your understanding

      • Why should technicians ohm out multimeter test leads before taking measurements, and what hazard is created if test probes are connected to current jacks during voltage testing?
        Show answer

        They should be ohmed out to verify that the resistance measurement is close to zero, by setting the meter to resistance and touching the probe tips together. If test probes are plugged into current jacks rather than voltage jacks, measuring voltage will create a short circuit. (Sections 01 and 02)

      • What causes most industrial sensor failures, and why do fuses not prevent them?
        Show answer

        Most industrial sensor failures come from incorrect wiring, in which the sensor output is connected directly to the power supply or AC line. Fuses do not prevent them because fast-acting fuses and electronic current-limiting circuits operate too slowly to protect solid-state sensors from short circuits or reverse polarity wiring. (Section 03)

      • What does an unexpected Working Counter in EtherCAT mean, and which registers record intermittent link drops?
        Show answer

        An unexpected Working Counter in a returned frame indicates a slave communication failure, and the master discards the input data. The Lost Link Counter registers, 0x0310 to 0x0313 (one per port), record intermittent link drops. (Section 06)
