6.1 Arduino for Robotics

Gerald Lê

01. Microcontrollers at the physical frontier

High-level robotic architectures such as ROS 2 often run on single-board computers or PCs with a general-purpose operating system like Linux. A standard Linux kernel is not set up as a real-time operating system: the mainline kernel offers several preemption models, and only the "Fully Preemptible Kernel" model turns Linux into a real-time OS. Without it, a time-critical thread can be held up by other work, so its timing is not guaranteed.[1]

At the physical hardware frontier, robots need tight, predictable timing. Motor switching, quadrature encoder counting and ultrasonic echo measurements cannot tolerate long scheduling delays. For this low-level control, roboticists use microcontrollers: single chips that combine a processor core, program memory, working memory and input/output pins. The ATmega328P, for example, packs 32 KB of flash, 2 KB of SRAM, 1 KB of EEPROM and 23 general-purpose I/O lines into one device.[2] The Arduino Uno, built around this chip, is a common starting point for prototyping robot control electronics.[3]

02. Hardware architecture: Arduino Uno R3 versus Uno R4

When designing an embedded robot controller, the board generation sets the electrical limits and the computing power. The classic benchmark is the Arduino Uno R3, powered by the Microchip ATmega328P microcontroller,[3][4] while newer designs often use the 32-bit Arduino Uno R4,[5] powered by the Renesas RA4M1.[6]

Hardware Specification Arduino Uno R3 Arduino Uno R4 (Minima / WiFi)
Microcontroller (MCU) Microchip ATmega328P (8-bit AVR)[4] Renesas RA4M1 (32-bit Arm Cortex-M4 with FPU)[5]
Clock Frequency 16 MHz[3] 48 MHz (3 times the R3 clock)[5]
Flash Memory / SRAM 32 KB Flash (0.5 KB used by the bootloader), 2 KB SRAM, 1 KB EEPROM[3] 256 KB Flash, 32 KB SRAM, 8 KB data memory (EEPROM)[5]
Operating Voltage 5 V[3] 5 V (keeps 5 V shield compatibility)[5]
Input Voltage (VIN) 7 V to 12 V recommended, 6 V to 20 V limit[3] 6 V to 24 V recommended, stepped down to 5 V by a buck converter[5]
DC Current per I/O Pin 20 mA listed by Arduino; 40 mA is the chip's absolute maximum rating[3][7] 8 mA maximum[5]
ADC Resolution 10-bit (1024 steps, about 4.9 mV per step at 5 V)[8] Up to 14-bit; analogRead() defaults to 10-bit for compatibility[8]
True Analog DAC Output None (PWM approximation only)[9] Up to 12-bit DAC on analog pin A0[5]
External Interrupt Pins (attachInterrupt()) Pins 2 and 3[10] Pins 2 and 3[10]
Built-in Peripherals USART, SPI, I2C[4] UART, SPI, I2C, CAN (external transceiver needed), op-amp, USB 2.0 full-speed (HID capable)[5]

The current limit per GPIO pin is a vital distinction for roboticists. On the Uno R3, Arduino lists 20 mA per I/O pin, and the ATmega328P's absolute maximum rating is 40 mA per pin; 20 mA is the datasheet's test condition at 5 V, not a safe target to run at continuously.[3][7] On the Uno R4, Arduino states that the RA4M1's GPIOs can handle up to 8 mA and warns never to connect devices that draw more current directly to a GPIO.[5]

03. GPIO pin functions and input modes

An Arduino Uno exposes 14 digital input/output pins (labeled 0 through 13) and 6 analog input pins (labeled A0 through A5).[3] Understanding their electrical behavior prevents confusing software bugs:

  • Digital Pins (0–13): Read or output binary voltages: logic HIGH (5 V) or logic LOW (0 V / Ground). On the Uno R3, pins 0 (RX) and 1 (TX) carry the serial link to the board's USB bridge chip (an ATmega16U2), which some of the processor's pins are reserved for; this USB link is also how sketches are uploaded and how the Serial Monitor talks to the board.[4] Connecting other hardware to pins 0 and 1 can therefore interfere with uploads and serial debugging.
  • PWM Output Pins (~): On both the Uno R3 and the Uno R4, pins 3, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 11 are the officially supported pins for Pulse Width Modulation with analogWrite().[9]
  • Analog Input Pins (A0–A5): Connect to the microcontroller's Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). On the Uno R3, analogRead(pin) maps voltages between 0 V and 5 V to an integer between 0 and 1023, so each step is about \(5\text{ V} / 1024 \approx 4.9\text{ mV}\).[8]

When a digital pin is configured with pinMode(pin, INPUT) and nothing is connected to it, the pin is a floating input. In this high-impedance state it picks up electrical noise from its surroundings and reports seemingly random changes between HIGH and LOW.[11]

To prevent floating inputs when connecting mechanical switches or bumpers, roboticists configure the pin as pinMode(pin, INPUT_PULLUP). This turns on a pull-up resistor inside the microcontroller (between 20 k\(\Omega\) and 50 k\(\Omega\) on most AVR-based boards), which holds the pin at HIGH when the switch is open.[11] When the robot's bumper switch closes, it connects the pin to Ground and the pin reads LOW, so the logic is inverted: LOW means pressed.[11]

04. Actuator power: why microcontrollers cannot drive motors

A beginner mistake in robotics is wiring a DC motor or large hobby servo directly to an Arduino GPIO pin. Arduino's own documentation notes that a pin's current is not enough to run most relays, solenoids or motors, and that short circuits or high-current loads on a pin can damage or destroy the pin or even the whole chip.[11]

Two physical effects explain why direct motor connection fails:

  • Current demands versus pin limits: An Uno R3 pin is listed for 20 mA (absolute maximum 40 mA) and an Uno R4 pin for 8 mA.[3][7][5] Even small DC gearmotors need far more than this when running, and much more again when starting or stalled, so they must be fed from a driver circuit, not from a pin.[11] Check the stall current on your motor's datasheet before choosing a driver.
  • Inductive kickback: A motor winding is a coil, which is an inductor. An inductor resists sudden changes in current, so when a switch abruptly cuts the current, the coil produces a voltage spike that can damage the switching electronics. This is why datasheets for motor drivers such as the L298 require diodes to carry this current when inductive loads are driven.[12]

05. H-bridge motor drivers: L298N versus TB6612FNG

To protect the microcontroller while supplying the high currents motors need, roboticists use an intermediate power stage known as an H-bridge motor driver: a power stage that takes low-current control signals from the microcontroller and switches the much larger motor current from a separate supply.[12] An H-bridge consists of four electronic switches arranged in an "H" shape around the motor. Closing diagonal pairs of switches sends current forward or backward through the motor, which gives two directions of rotation, and switching them rapidly with a PWM signal sets the speed.[12][13]

Two popular driver chips (often sold on breakout modules such as the "L298N" board) show how much driver technology has improved:

  • STMicroelectronics L298: An older dual full-bridge driver rated for supply voltages up to 46 V, 2 A DC per bridge (2.5 A repetitive peak) and 4 A total.[12] Its output transistors have a large saturation voltage drop: the datasheet gives a total drop (upper plus lower transistor) of 1.8 V typical and up to 3.2 V at 1 A, and up to 4.9 V at 2 A.[12] At 2 A, a 4.9 V drop means about \(4.9 \times 2 \approx 10\) W turned into heat in one bridge, which is why L298 modules carry a large finned heatsink. The L298 also needs an external set of fast-recovery diodes with a short reverse recovery time (trr of 200 ns or less, measuring how quickly a diode turns off when the voltage reverses) when it drives inductive loads such as motors.[12]
  • Toshiba TB6612FNG: A newer dual driver whose output transistors use an LD MOS structure (a laterally diffused MOSFET design that reduces internal resistance and heat generation) with low on-resistance: 0.5 \(\Omega\) typical for the upper and lower transistors combined (at a motor supply of 5 V or more).[13] At 1 A this is a drop of only about 0.5 V, so it runs much cooler than the L298 for the same current. It is rated for 1.2 A average and 4.2 A peak output current with a motor supply of up to 15 V, includes built-in regenerative diodes, and has a thermal shutdown circuit and a low-voltage detection circuit.[13]

06. Power architecture: the common ground rule

How should you power a DC motor in an Arduino robot? The foundational rule of robot electrical design is: from a separate battery through a motor driver, with grounds connected. Arduino's Servo library documentation gives the same advice for servos, which draw considerable power: use a separate supply and be sure to connect the grounds of the Arduino and the external supply together.[11][14]

Operating a robot therefore uses two power domains:

  1. The Logic Domain: The 5 V digital circuitry of the Arduino board, powered over USB or from a battery through the board's VIN input or a regulator.[3]
  2. The Motor Power Domain: A battery pack (for example a 2S or 3S lithium polymer pack) connected to the motor-supply terminal of the driver (VS on the L298, VM on the TB6612FNG).[12][13]

Crucially, the Ground (GND) wire from the motor battery and the Ground (GND) pin on the Arduino must be connected together.[14] What happens if you do not connect the Arduino ground to the motor battery ground? The logic signals float without a shared 0 V reference, causing erratic motor behavior or complete failure to switch.

The reason is that voltage is not an absolute quantity: it is a difference in electrical potential between two points. When the Arduino sets digital pin 9 HIGH (5 V), that 5 V is measured relative to the Arduino's own Ground pin. If the motor driver's logic does not share that same Ground, it has no reference for deciding whether pin 9 is at 5 V or 0 V. Its control inputs effectively float, pick up noise and can switch unpredictably, just like the floating inputs described in Section 03.[11]

07. Mobile robot power: Lithium Polymer (LiPo) safety

Autonomous mobile robots need light, energy-dense power sources, so many use rechargeable lithium-based batteries such as Lithium Polymer (LiPo) packs. Lithium-based cells can go into thermal runaway if mistreated (Battery University lists a thermal runaway temperature of about 150 °C for lithium cobalt oxide), so every robotics engineer should know the basic voltage limits.[15] The figures below are for cobalt-based lithium cells; always check your own pack's datasheet.

  • Nominal cell voltage: Battery University lists a nominal voltage of 3.60 V per cell for lithium cobalt oxide.[15] Cells are wired in series to raise the voltage: a 2S pack has two cells in series and a 3S pack has three, so their nominal voltages are about twice and three times the cell value. Hobby packs are often labelled with a slightly higher nominal value per cell, so read the label.
  • Maximum charged voltage: Cobalt-blend lithium-ion cells typically charge to 4.20 V per cell with a tolerance of ±50 mV (8.4 V for 2S, 12.6 V for 3S). Prolonged charging above 4.30 V on a cell designed for 4.20 V plates metallic lithium on the anode, and a fully charged cell has a lower thermal runaway temperature than a partly charged one.[16]
  • Discharge floor: Battery University lists a full-discharge cutoff of 3.00 V per cell for lithium cobalt oxide, with 2.50 V as the minimal voltage.[15] Treat 3.0 V per cell as the floor your robot should never go below; discharging further damages the cell.
  • Storage charge: If a robot will sit unused, do not store its battery empty or full. Battery University recommends putting some charge into an empty battery before storage, with 40 to 50 percent state of charge as ideal.[16]
  • Discharge C-Rating: The C-rate expresses current relative to a battery's rated capacity: a 1 Ah battery discharged at 1C supplies 1 A for one hour, and at 2C it supplies 2 A.[17] A pack's "C" rating is the maximum discharge rate its maker allows, so \(I_{max} = C \times \text{capacity in Ah}\). As a worked example, a 2200 mAh (2.2 Ah) pack rated 25C allows \(2.2 \times 25 = 55\text{ A}\).
LiPo safety protocols

Charge LiPo packs only with a charger made for lithium packs that respects the 4.20 V per cell limit.[16] Fit a low-voltage battery monitor or buzzer to your mobile robot so it warns you well before any cell approaches the 3.0 V floor.[15] Follow the battery and charger makers' instructions on charging and storage locations, and never leave a charging pack unattended.

08. Sketch structure and non-blocking sensor timing

An Arduino program (called a "sketch") is written in C++ and compiled into machine code for the board's microcontroller. Every sketch has two core functions: setup(), which runs once after each power-up or reset and is used to set pin modes and start libraries, and loop(), which then runs over and over so the program can respond to its inputs.[18] 

void setup() {
    // Runs exactly once at hardware power-on or microcontroller reset.
    // Configure pin directions, initialize serial communication, attach servos.
    Serial.begin(115200);
    pinMode(9, OUTPUT);
    pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP);
}

void loop() {
    // Executes repeatedly forever in an infinite cyclic loop.
    // Sample sensors, compute control laws, and update motor outputs.
}

A classic beginner pitfall is using delay(milliseconds) to sequence robot movements. While delay() runs, no other reading of sensors, calculations or pin manipulation can happen in your sketch; only interrupts, incoming serial data and running PWM outputs carry on.[19] If a robot is told to turn using delay(1000), it cannot read its bumper switches, measure distance sensors or check for an emergency stop command during that entire second.

The alternative, shown in Arduino's "Blink Without Delay" example, is non-blocking timing: the loop keeps running and checks millis() on every pass until enough time has elapsed.[19] millis() returns the number of milliseconds since the program started as an unsigned long, and it wraps back to zero after about 49 days; for short intervals micros() gives finer timing.[20] 

unsigned long previousMillis = 0;
const unsigned long interval = 50; // Execute control loop at 20 Hz

void loop() {
    unsigned long currentMillis = millis();
    if (currentMillis - previousMillis >= interval) {
        previousMillis = currentMillis;
        // Periodic control loop: read sensors and update motors
        updateRobotControl();
    }
    // High-frequency safety checks run on every single pass without delay
    checkEmergencyStop();
}

A common first distance sensor for mobile robots is the HC-SR04 ultrasonic ranging module, which measures from about 2 cm to 4 m on a 5 V supply.[21] To take a measurement, the microcontroller sends a 10-microsecond pulse on the Trigger pin; the module then sends eight cycles of 40 kHz ultrasound and holds its Echo pin HIGH for the time the sound takes to travel out and back.[21]

In dry air at 20 °C, sound travels at about 343 meters per second (0.0343 centimeters per microsecond).[22] Because the pulse travels to the obstacle and back, the distance is half of speed times echo time. The HC-SR04 datasheet expresses the same idea as "microseconds / 58 = centimeters".[21][22] 

distance_cm = (echo_time_microseconds * 0.0343) / 2.0;
Try it
  1. Wire an active-low bumper switch to digital pin 2 using pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP). Connect the other terminal of the switch to GND. Verify using the Serial Monitor that the pin reads 1 when open and 0 when pressed, with zero electrical floating.
  2. Connect an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor to an Arduino, trigger a measurement pulse, and print the resulting distance in centimeters to the Serial Monitor at 115,200 baud. Test against flat cardboard targets at 10, 30, and 100 cm.
  3. Wire a dual H-bridge driver (such as a TB6612FNG or L298N) to a separate battery and motor, ensuring the battery Ground and Arduino Ground pins are securely bridged. Test bidirectional motor rotation by toggling the direction pins.
