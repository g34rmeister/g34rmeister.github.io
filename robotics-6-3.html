6.3 Encoders and Shaft Encoders

Gerald Lê

01. Closing the loop: sensing motion and displacement

When a robotics engineer writes a program commanding a mobile robot to drive forward for two seconds, or tells a robotic arm joint to power on for 500 milliseconds, the system is running in an open-loop configuration. The microcontroller has sent power to the actuators, but it has no sensor confirmation of what movement actually happened. If the battery voltage drops, if the floor changes from smooth vinyl to thick carpet, or if a wheel hits an obstacle, the robot's real motion will drift away from what was commanded.

To control motion precisely, robots use closed-loop feedback control, and the most common motion sensor for this feedback is the rotary encoder (also called a shaft encoder). Mounted on a motor shaft or wheel axle, an encoder turns rotation into digital electrical signals that the controller can count.[1][2] Encoders give mobile robots the wheel-rotation data used to estimate distance travelled (odometry), let robot arms hold joint positions, and let speed controllers keep a steady velocity under changing loads.[1]

02. Incremental versus absolute encoders

Rotary encoders fall into two main categories: incremental and absolute.[1] The choice determines how a robot starts up and how it recovers from a power interruption:

  • Incremental Encoders: An incremental encoder produces a stream of pulses as the shaft turns, so it can only report a change in position; the controller counts pulses relative to wherever it started.[1][3] It keeps no record of its position: what happens to an incremental encoder's position when the power goes off? It is forgotten, so the robot has to home again. After power-up, the system must move to an index or home position to recover its location.[1] Some incremental encoders provide a third index channel (Channel Z) for this, which pulses once per revolution;[4] a counter can reload a known value when the index signal appears.[5]
  • Absolute Encoders: An absolute encoder reports a unique position value for each shaft angle. Once power is restored, it can report its exact position without any movement, so no homing routine is needed.[1] The controller usually reads this value over a digital serial interface such as SSI, BiSS or EnDat (see Section 08).[6] Some low-cost absolute encoders report their angle as a PWM duty cycle or an analog voltage instead of a serial word.[4]

Absolute encoders are further divided into single-turn and multi-turn devices. A single-turn encoder reports the angle within one 360-degree revolution, while a multi-turn encoder also keeps count of how many full rotations have happened.[1] As an example of typical resolutions, the SSI interface's standard word lengths are 13 bits for single-turn encoders (8,192 positions per turn) and 25 bits for multi-turn encoders.[6] Optical absolute encoders usually code each position in Gray code, in which only one bit changes between neighbouring positions, so a reading taken mid-transition is off by at most one step.[7]

03. Quadrature decoding: direction and resolution

If an incremental encoder had only one pulse channel (Channel A), the controller could work out speed from the pulse rate, but it could not tell the direction of rotation: clockwise and counter-clockwise rotation would produce identical pulse trains.

To solve this, quadrature encoders have two output channels, Channel A and Channel B. For most encoders these two square waves are 90 degrees out of phase (offset by a quarter of a cycle), and watching their state changes reveals the direction of rotation.[2][4]

The controller looks at which channel leads: when A leads B, the count increments, and when B leads A, it decrements.[5]

  • Forward Rotation: Channel A leads Channel B by 90 degrees, so when Channel A has a rising edge (LOW to HIGH), Channel B is still LOW.[5][2]
  • Reverse Rotation: Channel B leads Channel A by 90 degrees, so when Channel A has a rising edge, Channel B is already HIGH.[5][2]

The resolution you get from a quadrature encoder depends on the decoding mode used by the counter or software:[5][3][8]

  • X1 Decoding: The counter counts one edge of Channel A per cycle, so the counts per revolution (CPR) equal the encoder's pulses per revolution (PPR): \(\text{CPR} = \text{PPR} \times 1\).[5]
  • X2 Decoding: The counter counts both the rising and the falling edges of Channel A, giving two counts per cycle: \(\text{CPR} = \text{PPR} \times 2\).[5]
  • X4 Decoding (Full Quadrature): The counter counts every edge of both Channel A and Channel B. Each cycle contains four state changes (00 → 10 → 11 → 01 → 00), so X4 decoding gives four counts per pulse:[5][2][8] X4 decoding gives the finest position resolution, but because the four edges are not perfectly evenly spaced, speed computed from the count can be jumpier ("jitter").[3]

\[\text{CPR} = \text{PPR} \times 4\]

Worked example: a quadrature encoder is rated at 500 PPR. With 4x decoding, how many counts per revolution does the code see? Using the quadrature multiplier:

\[\text{CPR} = 500 \times 4 = 2000\text{ counts per revolution}\]

Read datasheet terminology carefully. Same Sky uses PPR (pulses per revolution) for the number of high pulses on output A or B in one turn, and CPR (counts per revolution) for the number of quadrature-decoded state changes, which is PPR multiplied by 4. However, some manufacturers use "CPR" to mean cycles per revolution, which equals PPR, and the terms PPR, CPR, LPR (lines per revolution on an optical disk) and "resolution" are not used consistently across the industry.[2][4] Because the two meanings of CPR differ by a factor of 4, a mix-up makes a robot's computed travel distance wrong by exactly four times. A good habit is to turn a newly mounted wheel exactly one full turn by hand while logging the raw count, to confirm the true multiplier. As a rule of thumb, choose an encoder whose resolution in edges per revolution is somewhat finer than the smallest positioning error you can accept; to know a joint to ±1°, that means more than 360 edges per revolution.[4]

05. Physical sensing technologies

How an encoder physically senses shaft movement decides how well it survives harsh conditions:[9]

Transducer Technology Physical Operating Principle Engineering Trade-Offs
Optical Encoders An LED (usually infrared) shines through a glass or plastic disk with transparent and opaque sections onto photodetectors, producing square-wave A and B pulses as the disk turns.[9] Higher resolution and accuracy than magnetic encoders. However, dust, dirt and oil easily block the light path, the disk can be damaged by vibration or temperature extremes, the LED limits lifetime, and current draw can exceed 100 mA.[9]
Magnetic Encoders A magnetized disk with alternating poles spins next to a Hall-effect or magnetoresistive sensor that detects the changing field.[9] Rugged, tolerant of shock and vibration, and unaffected by oil, dirt and moisture. However, they can suffer interference from nearby electric motors, have a limited temperature range, and usually offer lower resolution and accuracy.[9] A common hobby example is the AS5600 magnetic angle sensor, which reads a magnet on the shaft end and reports a full turn in 4,096 steps (12 bits, about 0.09°) over I2C.[10]
Capacitive Encoders A rotor with a sinusoidal pattern modulates a high-frequency signal from a stationary transmitter, and a stationary receiver detects the resulting changes in capacitance.[9] Tolerate dust, dirt and oil, cope well with vibration and temperature extremes, are immune to magnetic interference and electrical noise, and draw only about 6 to 18 mA. Same Sky (a capacitive encoder maker) also reports better accuracy and resolution than magnetic encoders.[9]

06. Kinematic calculations: from ticks to linear distance and velocity

In mobile robotics, encoder counts ("ticks") are converted into physical units of distance and speed. The formulas below follow directly from circle geometry: one wheel revolution moves the wheel's contact point by one circumference, \(\pi d\).

Given a wheel of outer diameter \(d\) (in meters) and an encoder with a verified resolution of \(\text{CPR}\) counts per revolution, the distance traveled by the wheel is:

\[\text{Distance } s = \left(\frac{\text{ticks}}{\text{CPR}}\right) \times (\pi \cdot d)\]

In a control loop that runs at a fixed interval of \(\Delta t\) seconds, the change in count \(\Delta\text{ticks} = \text{ticks}_{curr} - \text{ticks}_{prev}\) gives the wheel's angular velocity \(\omega\) in radians per second (one revolution is \(2\pi\) radians):

\[\omega = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}}{\text{CPR} \times \Delta t}\right) \times 2\pi\]

The linear velocity \(v\) of the wheel rim in meters per second is the angular velocity times the wheel radius \(r = d / 2\):

\[v = \omega \cdot r = \left(\frac{\Delta\text{ticks}}{\text{CPR} \times \Delta t}\right) \times (\pi \cdot d)\]

07. Microcontroller decoding: interrupts and state tables

In real robots, motor shafts spin at thousands of revolutions per minute. Worked example: a DC gearmotor runs at 3,000 RPM, and its motor shaft carries a 500 PPR encoder read with 4x decoding. The resulting rate of transitions is:

\[f_{interrupt} = \left(\frac{3000\text{ RPM}}{60\text{ s}}\right) \times 500\text{ PPR} \times 4 = 100,000\text{ transitions/sec (100 kHz)}\]

If a program tries to catch these transitions by polling the encoder pins with digitalRead() inside loop(), it must check the pins constantly to avoid missing pulses, which leaves almost no time for anything else; any slow step in the loop means missed counts and a position error that builds up.[11]

Arduino's documentation names reading a rotary encoder as a good use for hardware interrupts: an interrupt lets the board catch every pulse while the main program gets on with other work.[11] When the chosen edge occurs on an interrupt-capable pin, the processor pauses the main program, runs a short Interrupt Service Routine (ISR) that updates the count, and then resumes. Variables shared with the main program must be declared volatile, and the ISR should be as short and fast as possible.[11] Some libraries go as far as writing the ISR in assembly language for speed.[12] The example below counts every change on Channel A (X2 decoding) and uses Channel B to decide the direction: 

volatile long encoderCount = 0;
const byte pinA = 2;
const byte pinB = 3;

void encoderISR() {
    // Read current state of both channels
    byte a = digitalRead(pinA);
    byte b = digitalRead(pinB);
    if (a != b) {
        encoderCount++;   // A leads B: forward
    } else {
        encoderCount--;   // B leads A: reverse
    }
}

void setup() {
    pinMode(pinA, INPUT_PULLUP);
    pinMode(pinB, INPUT_PULLUP);
    attachInterrupt(digitalPinToInterrupt(pinA), encoderISR, CHANGE);
}

On both the Arduino Uno R3 and the Uno R4 (Minima and WiFi), attachInterrupt() works only on pins 2 and 3.[11] On a two-wheeled differential-drive robot, that leaves room for only one channel from each wheel's encoder on these interrupts, which limits an Uno to X1 or X2 decoding through attachInterrupt(). The Uno R3's ATmega328P also has a separate pin-change interrupt feature, which can be set up at register level to watch more encoder channels.[13] Other boards, such as the Nano 33 BLE or the Nano ESP32, allow interrupts on all pins.[11]

Widely used Arduino encoder libraries decode X4 with a 16-entry state table: the old and new states of A and B form a 4-bit index whose entry says whether the count changes by 0, +1, −1, or ±2 (when an edge was missed).[12][14] On larger processors the decoding is often done by a dedicated hardware quadrature counter rather than by software interrupts.[8] Some microcontrollers, such as the ESP32, include a hardware pulse-counter peripheral that can decode quadrature signals and filter glitches without running an ISR on every edge. Its counter is only 16 bits wide, so software must still extend the count and handle overflow.[15]

08. Absolute encoders in industrial robotics: multi-axis arms and serial protocols

For robot arms and similar automation, US Digital recommends absolute encoders, because rotating a joint to find an index position "could be dangerous to the equipment or people in its environment".[1]

Why do 6-axis industrial robot arms use absolute multi-turn encoders on their joints rather than incremental encoders? Because absolute encoders allow the arm to know its exact physical pose immediately on power-on without requiring dangerous homing movements through the cell.[1]

As an illustration, picture an industrial robot cell where an arm holds a heavy welding fixture inside a car body. With incremental encoders, an emergency stop or power outage would lose the joint positions, and to re-home the arm would have to move each joint until it found its home switch, possibly colliding with the car body and tooling on the way. With absolute encoders, the controller reads every joint angle as soon as power returns, with no movement, so production can resume safely.[1]

Industrial absolute encoders send their position to motion controllers over digital serial interfaces:[6]

  • SSI (Synchronous Serial Interface): The encoder sends its position bits in step with a clock supplied by the controller, over two twisted pairs to the RS-422 standard, at clock rates up to 1.5 MHz depending on cable length. Communication is one-way, from encoder to controller.[6]
  • BiSS (Bidirectional Synchronous Serial Interface): An open protocol that is bidirectional, so the controller can read and write encoder memory and request extra data such as temperature. It supports clock speeds up to 10 MHz and uses Cyclic Redundancy Checks (CRC) to detect errors.[6]
  • EnDat 2.2 (HEIDENHAIN): A digital, bidirectional interface that transmits absolute (and incremental) position values, lets stored parameters such as the encoder's electronic ID label be read and updated, and reports diagnostics, error messages and additional data such as temperature.[16] It is the only serial absolute-encoder interface on this list that remains proprietary.[6]

09. Engineering pitfalls: backlash, frequency limits, and electrical noise

Integrating encoders into real robot hardware means avoiding several classic failure modes:

  • Gearbox Backlash: Mounting an encoder on the fast motor shaft of a gearmotor gives excellent resolution, but the encoder cannot see the mechanical play ("backlash") between gear teeth. When the motor reverses, the motor shaft turns a little before the gear teeth re-engage and move the output, so the controller sees motion that the wheel or joint did not make. A controller with only a motor encoder cannot see what is happening on the load side.[17] When high precision is needed, add a second encoder on the output (load) shaft, as dual-loop servo systems do: the motor encoder helps keep the loop stable, and the load encoder gives the accuracy. Industry sources warn that using only the output encoder for feedback can make the loop harder to stabilise, because the backlash then sits inside the loop.[17]
  • Processor Interrupt Saturation: Only one ISR runs at a time, and other interrupts wait until it finishes.[11] An encoder with very high PPR on a fast shaft can request interrupts so often that a small microcontroller spends nearly all its time in the ISR, starving the main loop. Use the worked example in Section 07 to estimate your interrupt rate before choosing an encoder.
  • Electromagnetic Interference (EMI): Encoder signals are fast, low-voltage digital pulses. Running encoder wires alongside motor cables that carry PWM current can couple noise into them and cause false counts. Route encoder wiring away from motor power cables, and use twisted-pair or shielded cable where noise is a problem; industrial interfaces such as SSI use twisted pairs for this reason.[6] Industrial encoders reduce noise further with differential (RS-422 line-driver) outputs, which send each channel together with its inverse so that noise picked up equally on both wires cancels at the receiver.[18]
